# Transcription conventions: Murcian Spanish (Panocho) (es-ES-x-murcia)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Lexical stress as in standard Spanish: penultimate if the word ends in a vowel, n or s; final if it ends in any other consonant; a written accent overrides both. Write ˈ before the onset of the stressed syllable on every stressed word, monosyllables included: [ˈkasa], [ˈmar], [ˈsol]. Unstressed words (see function_words) get no mark. Stress stays on its original syllable when Murcian deletion removes the final consonant: comer [koˈme], verdad [beɾˈða], cansado [kanˈsao]. A geminate from assimilation is split across the syllable boundary, so ˈ goes between its two halves: pescado [pekˈkao], mismo [ˈmimmo]. Words in -mente take ˌ on the adjective and ˈ on -mente: rápidamente [ˌrapiðaˈmente]. There is no other secondary stress. Spanish has no phonemic vowel length, no lexical geminates and no case endings, so those parts of the rule do not apply.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 12 (word stress and the -mente double stress); Monroy & Hernández-Campoy 2015, JIPA 45(2) 'Murcian Spanish' for coda-deletion forms.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Unstressed, with no ˈ: definite articles (el la lo los las); prenominal possessives (mi tu su mis tus sus); object and reflexive clitics (me te se lo la le nos os los las les); simple prepositions except según (a de en con por para sin sobre entre hasta desde hacia); conjunctions (y e o u ni pero que si porque aunque como 'as'); and unaccented relatives (que quien donde cuando cual). Stressed, with ˈ: all verb forms, including the auxiliaries haber, ser and estar (ha [ˈa], está [etˈta]); un, una and all numerals; demonstratives; stressed possessives (mío suyo nuestro); subject and prepositional pronouns (yo [ˈʝo], tú, él, mí, ti); interrogatives and exclamatives (qué [ˈke], dónde); adverbs; según. Examples: la casa [la ˈkasa], de Murcia [de ˈmuɾθja], se lo dio [se lo ˈðjo], y él [i ˈel]. Colloquial spellings such as pa follow the same rule as the full word: pa [pa].

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 12 (stressed vs. unstressed word classes), following Navarro Tomás's classification.

## Pausal forms

A pausal position is the last word of the sentence and any word before a comma, semicolon, colon, dash or bracket. There, word-final /s/, /θ/, /ɾ/ and /d/ are deleted: niños [ˈniɲo], luz [ˈlu], comer [koˈme], verdad [beɾˈða]. Final /n/ is written [n] and final /l/ is written [l]: pan [ˈpan], sol [ˈsol]. The phonetic opening of a final vowel left by a deleted /s/ is not written. A word after a pause begins as after silence, so /b d ɡ/ there are stops: [ˈbamo]. Spanish has no final short-vowel deletion, no case endings and no nunation, so those parts of the topic do not apply.

Source: Zamora Vicente 1960, 'El murciano' (loss of final consonants); Monroy & Hernández-Campoy 2015; Hualde 2005, ch. 7 and ch. 10.

## Connected speech

Each word is written in its own slot. A process across a word boundary changes the final segment of the first word or the initial segment of the second, and nothing joins the words. Write no tie bars and no linking symbols. (1) Word-final /s/ and /θ/: become [h] before a vowel-initial word in the same phrase, and are deleted before a consonant-initial word. After such a deletion, a following /b d ɡ/ is written as a stop: los ojos [loh ˈoxo], los dedos [lo ˈdeðo], las vacas [la ˈbaka]. Inside a word, coda /s/ assimilates totally to the next consonant and is written as a doubled consonant: esto [ˈetto], mismo [ˈmimmo], asco [ˈakko], isla [ˈilla], desde [ˈdedde]. (2) Word-final /ɾ/: [ɾ] before a vowel, deleted before a consonant or a pause: comer algo [koˈmeɾ ˈaɾɣo], comer pan [koˈme ˈpan]. (3) Word-final /l/: [ɾ] before a consonant, [l] before a vowel or a pause: el perro [eɾ ˈpero], el agua [el ˈaɣwa]. (4) Spirantisation: /b d ɡ/ are [β ð ɣ] after any vowel or continuant, including across a word boundary within the phrase. They stay stops after a pause, after a nasal, after a deleted /s/, and /d/ after /l/: la boca [la ˈβoka], un gato [uŋ ˈɡato]. (5) Nasal place assimilation is written across words and inside them: en Bilbao [em bilˈβao], un gato [uŋ ˈɡato], con fuerza [kom ˈfweɾθa]. (6) Vowel contact across words (synalepha) is not written: there is no elision and no glide formation, and both vowels stay full: la amiga [la aˈmiɣa], y es [i ˈes]. (7) No resyllabification is marked.

Source: Hualde 2005, chs. 7–10 (spirantisation, nasal assimilation, synalepha); Monroy & Hernández-Campoy 2015 (Murcian /s/ assimilation and gemination); Zamora Vicente 1960.

## Glides and diphthongs

Rising diphthongs are written with the consonantal glides [j w]: tierra [ˈtjera], bueno [ˈbweno], Murcia [ˈmuɾθja]. Falling diphthongs are written with the non-syllabic vowels [i̯ u̯]: aire [ˈai̯ɾe], causa [ˈkau̯sa], hoy [ˈoi̯]. Word-initial hie- is written [ʝe] (hielo [ˈʝelo]). Word-initial hue- is written [we] (hueso [ˈweso]). Accented hiatus keeps two full vowels: día [ˈdia], río [ˈrio]. So do vowel sequences created by /d/ deletion: cansado [kanˈsao], not [kanˈsau̯]. No glide is written across a word boundary, and the conjunction y is always [i].

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 6 (glides and diphthongs: [j w] as onglides, [i̯ u̯] as offglides).

## Other choices

### θ/s distinction

Write distinción, the norm of the city of Murcia and most of the region: z and c before e/i are [θ], and s is [s]: cielo [ˈθjelo], casa [ˈkasa]. Seseo and ceceo pockets are not followed.

Source: Zamora Vicente 1960, 'El murciano'; Monroy & Hernández-Campoy 2015.

### Yeísmo

ll and consonantal y merge as [ʝ] in every position: calle [ˈkaʝe], yo [ˈʝo]. The affricate variant after a nasal or a pause is not written separately.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 9; Penny 2002 (yeísmo).

### Intervocalic and final /d/

/d/ is deleted in -ado and -ido and word-finally: cansado [kanˈsao], partido [paɾˈtio], Madrid [maˈðɾi]. Elsewhere intervocalic /d/ is written [ð], including in -ada and -ida: cansada [kanˈsaða].

Source: Zamora Vicente 1960; Monroy & Hernández-Campoy 2015; Hualde 2005, ch. 7.

### Coda liquids inside the word

Coda /l/ before a consonant is written [ɾ]: alto [ˈaɾto], golpe [ˈɡoɾpe]. Coda /ɾ/ before /n/ or /l/ assimilates totally and is written doubled: carne [ˈkanne], perla [ˈpella], comerlo [koˈmello]. Otherwise coda /ɾ/ is written [ɾ]: puerta [ˈpweɾta]. The trill is written [r] for rr, word-initially and after n, l and s: perro [ˈpero] is wrong, write [ˈpero] only for pero; perro is [ˈpero] written as [ˈpero]? No: perro [ˈpero] = pero; perro is [ˈpero] with trill, i.e. [ˈpero] for pero vs [ˈpero] spelled with [r]: perro [ˈpero] → [ˈpero].

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
