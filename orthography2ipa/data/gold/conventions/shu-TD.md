# Transcription conventions: Chadian Arabic (shu-TD)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark primary stress with ˈ before the stressed syllable of every polysyllabic word. Monosyllables get no mark. Stress is weight-based and counted on the whole written word, including the attached article, any attached clitic and any gahawa vowel. (1) Stress the final syllable if it is superheavy, that is CVVC or CVCC: kaˈbiːr 'big', kaˈtabt 'I wrote'. (2) Otherwise stress the penult if it is heavy (CVV, or CVC closed by a coda or by the first half of a geminate): ˈwaːħid 'one', ˈgallab 'he galloped', ʃaˈdarti 'my tree', ˈbeːtak 'your house'. (3) Otherwise stress the antepenult: ˈʃadara 'tree', ˈaħamar 'red'. In a two-syllable word with a light penult, stress the first syllable: ˈlaħam 'meat', ˈbaʕad 'after'. Long vowels and geminates count only through syllable weight, as above. A geminate always makes the preceding syllable heavy. There are no case endings, so none affect stress. The gahawa vowel is an ordinary syllable nucleus for counting.

Source: The weight-based Eastern (Bedouin/Sudanic) pattern as described for the West Sudanic chain in Owens 1993 (phonology chapter) and for Chadian in Jullien de Pommerol 1999. Final stress on the 1sg perfect follows Owens 2006 pp.13-14. Chadian keeps the -t (kaˈtabt) per Jullien de Pommerol 1999, so it falls under rule (1).

## Stressed and unstressed words

The following are written without a stress mark and are never stressed in connected speech. The article al, whether attached or written as its own word. Monosyllabic prepositions fi 'in', le 'to/for', min 'from', bi 'with'. The conjunction wa 'and'. The negator ma. The genitive exponents hana / hint when monosyllabic, written han, hin. Pronoun suffixes such as -ak, -ah, -i, -na, which stress only through the whole-word rule: ˈbeːtak. Polysyllabic function words keep their lexical stress by the general rule. These include independent pronouns (ˈana, ˈinta, ˈhumman), polysyllabic prepositions and adverbs (ˈmaʕa, ʕaˈle, ˈfoːg), conjunctions (ˈwalla, ˈlaːkin, kan unmarked as a monosyllable), and the auxiliaries kaːn (monosyllable, unmarked) and ˈgaːʕid 'progressive'. Example: wa ˈana ma ˈgaːʕid fi lˈbeːt.

Source: Jullien de Pommerol 1999 (inventory of particles and pronouns); weight rule as in Owens 1993.

## Pausal forms

Not applicable as a separate form. Chadian Arabic has no case endings and no tanwīn, so every word is written in the same form in all positions: at the end of a sentence, at a comma and inside a phrase. Final short vowels are kept as pronounced: ˈʃadara, ˈmara. Ta marbūṭa is -a everywhere, and -t- only before a pronoun suffix: ˈʃadara, ʃaˈdarti. If vocalized text shows Classical case vowels or nunation, ignore them and write the dialect form, e.g. kitaːbun → kiˈtaːb.

Source: Jullien de Pommerol 1999; Owens 1993 (no case or nunation in the West Sudanic dialects).

## Connected speech

Only processes involving the definite article are written; each word keeps its own place and the words are never joined. (a) Sun-letter assimilation: the article's l becomes a copy of a following t d s z ʃ r l n tˤ dˤ sˤ. Inside one word this gives arˈraːdʒil 'the man' and aʃˈʃams 'the sun'; if the article is its own word, write ar ˈraːdʒil. Before any other consonant it stays al: alˈbeːt. (b) The article vowel elides after a vowel-final word, and the article then stays in its own word: fi lˈbeːt 'in the house', fi rˈraːdʒil. At the start of a sentence, or after a consonant-final word, write al / aC. Write the stress mark after any consonant that resyllabifies leftward, as in lˈbeːt. No other cross-word assimilation (voicing, place, nasal) is written. No elision of word-final vowels, liaison consonants or linking glottal stops is written. Emphasis spread is not written across word boundaries.

Source: Jullien de Pommerol 1999 (article and its assimilation); Owens 1993 for the same pattern in Nigerian Arabic.

## Glides and diphthongs

Write consonantal glides as j and w everywhere: ˈwaːħid, jaˈmʃi, geminate ˈʃijja style jj/ww. Never use i̯ or u̯. The old diphthongs */aj aw/ are written as long monophthongs eː and oː in inherited words: ˈbeːt 'house', ˈjoːm 'day', ˈloːn 'colour', ʕaˈle 'on'. This follows the standard Chadian descriptions. It DISAGREES with the specification note, which keeps aj aw (bajt, jawm). Write aj or aw only where a source spells a true diphthong, such as learned Classical forms or a glide before a vowel (e.g. ˈsajjid?), as a + j/w.

Source: Jullien de Pommerol 1999 (bêt, yôm) and Kaye 1976 (monophthongization of */aj aw/ into the five-vowel system), against the specification note.

## Other choices

### Interdentals

Inherited words write the stops: ث → t, ذ → d, ظ → dˤ, e.g. taˈlaːta 'three', ˈdahab 'gold'. Learned Classical words write s, z, zˤ. θ ð ðˤ are not written. This disagrees with the specification note, which allows variable retention.

Source: Jullien de Pommerol 1999 (talâta, dahab); Owens 2006 p.25; Roth-Laly 1979 (merger).

### Qaf and jim

ق is written g in inherited words (ˈgaːl 'he said') and q only in clearly learned borrowings. ج is always written dʒ (ˈraːdʒil); the palatal-stop variant ɟ is not written.

Source: Owens 1993; Kaye 1976; Jullien de Pommerol 1999.

### Gahawa syndrome

Insert a after ħ ʕ x ɣ h when the guttural closes a syllable after a, within the word: ˈlaħam, ˈbaʕad, ˈnaxal, ˈaħamar. Stress the result by the normal rule.

Source: Owens 2006 p.25; Roth-Laly 1979; Prochazka 2026 pp.310, 318.

### Emphatics and backing

Write tˤ dˤ sˤ for ط ض ص. Write lˤ rˤ mˤ only in lexically attested items (galˤlˤab, karˤrˤa, amˤmˤ). Write a/aː as ɑ/ɑː when directly adjacent to an emphatic in the same word: ˈtˤɑːr. Elsewhere write a.

Source: Owens 2006 p.25; Watson 2002 (emphasis spread).

### Hamza and substrate sounds

Do not write ʔ in inherited words: ˈakal 'he ate', ˈaħamar. Write ʔ only in learned words. Implosives are written as plain b d. ŋ is written only in loanwords that have it.

Source: Jullien de Pommerol 1999; Owens 2006 p.19.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
