# Transcription conventions: Sudanese Arabic (apd-SD)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Write primary stress with ˈ before the onset of the stressed syllable. Mark it on every polysyllabic stressed word and never on monosyllables. Split geminates across the syllable boundary and write them as doubled letters: muˈdarris. Syllable weight: CV is light; CVV and CVC are heavy; CVVC and CVCC are superheavy. A geminate closes the syllable before it. Apply the steps in this order. (1) A final superheavy syllable takes the stress: kiˈtaːb, ʃaˈribt, maˈdaːris. (2) Otherwise a heavy penult takes the stress: ˈbeːti, muˈdarris, ˈkatbat, ɡaˈʕiːda. (3) Otherwise, when the penult is light, a heavy antepenult gives penult stress: madˈrasa, makˈtaba. (4) A word of exactly three light syllables (CV.CV.CV(C)) takes antepenult stress: ˈkatabu, ˈʃadara. (5) In a word of four or more syllables whose last three are light, the penult takes the stress: katabˈitu. Stress never falls further back than the antepenult. Long vowels count only as weight. Write them long where the dialect form has them, and do not shorten them because they are unstressed: kaˈtaːbi. The colloquial has no case endings, so none is ever written or counted. A suffix shifts the stress by the same rules: ˈbeːt → beːˈtak. Lexicalised adverbial -an counts as an ordinary syllable: ˈʃukran, ˈabadan.

Source: Follows the Cairene-type quantity-sensitive pattern set out for Egypto-Sudanic dialects in Watson (2002), ch. 5 (stress). Dickins (2007) and Hamid (1984) report it as largely shared by Khartoum Arabic, but neither was checked to a page. Where Levantine-type antepenult stress (ˈmadrasa) differs, the Cairene/Sudanese penult (madˈrasa) is followed. The specification notes give no stress rule.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Unstressed, never marked with ˈ, and kept in their short citation forms: the article al/l; proclitic b-, l-, w-, ħa- (future); the prepositions fi, min, ʕala, maʕa, ʕan, lee, ʕaʃaːn; the conjunctions wa/w, aw, laːkin, ɡabul ma, lamman, kan; the negator ma; the vocative ja; the relative/complementiser al and ʔinnu; and object/possessive suffixes such as -ak, -ik, -u, -ha, -na, -kum, -hum. Their own stress shifts to the host by the stress rule: beːˈtak. Because there is no copula, no auxiliary is written for 'be'. Stressed, and marked when polysyllabic: independent pronouns ˈana, ˈinta, ˈinti, hu, hi, ˈniħna, ˈintu, ˈhumman; demonstratives da, di, deːl, ˈdaːk, ˈdiːk; question words ˈʃinu, ˈmunu, weːn, keːf, ˈmata, ˈleːh; aspectual/auxiliary verbs such as ɡaˈʕid, ˈkaːn, ˈbiɡa. Examples: ˈana ma ˈmaʃeːt al ˈsuːɡ; hu ˈɡaːʕid fi lbeːt.

Source: Function-word inventory follows Dickins (2007) and the Sudanese forms in Holes (2004, ch. on dialect variation; not checked to a page). Negation with ma and no -ʃ suffix is the Sudanese norm, unlike Egyptian ma…-ʃ. Clitic stress-shift follows Watson (2002), ch. 5.

## Pausal forms

Nowhere. The colloquial has no case endings, no nunation and no final short-vowel deletion that is tied to pause. Each word has the same form at a sentence end, at a comma and medially. Do not insert MSA pausal or context forms. Tāʾ marbūṭa is -a in every position (madˈrasa). It becomes -at only in a construct phrase or before a suffix, which is a morphological rule, not a pausal one: madˈrasat al ˈbanaːt, madraˈsatna. Tanwīn appears only in lexicalised adverbs as -an, the same everywhere: ˈʃukran, taˈmaːman. Word-final long vowels of the spelling are written short unless a suffix follows: ˈʃafu 'they saw', ʃaˈfuːhu.

Source: Absence of case and nunation in the dialect follows Watson (2002), ch. 2 and the dialect overview, and Holes (2004). Tāʾ marbūṭa -a/-at follows Dickins (2007), not checked to a page.

## Connected speech

Write each word in its own slot. Only two cross-boundary processes are written. (1) Article vowel elision: after a vowel-final word, the article is written l (fi lbeːt, ʕala ssuːɡ). At utterance start or after a consonant it is al (al ˈbeːt kaˈbiːr, min albeːt). The article assimilates fully to a following coronal (t d s z ʃ sˤ dˤ tˤ zˤ r l n), written as gemination within the article's word: aʃʃams, fi ssuːɡ. Before ɟ and k it does not assimilate: alɟaˈbal, alkiˈtaːb. (2) The automatic onset glottal stop is never written at the start of a word, so the vowel links freely: ˈana ˈɡult. All other processes are left unwritten: epenthetic vowels between words, nasal place assimilation across words (min beːt, not mim beːt), voicing assimilation and emphasis spread across words. No resyllabification marks or linking ties are used.

Source: Article forms and sun-letter assimilation follow Dickins (2007) and Watson (2002), ch. 3 (phonological processes). The decision not to write cross-word epenthesis and assimilation is a broad-transcription choice consistent with the specification notes.

## Glides and diphthongs

Glides are always the consonants j and w, never i̯ or u̯. Geminated glides are doubled: ˈsajjid, ˈɡawwi. Old Arabic *ay and *aw are monophthongised and written eː and oː: beːt, joːm, ˈleːh, ˈfoːɡ. Where a diphthong survives, which is rare and found mainly in MSA loans and some lexemes, it is written vowel plus j/w: ˈʃaːj 'tea'. Intervocalic glides are written as consonants: ˈʃaːjif, ˈgawi.

Source: Monophthongisation *ay > eː, *aw > oː in Sudanese follows Dickins (2007) and Watson (2002), ch. 2. The j/w symbolisation follows standard Arabist IPA practice.

## Other choices

### jīm

ج is always ɟ: ɟaˈbal, raːɟil, ˈɟaːj. Do not write dʒ, even though dʒ occurs as a variant; choose ɟ in every row.

Source: Leddy-Cecere (2021), §3.1.1; specification notes; Dickins (2007).

### qāf

ق is ɡ in colloquial words: ɡaːl, ˈɡaːʕid, suːɡ. In learned, religious or MSA vocabulary that keeps q in Sudanese usage it is q: alqurˈʔaːn, alqaːˈnuːn. Never ʔ.

Source: Leddy-Cecere (2021), §3.1; Youssef (2021), §3.1; specification notes.

### interdentals

ث is t (taˈlaːta) and ذ is d (diːb). ظ and ض are dˤ (dˤahar, dˤarab). In MSA loans that use the sibilant reflexes they are s, z, zˤ: ˈsaːnawi, zˤaːhir. Never θ, ð or ðˤ.

Source: Leddy-Cecere (2021), §3.1; specification notes.

### emphatics and vowel allophony

Write the emphatics as tˤ, sˤ, dˤ, zˤ. Write short vowels as a, i, u and long vowels as aː, iː, uː, eː, oː. Emphatic or pharyngeal backing of a/aː is not written: tˤaːlib, not tˤɑːlib.

Source: Watson (2002), ch. 2; broad-transcription convention of the specification.

### hamza

Write ʔ only where the dialect keeps a phonemic glottal stop, medially or finally, mostly in MSA-derived words: saˈʔal, suˈʔaːl. Hamza that the dialect has lost is not written: biːr, raːs, ɟa. A word-initial glottal onset is never written.

Source: Dickins (2007); Watson (2002), ch. 2; not checked to a page.

### gemination

Write geminates as doubled symbols, not with ː: ˈkattab, ˈsajjid, aʃʃams.

Source: Common Arabist IPA practice; Watson (2002).

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
