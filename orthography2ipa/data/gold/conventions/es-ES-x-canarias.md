# Transcription conventions: Canarian Spanish (es-ES-x-canarias)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark primary stress with ˈ before the first segment of the stressed syllable, including any onset consonants and onglide: casa [ˈkasa], tierra [ˈtjera], bueno [ˈbweno], ciudad [sjuˈða]. Placement is lexical and follows the spelling. A written accent marks the stressed vowel: café [kaˈfe], árbol [ˈaɾβol]. Without an accent, a word ending in a vowel, n or s takes stress on the penult (joven [ˈhoβeŋ], lunes [ˈluneh]). Any other unaccented word takes stress on the final syllable (papel [paˈpel]). Mark ˈ on every stressed word, monosyllables included (sol [ˈsol], mar [ˈmaɾ], yo [ˈʝo]). Words listed as unstressed under function_words get no mark. Syllabify by maximal onset with Spanish onset clusters (pl, bl, tr, dr, kr, gr, fl, fr): otro [ˈotɾo]. Enclitics written attached to the verb belong to that one word and do not move stress: dámelo [ˈdamelo]. Adverbs in -mente get ˌ on the adjective's stressed syllable and ˈ on men: rápidamente [ˌrapiðaˈmente]. Spanish has no phonemic long vowels, no geminates and no case endings, so those parts of the topic do not apply. A sequence of identical vowels across a boundary is written as two vowels, never as a long vowel.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on word stress (the orthographic accent rules and the two stresses of -mente adverbs); Navarro Tomás, Manual de pronunciación española, is the classic source for the stress inventory.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Unstressed words, written with no ˈ: definite articles (el, la, los, las, lo); prenominal possessives (mi, tu, su, mis, tus, sus, nuestro as determiner); object and reflexive clitics (me, te, se, lo, la, le, nos, os, los, las, les); all simple prepositions except según (a, de, en, con, por, para, sin, sobre, hasta, desde); the conjunctions y, e, o, u, ni, pero, que, si, aunque and porque; relative que, quien, donde, cuando and como; and the titles don, doña, san. Examples: el niño [el ˈniɲo], de la casa [de la ˈkasa], me lo dijo [me lo ˈðixo] → see /x/ below, so [me lo ˈðiho]. Stressed words, marked with ˈ: indefinite articles (un [ˈuŋ], una [ˈuna]); demonstratives (este [ˈehte]); tonic pronouns (yo, tú, él, ella, usted [uhˈte], ustedes, nosotros); postnominal and pronominal possessives (mío, suyo); interrogatives and exclamatives with an accent (qué [ˈke], dónde [ˈdonde]); según; numerals; negative no [ˈno]; and every verb form, auxiliaries included (ha [ˈa], he [ˈe], es [ˈeh], está [ehˈta]): ha llegado [ˈa ʝeˈɣao].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on word stress (list of stressed and unstressed word classes), following Navarro Tomás's classification of palabras acentuadas and inacentuadas.

## Pausal forms

Canarian Spanish has no case endings, final short-vowel deletion or nunation, so those parts of the topic do not apply. A pause applies at the end of the sentence and at every comma or other punctuation mark inside it. The pausal rules are three. (1) A word-final /s/ before a pause is written [h], as in every other coda position. It is not deleted: los perros. [loh ˈperoh]. (2) A word-final /n/ before a pause is written velar [ŋ]: tren. [ˈtɾeŋ]. (3) The first word after a pause, or at the start of the sentence, counts as post-pausal. Its initial /b d g/ is a stop [b d g] and its initial ʝ is written [ʝ]: , dime [ˈdime]. Final vowels are never dropped at a pause.

Source: Zamora Vicente 1960, section on Canarian (canario): aspiration of final /s/ and velar /n/. Hualde 2005, chapters on /s/ weakening and on nasals and the approximant allophones (stops after pause).

## Connected speech

Each word keeps its own segments in its own place. No ‿ or other linking marks are written, and words are never merged. The processes below change only the segments at the edge of a word, conditioned by the neighbouring word in the sentence. (1) Spirantization: voiced /b d g/ are written [β ð ɣ] after a vowel or a consonant. They stay stops [b d g] after a pause, after a nasal, and for /d/ also after /l/: la boca [la ˈβoka], un beso [um ˈbeso], el dedo [el ˈdeðo]. The same holds inside words: hablar [aˈβlaɾ], hombre [ˈombɾe]. (2) Nasal place assimilation: a word-final /n/ before a consonant takes that consonant's place: un beso [um ˈbeso], con fuerza [koɱ ˈfweɾsa], en casa [eŋ ˈkasa], son chicos [soɲ ˈtʃikoh]. Before a vowel, or at a pause, it is velar [ŋ]: en el agua [eŋ el ˈaɣwa]. (3) Coda /s/ aspiration: a word-final /s/ is written [h] before a consonant, before a vowel and at a pause. It is not resyllabified as [s]: los otros [loh ˈotɾoh], es tarde [ˈeh ˈtaɾðe]. (4) Vowel contact across words (synalepha) is not written. Both vowels stay, with no glide symbols and no elision: la alumna [la aˈlumna], mi amigo [mi aˈmiɣo], de esa [de ˈesa]. The conjunction y is always [i]: y ella [i ˈeʝa]. (5) Resyllabification is not marked. A word-final consonant stays at the end of its own word, and stress marks stay inside their own word.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapters on the approximants /b d g/, on nasal assimilation, and on vowels in contact (synalepha), and the dialectal section on /s/ weakening. Zamora Vicente 1960, section on Canarian. Prevocalic aspiration of word-final /s/ follows the descriptions of Canarian and Caribbean speech rather than the resyllabified [s] of northern Peninsular norms.

## Glides and diphthongs

Write a prenuclear glide (rising diphthong) as [j] or [w]: tierra [ˈtjera], bueno [ˈbweno], cuidado [kwiˈðao], ciudad [sjuˈða]. Write a postnuclear glide (falling diphthong) as non-syllabic [i̯] or [u̯], including word-final y: aire [ˈai̯ɾe], causa [ˈkau̯sa], hoy [ˈoi̯], muy [ˈmui̯], reino [ˈrei̯no]. Triphthongs combine the two: buey [ˈbwei̯]. Written hiatus keeps two syllabic vowels: día [ˈdia], país [paˈih], río [ˈrio]. Word-initial hi + vowel and medial y or ll are written as the consonant [ʝ]: hielo [ˈʝelo], mayo [ˈmaʝo]. Word-initial hu + vowel is written [w]: hueso [ˈweso]. No glides are formed across word boundaries (see connected_speech).

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on glides and diphthongs (j w for onglides, i̯ u̯ for offglides; consonantal /ʝ/ against glide /j/).

## Other choices

### seseo and the letters c, z

There is no /θ/. Write c before e or i, and z, as [s], and treat them exactly like /s/, including coda aspiration: cielo [ˈsjelo], zapato [saˈpato], luz [ˈluh], hacer [aˈseɾ].

Source: Zamora Vicente 1960, section on Canarian; Penny 2002, section on seseo in Andalusian, Canarian and American Spanish.

### yeísmo

ll and consonantal y are one phoneme, written [ʝ] in every position, after a nasal or l included: calle [ˈkaʝe], yo [ˈʝo], llamar [ʝaˈmaɾ], cónyuge [ˈkoɲʝuhe].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on palatals (/ʝ/); Penny 2002, section on yeísmo; Zamora Vicente 1960, section on Canarian.

### /x/ (letters j, g before e or i)

The specification calls aspiration of /x/ 'partial', but the standard Canarian descriptions give a glottal realization. This set follows them: write /x/ as [h] everywhere: joven [ˈhoβeŋ], gente [ˈhente], ojo [ˈoho], reloj [reˈlo]. Word-final j is dropped, as in common speech. Word-initial h from Latin F (rural jumo for humo) is not written; write h as nothing: humo [ˈumo].

Source: Zamora Vicente 1960, section on Canarian (aspirated j); Hualde 2005, dialectal section on /x/.

### ch

Following the specification's note on affricate preservation, write ch as [tʃ] in every position: noche [ˈnotʃe], chico [ˈtʃiko]. Zamora Vicente describes a more palatal, weakly fricated Canarian realization, sometimes close to [c] or [ʝ]. That realization is not written.

Source: Zamora Vicente 1960, section on Canarian (the Canarian ch).

### /d/ loss

Intervocalic d in -ado, -ada, -ados and -adas: (1) In -ado and -ados, in participles and adjectives alike, delete it: cansado [kanˈsao], pescados [pehˈkaoh]. (2) In -ada and -adas, keep it as [ð]: cansada [kanˈsaða]. Word-final d is deleted: verdad [beɾˈða], usted [uhˈte], Madrid [maˈðɾi]. Every other intervocalic d is [ð]: todo [ˈtoðo], nada [ˈnaða].

Source: Specification note (-d deletion in -ado); Zamora Vicente 1960, section on Canarian; Hualde 2005, dialectal section on weakening of intervocalic /d/.

### coda /s/ before a consonant inside a word

Every syllable-final /s/, including the s-part of x before a consonant, is written [h]. The following consonant is not geminated or devoiced: mismo [ˈmihmo], este [ˈehte], resbalar [rehβaˈlaɾ], extraño [ehˈtɾaɲo]. Intervocalic x is [ks]: taxi [ˈtaksi]. The Eastern Andalusian gemination and vowel opening are not written, and vowels are always a e i o u.

Source: Zamora Vicente 1960, section on Canarian (aspiration less extreme than Andalusian); Hualde 2005, dialectal section on /s/ weakening.

### rhotics and liquids

Write the tap [ɾ] between vowels, after obstruents and in coda: pero [ˈpeɾo], tres [ˈtɾeh], mar [ˈmaɾ]. Write the trill [r] for rr, word-initial r, and r after n, l or s, also across words: perro [ˈpero], rosa [ˈrosa], honra [ˈonra], los ríos [loh ˈrioh]. Coda /ɾ/ and /l/ are not neutralized: alma [ˈalma], carne [ˈkaɾne]. That neutralization is local rural Canarian and is not part of the standard.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on rhotics; Zamora Vicente 1960, section on Canarian.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
