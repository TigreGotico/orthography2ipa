# Transcription conventions: Chilean Spanish (es-CL)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark primary stress with ˈ before the stressed syllable of every stressed word, monosyllables included ([ˈsol], [ˈpan]). The stressed syllable is found from the spelling. A written accent marks it (café [kaˈfe], árbol [ˈaɾβol], música [ˈmusika]). Otherwise, words ending in a vowel, -n or -s stress the penultimate (casa [ˈkasa], comen [ˈkomen], libros [ˈliβɾos]). All other words stress the final syllable (hablar [aˈβlaɾ], papel [paˈpel]). Clitics written attached to a verb leave stress where the spelling puts it (dámelo [ˈdamelo], decirlo [deˈsiɾlo]). Adverbs in -mente keep the stress of the adjective and add one on -men-; mark both with ˈ (rápidamente [ˈrapiðaˈmente]). Secondary stress is not marked in any other case. Intonation is not transcribed. Spanish has no phonemic long vowels and no geminates (rr is a single trill [r], cc is [ks]), so length is never marked. Spanish has no case endings. Words listed as unstressed under function_words get no ˈ.

Source: Hualde, J.I. (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chapter on word stress (orthographic-accent rule, two stresses in -mente adverbs).

## Stressed and unstressed words

These words are unstressed and written without ˈ: definite articles el la los las lo ([el], [la], [los]); prenominal possessives mi tu su mis tus sus nuestro/a(s) vuestro/a(s) ([mi], [su]); object and reflexive clitics me te se lo la le nos os los las les ([me], [se]); all simple prepositions except según (a de en con por para sin sobre entre hasta desde hacia contra ante bajo tras: [a], [ðe], [en], [kon], [poɾ], [paɾa]); conjunctions y e o u ni que pero sino si porque ([i], [o], [ke], [peɾo], [poɾke]); relatives without written accent que quien donde cuando como cual ([ke], [donde]); and tan, the reduced tan. Everything else is stressed and gets ˈ: subject and prepositional pronouns (yo [ˈʝo], él [ˈel], mí [ˈmi], usted [uhˈte]); indefinite articles un una unos unas ([ˈun], [ˈuna]); demonstratives; numerals; postnominal possessives (mío [ˈmio]); interrogatives and exclamatives with written accent (qué [ˈke], cómo [ˈkomo]); según [seˈɣun]; and all verb forms, auxiliaries included (ha [ˈa], he [ˈe], es [ˈes], está [ehˈta]).

Source: Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chapter on stress (list of stressed vs. unstressed word classes), following the Navarro Tomás tradition.

## Pausal forms

Spanish has no pausal forms, case endings or nunation (tanwīn). A word is written the same at a comma, at the end of a sentence and inside a phrase, with one exception. Word-final /s/ is written [s] before a pause or a vowel and [h] before a consonant (see connected_speech). So: las casas. [lah ˈkasas], los amigos [los aˈmiɣos]. Word-final /d/ is deleted in every position, not only before a pause (verdad [beɾˈða], Madrid [maˈðɾi]). Final vowels never change.

Source: Lipski, J.M. (1994), Latin American Spanish, chapter on Chile (aspiration of /s/, loss of final /d/); Hualde (2005) for the absence of pausal morphology.

## Connected speech

Write three processes across word boundaries, each inside its own word, and never join words. (1) Spirantization: /b d g/ are [β ð ɣ] everywhere except after a pause, after a nasal, and, for /d/ only, after /l/. This holds inside words (lado [ˈlaðo], abogado [aβoˈɣaðo]) and at the start of a word after a vowel or a non-nasal consonant in the previous word (la vaca [la ˈβaka], las bocas [lah ˈβokas], de Chile [de ˈtʃile] but casa de [ˈkasa ðe]). Sentence-initially and after a nasal they are stops (un gato [ˈuŋ ˈgato]), and /d/ is a stop after /l/ (el dedo [el ˈdeðo]). (2) Nasal place assimilation: a word-final /n/ takes the place of a following consonant: [m] before p b m (un beso [ˈum ˈbeso]), [ɱ] before f (en Francia [eɱ ˈfɾansja]), [ŋ] before k g x (en casa [eŋ ˈkasa]), [n] elsewhere. The same applies inside words (tengo [ˈteŋgo], cambio [ˈkambjo]). (3) Coda /s/ aspiration (moderate): /s/ before a consonant is [h], inside a word or across a boundary (este [ˈehte], mismo [ˈmihmo], los perros [loh ˈperos]). Word-final /s/ before a vowel-initial word or a pause stays [s]. No vowel elision, synalepha or cross-word glide formation is written; each word keeps its own vowels (casa amarilla [ˈkasa amaˈɾiʝa], mi hijo [mi ˈixo]). Resyllabification is not written (no syllable marks). Final -d is deleted (usted [uhˈte]). Word-medial participial -ado keeps [ð] ([kanˈsaðo]).

Source: Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chapters on obstruent spirantization and nasal assimilation; Lipski (1994), Latin American Spanish, chapter on Chile, for moderate /s/ aspiration and loss of -d.

## Glides and diphthongs

Prenuclear (rising) glides are written [j w]: tierra [ˈtjera], bueno [ˈbweno], ciudad [sjuˈða], cuidar [kwiˈðaɾ]. Postnuclear (falling) glides are written [i̯ u̯]: aire [ˈai̯ɾe], reina [ˈrei̯na], causa [ˈkau̯sa], hoy [ˈoi̯], muy [ˈmui̯]. Triphthongs combine both (buey [ˈbwei̯]). A written accent on i or u marks hiatus, with two full vowels: día [ˈdia], país [paˈis], baúl [baˈul]. Sequences of non-high vowels are always written in hiatus (poeta [poˈeta], teatro [teˈatɾo]), never as the casual glide [ˈpweta]. The conjunction y is [i]. Consonantal y/ll is [ʝ], distinct from the glide (yo [ˈʝo], calle [ˈkaʝe], ayer [aˈʝeɾ]).

Source: Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chapter on diphthongs and glides (j w vs. i̯ u̯ notation, hiatus).

## Other choices

### velar fricative /x/

Write /x/ (j, g before e/i, x in México) as [x] before a o u and consonants, and as palatal [ç] before e i: gente [ˈçente], mujer [muˈçeɾ], jirafa [çiˈɾafa], México [ˈmeçiko], but jamás [xaˈmas], ajo [ˈaxo], reloj [reˈlox]. /k g/ are not fronted (queso [ˈkeso], guerra [ˈgera]).

Source: Lipski (1994), Latin American Spanish, chapter on Chile (palatalization of /x/ before front vowels); agrees with the specification note.

### ch

Always write [tʃ] (chico [ˈtʃiko], Chile [ˈtʃile]). The deaffricated [ʃ] that the specification notes as casual and stigmatised is not transcribed, since the standard pronunciation keeps the affricate.

Source: Lipski (1994), Latin American Spanish, chapter on Chile; Fontanella de Weinberg (1992), El español de América, on Chilean consonantism.

### seseo and yeísmo

c before e/i, z and s are all [s] (cielo [ˈsjelo], zapato [saˈpato]). ll and consonantal y are both [ʝ] (llamar [ʝaˈmaɾ]). h is silent (hacer [aˈseɾ]).

Source: Fontanella de Weinberg (1992), El español de América; Penny (2002), A History of the Spanish Language, on American seseo and yeísmo.

### rhotics

Trill [r] word-initially, after n l s, and for rr (rojo [ˈroxo], honra [ˈonra], perro [ˈpero]). Tap [ɾ] elsewhere, coda included (pero [ˈpeɾo], comer [koˈmeɾ], verde [ˈbeɾðe]). Assibilated variants are not written.

Source: Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chapter on rhotics.

### vowels

Five vowels [a e i o u], with no reduction in unstressed syllables and no length or tenseness marks: estudiante [ehtuˈðjante].

Source: Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chapter on vowels.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
