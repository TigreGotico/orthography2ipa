# Transcription conventions: Costa Rican Spanish (es-CR)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark primary stress with ˈ before the stressed syllable of every stressed word, including stressed monosyllables. Place it by the lexical and orthographic rule. A written accent marks the stressed syllable. With no accent, a word ending in a vowel, n or s stresses the penult, and a word ending in any other consonant stresses the final syllable. Examples: casa [ˈkasa], comen [ˈkomen], ciudad [sjuˈdad], café [kaˈfe], árbol [ˈaɹbol], sol [ˈsol]. Voseo verb forms are final-stressed: vos tenés [ˈbos teˈnes], hablás [aˈblas]. Adverbs in -mente carry one mark only, on the stem: rápidamente [ˈrapidamente] becomes [ˈɹ̝apidamente]. No secondary stress is written. Spanish has no phonemic long vowels, no geminates and no case endings, so none are marked. Doubled letters across morphemes are written as written: innato [inˈnato].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on stress (lexical stress and orthographic accent rules); standard Latin American usage as in Fontanella de Weinberg 1992 for voseo forms.

## Stressed and unstressed words

These words are unstressed and get no ˈ: definite articles (el, la, los, las, lo); all simple prepositions except según (a, de, en, con, por, para, sin, sobre, entre, hasta, desde); conjunctions and complementizers (y, e, o, u, ni, pero, que, si, porque, aunque, como and cuando when not interrogative); object and reflexive clitics (me, te, se, lo, la, le, nos, los, las, les); prenominal possessives (mi, tu, su, nuestro); and relative pronouns (que, quien, donde). These words are stressed and get ˈ: indefinite articles (un, una); subject and prepositional pronouns (yo, vos, usted, él, ella, nosotros, ustedes, ellos, mí, ti); demonstratives; interrogatives and exclamatives (qué, cómo, dónde, cuándo); numerals; negative no; según; postnominal possessives (mío); and all verb forms, including the auxiliaries haber, ser and estar. Examples: la casa [la ˈkasa], de mi tío [de mi ˈtio], se lo dije [se lo ˈdixe] (with the jota convention below: [se lo ˈdihe]), un perro [ˈun ˈpeɹ̝o], no sé [ˈno ˈse], ha comido [ˈa koˈmido].

Source: Hualde 2005, sections on stressed and unstressed words in the chapter on stress (word classes that are inherently unstressed).

## Pausal forms

There are no pausal forms. At the end of a sentence or at a comma, each word keeps the same form it has inside the phrase. Spanish has no case endings and no nunation, and final vowels are not dropped or reduced. Final /s/ stays [s] before a pause in the Central Valley norm: más [ˈmas], los niños [los ˈniɲos]. Final /ɾ/ follows the rhotic convention below at a pause as everywhere: comer [koˈmeɹ].

Source: Hualde 2005 (no pausal morphology in Spanish); Central Valley coda /s/ retention as in the lect notes and Lipski 1994, Latin American Spanish, chapter on Costa Rica.

## Connected speech

Each word is written in its own citation form, and no cross-word process is written. Resyllabification is not shown: los otros [los ˈotɹ̝os], not [lo ˈso-]. Synalepha is not shown, so both vowels are kept: la alumna [la aˈlumna], de este [de ˈeste]. No nasal place assimilation: un poco [ˈun ˈpoko], en Guatemala [en ɡwateˈmala]. No /s/ voicing: mismo [ˈmismo], los dos [los ˈdos]. No spirantization of /b d ɡ/: they are always written [b d ɡ], as in lado [ˈlado] and la boca [la ˈboka]. Word-internal allophony is limited to the rhotic, jota and glide conventions. The transcription stays broad, close to phonemic. The notes write only the rhotic allophones, so the other allophones (such as [β ð ɣ], [ŋ] and [z]) described by Hualde are left out.

Source: Broad phonemic practice following the phoneme inventory of Hualde 2005 (chapters on phonemes and allophones). It departs from Hualde's narrow allophonic transcription by not writing approximant, assimilated or voiced allophones.

## Glides and diphthongs

Write prenuclear glides as [j w]: bien [ˈbjen], bueno [ˈbweno], ciudad [sjuˈdad], cuando [ˈkwando]. Write postnuclear glides as [i̯ u̯]: hay [ˈai̯], muy [ˈmui̯], seis [ˈsei̯s], causa [ˈkau̯sa], hoy [ˈoi̯]. Hiatus with a written accent keeps a full vowel: día [ˈdia], país [paˈis], tío [ˈtio]. Orthographic y and ll before a vowel are the consonant [ʝ] (yeísmo): yo [ˈʝo], calle [ˈkaʝe], hielo [ˈʝelo]. The conjunction y is [i] in every context, and no glide is written across the word boundary.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on glides and diphthongs (rising vs falling diphthongs, /ʝ/ vs glide).

## Other choices

### rhotics (erre tica)

Use [ɹ̝] for the trill phoneme: word-initial r, r after n, l or s, and rr. Examples: rosa [ˈɹ̝osa], perro [ˈpeɹ̝o], honra [ˈonɹ̝a], alrededor [alɹ̝edeˈdoɹ]. Use [tɹ̝] for the cluster tr: tres [ˈtɹ̝es], otro [ˈotɹ̝o]. Use [ɾ] for the tap between vowels and in all other onset clusters: pero [ˈpeɾo], brazo [ˈbɾaso], grande [ˈɡɾande]. Use [ɹ] for coda r, word-internal and word-final, including before a vowel-initial word: parte [ˈpaɹte], comer algo [koˈmeɹ ˈalɡo].

Source: Lect notes; Lipski 1994, Latin American Spanish, chapter on Costa Rica (assibilated and approximant rhotics); Fontanella de Weinberg 1992, section on Central American Spanish.

### jota

Write j, and g before e or i, as [h]: gente [ˈhente], ojo [ˈoho], México [ˈmehiko].

Source: Lipski 1994, chapter on Costa Rica (weak /x/ as [h]); Hualde 2005 (dialectal [h] for /x/ in Central America and the Caribbean). This is chosen over the [x] of central Peninsular descriptions.

### seseo and other segments

Seseo applies: c before e or i, z and s are all [s], as in cielo [ˈsjelo] and zapato [saˈpato]. Write b and v as [b], ch as [tʃ] and ñ as [ɲ]. Use five full vowels [a e i o u] with no reduction. Intervocalic /d/ is kept: cansado [kanˈsado]. Orthographic x is [ks]: taxi [ˈtaksi].

Source: Hualde 2005 (Latin American seseo and yeísmo); Penny 2002 (history of seseo in American Spanish); Fontanella de Weinberg 1992.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
