# Transcription conventions: Salvadoran Spanish (es-SV)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark primary stress with ˈ at the start of the stressed syllable, before the whole onset including clusters: otro [ˈotɾo], ciudad [sjuˈðað], país [paˈis]. Place it by the orthographic accent rules. A written acute marks the stressed vowel. Without one, words ending in a vowel, -n or -s are stressed on the penult (casa [ˈkasa], comen [ˈkomeŋ]). Words ending in any other consonant are stressed on the final syllable (hablar [aˈβlaɾ], reloj [reˈloh]). Salvadoran voseo verb forms follow the same rule from their spelling: tenés [teˈnes], sos [ˈsos], vení [beˈni]. Enclitics written together with the verb do not move the stress: dámelo [ˈdamelo], decime [deˈsime]. Every stressed word gets a mark, monosyllables included: yo [ˈʝo], sol [ˈsol]. Adverbs in -mente keep the adjective's stress as secondary ˌ and take primary stress on -men-: rápidamente [ˌrapiðaˈmente]. Do not mark syllable boundaries otherwise. Spanish has no phonemic long vowels, geminates or case endings: write no length mark, and write a double consonant letter as one sound. rr and cc are not geminates: perro [ˈpero] (trill), acción [akˈsjoŋ].

Source: Real Academia Española & ASALE (2011), Nueva gramática de la lengua española: Fonética y fonología, chs. on stress; Hualde, J.I. (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, CUP, ch. on stress (incl. -mente double stress); Quilis, A. (1993), Tratado de fonología y fonética españolas, Gredos.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Write the following closed list without a stress mark.
- Definite articles: el, la, los, las, lo.
- Short possessive determiners: mi, tu, su, mis, tus, sus, nuestro before a noun.
- Object and reflexive clitics: me, te, se, nos, os, lo, la, los, las, le, les.
- All simple prepositions except según: a, de, en, con, por, para, sin, sobre, desde, hasta, entre, hacia.
- Conjunctions: y, e, o, u, ni, pero, que, si, porque, aunque, mas, pues.
- Relatives used non-interrogatively: que, quien, donde, como, cuando, cuanto, cual.
- Forms of address and titles before a name: don, doña, san, santa, fray, sor.
- The adverb tan.
Examples: la casa [la ˈkasa], de mi tío [de mi ˈtio], lo que dice [lo ke ˈdise], para vos [paɾa ˈβos], don Juan [doŋ ˈhwaŋ].
Mark stress on everything else.
- Subject and prepositional pronouns: yo, tú, vos, él, ella, usted, nosotros, ellos, mí, ti.
- Interrogatives and exclamatives with an accent: qué, quién, dónde, cómo, cuándo.
- Indefinite articles: un, una, unos, unas, as in un perro [ˈuŋ ˈpero].
- Demonstratives, numerals, and tonic possessives such as mío and suyo.
- Adverbs, nouns, adjectives, and all verb forms. This includes auxiliaries haber, ser and estar in periphrases: ha dicho [ˈa ˈðitʃo], está comiendo [ehˈta koˈmjendo].

Source: Quilis, A. (1993), Tratado de fonología y fonética españolas, Gredos, chapter on palabras acentuadas e inacentuadas; Navarro Tomás, T. (1918/1957), Manual de pronunciación española, §§ on palabras inacentuadas; Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, ch. on stress (unstressed function words; indefinite articles stressed).

## Pausal forms

Pausal forms in the Arabic sense do not apply: Spanish has no case endings, final short-vowel deletion or nunation. The only effect of a pause is the choice of a word's final allophone. Treat the end of the sentence and any comma, colon, semicolon or dash as a pause. Before a pause, follow these rules:
- Final /n/ is velar [ŋ]: pan [ˈpaŋ], también [tamˈbjeŋ].
- Final /s/ stays [s]: dos [ˈdos], vos [ˈβos].
- Final /d/ is [ð], or [d] after a consonant: usted [uhˈteð], verdad [beɾˈdað].
- Final /ɾ/ is [ɾ]: comer [koˈmeɾ].
- Vowels are unchanged.
After a pause, a word-initial /b d ɡ/ is a stop, as at the start of the sentence.

Source: Lipski, J.M. (1994), Latin American Spanish, Longman, chapter 'El Salvador' (velar final /n/, retention of prepausal /s/ vs. preconsonantal weakening); Canfield, D.L. (1960), 'Observaciones sobre el español salvadoreño', Filología 6: 29–76.

## Connected speech

Write each word separately in its own place. Apply these processes across word boundaries in the same way as inside a word, changing only the segment on each side of the boundary.
(1) Coda /s/. Before a consonant it is [h], inside a word or at the end of a word: este [ˈehte], mismo [ˈmihmo], los niños [loh ˈniɲos], es que [ˈeh ke]. Word-final /s/ before a vowel stays [s] and is not moved to the next word: los amigos [los aˈmiɣos]. Do not aspirate word-initial or intervocalic /s/. That is a stigmatized rural feature (la semana [la seˈmana], not [la heˈmana]).
(2) Nasal place. Final or coda /n/ is [m] before /p b m/: un beso [ˈum ˈbeso], también [tamˈbjeŋ]. It is [ŋ] before /k ɡ x/, before a vowel and before a pause: un gato [ˈuŋ ˈɡato], un amigo [ˈuŋ aˈmiɣo], en agua [eŋ ˈaɣwa]. It is [n] before every other consonant, including /f t d s l r tʃ ʝ/: en tu casa [en tu ˈkasa].
(3) Voiced obstruents. /b d ɡ/ are stops [b d ɡ] after a pause and after any consonant, including [h ɾ l s]: algo [ˈalɡo], desde [ˈdehde], las vacas [lah ˈbakas], el dedo [el ˈdeðo]. They are approximants [β ð ɣ] after a vowel or glide, including across a word boundary: la boca [la ˈβoka], lo digo [lo ˈðiɣo], mi gato [mi ˈɣato].
(4) Rhotics. Word-initial r is always the trill [r], even after a vowel: la rosa [la ˈrosa].
(5) Vowels. Synalepha and vowel fusion are not written. Adjacent vowels across a boundary keep their full syllabic form, and no glide is created: mi amigo [mi aˈmiɣo], la amiga [la aˈmiɣa], todo esto [ˈtoðo ˈehto].
No liaison or resyllabification is written beyond these allophone choices.

Source: Lipski (1994), Latin American Spanish, chapter 'El Salvador' (aspiration of preconsonantal /s/, velarized /n/, occlusive /b d g/ after consonants in Central America); Canfield (1960), 'Observaciones sobre el español salvadoreño'; Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chs. on nasal assimilation, spirantization and synalepha (general Spanish baseline). This departs from the specification note, which models only seseo and yeísmo; the Salvadoran /s/, /n/ and /b d g/ patterns are added from Lipski and Canfield.

## Glides and diphthongs

Inside a word, write the non-syllabic high vowel of a rising diphthong (glide before the nucleus) as [j] or [w]: tierra [ˈtjera], bueno [ˈbweno], ciudad [sjuˈðað], agua [ˈaɣwa]. Write it as [i̯] or [u̯] in a falling diphthong (glide after the nucleus): aire [ˈai̯ɾe], hay [ˈai̯], muy [ˈmui̯], auto [ˈau̯to], hoy [ˈoi̯]. When a written accent or hiatus puts the stress on i or u, write a full vowel: día [ˈdia], país [paˈis], baúl [baˈul]. Keep the glide [j] distinct from the consonant spelled y or ll. That consonant is written [ʝ] in every position, because of yeísmo: yo [ˈʝo], calle [ˈkaʝe], mayo [ˈmaʝo], silla [ˈsiʝa]. Weakening or loss of this consonant next to front vowels (silla [ˈsia], gallina [ɡaˈina]) is a recognized Salvadoran feature, but it is variable and is not written. Never create glides across word boundaries.

Source: Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chs. on diphthongs/glides and on palatal /ʝ/; Lipski (1994), Latin American Spanish, chapter 'El Salvador' (yeísmo; weak intervocalic /ʝ/ with loss next to front vowels, not transcribed here).

## Other choices

### velar fricative /x/

Write /x/ (spelled j, or g before e/i) as glottal [h]: gente [ˈhente], ajo [ˈaho], reloj [reˈloh], México [ˈmehiko]. Silent h is not written: hora [ˈoɾa].

Source: Lipski (1994), Latin American Spanish, chapter 'El Salvador' (weak aspirated /x/ in Central America); Canfield (1960).

### seseo and coronal fricatives

Write c before e/i, z and s all as /s/, subject to the coda rule: cielo [ˈsjelo], zapato [saˈpato], hacer [aˈseɾ]. Write x as [ks] between vowels (examen [ekˈsameŋ]). Before a consonant, x is /s/ and so [h] (extraño [ehˈtɾaɲo]). Spanish-spelled Nawat loans take ordinary Spanish letter values; tz is [ts].

Source: Lipski (1994), Latin American Spanish, general chapters (seseo); Lipski (2011), 'Geographical and social varieties of Spanish: An overview', Handbook of Hispanic Linguistics, pp. 1–26.

### rhotics

Write the tap [ɾ] for single r between vowels and in codas: pero [ˈpeɾo], comer [koˈmeɾ]. Write the trill [r] for rr, for word-initial r, and for r after l, n or s: perro [ˈpero], rosa [ˈrosa], honra [ˈonra], Israel [ihraˈel]. No assibilated rhotics: Salvadoran Spanish lacks the assibilation found in Guatemala and Costa Rica.

Source: Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, ch. on rhotics; Lipski (1994), chapters 'El Salvador' vs. 'Guatemala'/'Costa Rica'.

### final /d/

Write word-final /d/ as [ð], or [d] after a consonant, in every position. Do not delete it, although it is often weak or lost in casual speech: usted [uhˈteð], verdad [beɾˈdað], Madrid [maˈðɾið]. Intervocalic -ado keeps [ð]: cansado [kanˈsaðo].

Source: Lipski (1994), Latin American Spanish, chapter 'El Salvador' (weakening of final /d/, not transcribed); Hualde (2005), ch. on /d/.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
