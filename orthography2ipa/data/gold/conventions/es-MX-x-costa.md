# Transcription conventions: Mexican Spanish (Coastal) (es-MX-x-costa)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark primary stress with ˈ at the start of the stressed syllable of every stressed word, monosyllables included: [ˈsol], [ˈkasa], [kaˈfe]. Leave unstressed function words (see function_words) unmarked. Place stress by spelling, as Spanish stress is lexical. A written accent marks the stressed vowel: [ˈaɾβol], [teˈlefono]. Without one, stress the penult if the word ends in a vowel, n or s: [ˈkasa], [ˈtjeneŋ], [ˈlibɾoh]. Otherwise stress the last syllable: [paˈpel], [kanˈtaɾ], [beɾˈðað]. Decide stress from the underlying spelled form, never from the surface form after aspiration or velarisation: 'canción' [kanˈsjoŋ]. Syllabify inside the word: one consonant goes to the following vowel; two consonants split, except obstruent + l/ɾ clusters, which are onsets ('hablar' [aˈβlaɾ], 'atlas' [ˈatlah]); an aspirated coda stays in its syllable ('este' [ˈehte], 'después' [dehˈpweh]). Adverbs in -mente carry one mark, on the adjective's stressed syllable: [ˈɾapiðamente]. Spanish has no phonemic vowel length, no geminates and no case endings, so these never occur: write no ː and no doubled consonants ('perro' [ˈpero] with trill, see other).

Source: Hualde 2005, The Sounds of Spanish, chapter on word stress (orthographic accent rules and stress placement).

## Stressed and unstressed words

Leave these words UNSTRESSED, with no ˈ. Definite articles: el la los las lo, e.g. [la ˈkasa], [loh ˈniɲoh]. Unstressed possessives: mi tu su mis tus sus nuestro (prenominal), e.g. [mi ˈmaðɾe]. Object and reflexive clitics, whether before the verb or joined to it, and the same when hyphen-split into words of their own: me te se lo la le nos los las les, e.g. [me ˈðiho]. All simple prepositions except según: a ante bajo con contra de desde en entre hacia hasta para por sin sobre tras. Conjunctions: y e ni o u que pero mas sino aunque porque, and si meaning 'if'. Relative (non-interrogative) que quien cual donde cuando como. Titles before names: don doña fray san. Write STRESSED, with ˈ: indefinite articles un una unos unas ([ˈuŋ ˈlibɾo]); demonstratives; subject pronouns yo tú él ella usted nosotros ellos; prepositional pronouns mí ti sí; tonic possessives mío tuyo suyo; interrogatives and exclamatives qué quién cuál dónde cuándo cómo cuánto; numerals; según; no; sí; all adverbs, nouns, adjectives; every verb form, including the auxiliaries haber, ser and estar ([ˈa ˈβenið]... [ˈeh ˈmio]). The test is the word, not the sentence: never shift or drop a stress for phrasal rhythm or emphasis.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on stress (lists of stressed and unstressed words); Quilis 1993, Tratado de fonología y fonética españolas, palabras tónicas y átonas.

## Pausal forms

Pausal forms in the Arabic sense (dropping final short vowels or case endings, nunation/tanwīn) do not apply to Spanish. Word-final segments depend only on what follows, and a pause (sentence end, comma, any punctuation) counts like a following consonant. Before a pause, coda /s/ is [h]: [ˈkasah]. Before a pause, final /n/ is [ŋ]: [kanˈsjoŋ]. Final vowels are never dropped or devoiced in writing. A word right after a pause or at sentence start begins with stop [b d g], not [β ð ɣ]: '¿Dónde?' [ˈdonde].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapters on fricatives (aspiration of /s/) and nasals (velarisation of final /n/); Lope Blanch 1972 on coastal Mexican norms.

## Connected speech

Write segmental processes that depend on the neighbouring word on the word they affect. Each word stays a separate IPA word; never join words, never move a consonant into the next word, and never mark syllable boundaries across words. (1) Spirantisation: /b d g/ are stops [b d g] after a pause, after a nasal, and for /d/ after l. Elsewhere, including after a vowel, after [h] and after ɾ, and across word boundaries, they are [β ð ɣ]: [la ˈβoka], [um ˈbeso], [el ˈdeðo], [loh ˈðeðoh], [aˈβlaɾ]. (2) Final /n/ assimilates to a following consonant: [m] before p b m ([um ˈpeso]), [ɱ] before f ([eɱ ˈfɾansja]), [ŋ] before k g h, a vowel or a pause ([uŋ ˈɡato], [uŋ ˈamiɣo], [ˈβjeneŋ]), and [n] before every other consonant ([en ˈtoðo], [uŋ]→[un ˈlibɾo]). The same rule applies inside words: [ˈteŋɡo], [eɱˈfeɾmo]. (3) Coda /s/ is [h] before a consonant or pause, inside a word or across a boundary: [ˈehte], [ˈmihmo], [loh ˈniɲoh]. Word-final /s/ before a vowel-initial word stays [s]: [los aˈmiɣoh], [ˈmas ˈaɣwa]. Never write /s/ deletion. (4) Do not write vowel contraction, synalefa or elision across words: keep every vowel of each word, [la aˈmiɣa], [ˈkasa ˈaɾta]. Write no ‿ ligature.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapters on stops/approximants, nasals, fricatives and the syllable in connected speech; aspiration pattern as described for Gulf coast Mexican in Lope Blanch 1972.

## Glides and diphthongs

Write a prenuclear (rising) glide as [j w]: [ˈtjena], [ˈbweno], [kwiˈðaðo], [ˈpjano]. Write a postnuclear (falling) glide as [i̯ u̯]: [ˈai̯ɾe], [ˈau̯to], [ˈoi̯], [ˈmui̯], [ˈbei̯nte]. Treat orthographic y/ll before a vowel as the consonant [ʝ], not a glide: [ˈʝo], [ˈmaʝo], [ˈʝelo] ('hielo'), [ˈkaʝe]. Word-initial hue/hui is [w], not [gw]: [ˈweβo]. A stressed i or u next to another vowel is a full vowel in hiatus, unmarked: [ˈdia], [ˈrio], [paˈis], [ɾeˈuno]. Unstressed e o a next to vowels stay full vowels: [poˈeta], [teaˈtɾo]. The conjunction y is always [i]. Never form glides across word boundaries.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on glides and diphthongs (notation [j w] vs [i̯ u̯] and hiatus).

## Other choices

### velar fricative /x/

Write orthographic j, ge, gi and Mexican x (México, Oaxaca) as glottal [h] everywhere: [ˈhente], [ˈkaha], [ˈmehiko]. Never write [x].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on fricatives (dialectal [h] for /x/); lect notes.

### seseo and yeísmo

Write c before e/i, z and s as [s]: [ˈsjelo], [ˈsapato]. Write ll and consonantal y as [ʝ] in every position, including after a nasal or pause, with no [ɟʝ] and no [ʎ]: [ˈʝeɣa], [kon ˈʝo]. Write ch as [tʃ].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapters on fricatives and palatals; Penny 2002 on Latin American seseo and yeísmo.

### rhotics and liquid neutralisation

Write the trill [r] for rr, word-initial r and r after n, l or s: [ˈpero] 'perro' vs [ˈpeɾo] 'pero', [el ˈrio], [onˈraðo]. Write every other r, including codas, as the tap [ɾ]: [kanˈtaɾ], [ˈmaɾ aˈsul]. Do not write the variable coastal l/ɾ neutralisation the notes mention; keep /l/ and /ɾ/ as spelled, per the educated norm.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on liquids; Lope Blanch 1972 on the prestige norm.

### weak /d/ and final /d/

Write intervocalic /d/ as [ð] and never delete it: [kanˈsaðo]. Write word-final d as [ð]: [beɾˈðað], [uˈsteð]. Do not write casual deletion.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on stops and approximants (deletion treated as stylistic).

### tl and x clusters

Keep tl as a tautosyllabic onset, the Mexican norm: [ˈatlah], [atˈlantiko] is wrong, write [aˈtlantiko]. Write intervocalic x as [ks]: [ekˈsameŋ], [ˈtaksi].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on the syllable (Mexican tl onset); Lope Blanch 1972.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
