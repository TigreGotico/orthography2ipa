# Transcription conventions: Western Andalusian Spanish (es-ES-x-andalusi-w)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Primary stress is lexical and falls where standard Spanish spelling places it. A written accent marks the stressed vowel. Without one, stress falls on the penult if the word ends in a vowel, -n or -s, and on the final syllable otherwise. Place ˈ before the onset of the stressed syllable, taking the longest legal onset: [ˈkasa], [kanˈsao], [ˈpweβlo], [ehˈpaɲa]. Stress stays on its lexical syllable even after final consonants are aspirated or deleted: comer [koˈme], verdad [beɾˈða], Jesús [heˈsuh]. Every stressed word, monosyllables included, gets ˈ: sol [ˈso], pan [ˈpaŋ]. Adverbs in -mente keep both stresses: claramente [ˈklaɾaˈmente]. Secondary stress is not marked otherwise. Spanish has no phonemic long vowels, geminates or case endings, so that part does not apply. Clusters such as mismo are always written with coda [h] ([ˈmihmo]), never as the narrow assimilated geminate [ˈmimmo].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on word stress (lexical stress, the orthographic default rules, two stresses in -mente adverbs).

## Stressed and unstressed words

Unstressed words get no ˈ. These are the definite articles el la lo los las; prenominal possessives mi tu su mis tus sus nuestro; all object and reflexive clitics me te se lo la le nos os los las les, whether proclitic or hyphen-joined enclitic; all simple prepositions except según (a de en con por para sin sobre hasta desde); conjunctions y e o u ni pero que si como (comparative) aunque porque; and relatives que quien donde cuando cual used non-interrogatively. Stressed words get ˈ. These are subject and prepositional pronouns (yo tú él ella nosotros mí ti); the indefinite articles un una unos unas; demonstratives; interrogatives and exclamatives (qué cómo dónde); numerals; según; the negator no; postnominal possessives (mío); and all verbs, including auxiliary haber and ser/estar. Examples: los niños [loh ˈniɲoh], se lo dio [se lo ˈðjo], para mí [paɾa ˈmi], un perro [ˈum ˈpero], ha venido [ˈa βeˈniðo], no sé [ˈno ˈse].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on stress, list of inherently stressed vs unstressed word classes (following Navarro Tomás' classification).

## Pausal forms

Pausal forms apply before any pause: at the end of the sentence and before a comma, colon, semicolon or other punctuation mark. In the word before a pause, final /s/ (written s, z, or c in seseo words) is deleted: las casas. → [lah ˈkasa], la luz, → [la ˈlu], más. → [ˈma]. Final /ɾ/ and /l/ are also deleted: comer. → [koˈme], papel, → [paˈpe]. Inside a phrase the same /s/ is [h] (see connected_speech). The following apply in every position, not only at pause: final /n/ is [ŋ] ([ˈpaŋ]), and final /d/ is deleted (verdad [beɾˈða], Madrid [maˈðɾi]). Final vowels are written with their normal quality, and the vowel opening that marks plurals (an Eastern Andalusian feature) is not written. Case endings and nunation do not apply to Spanish.

Source: Zamora Vicente 1960, chapter 'El andaluz' (loss of final -s, -r, -l, -d; Western vs Eastern treatment of the preceding vowel); Hualde 2005, chapter on /s/ aspiration and deletion in southern Peninsular Spanish.

## Connected speech

Write each word in its own slot. Change its final or initial segment according to its neighbour, with no ligatures and no syllable dots. (1) Word-final /s/ before a consonant or a vowel inside the phrase is [h], and it stays written on the first word: los amigos [loh aˈmiɣo], es verdad [ˈeh βeɾˈða]. (2) Word-final /n/ before a consonant takes the following place: [m] before p b m f, [n] before t d s l ɾ r tʃ ʃ ʝ, [ŋ] before k ɡ h w, and before a vowel or pause. Examples: un beso [ˈum ˈbeso], con todo [kon ˈtoðo], son amigos [ˈsoŋ aˈmiɣo]. (3) Word-final liquids before a consonant are [ɾ] and before a vowel keep their identity: el niño [eɾ ˈniɲo], el agua [el ˈaɣwa], comer pan [koˈmeɾ ˈpaŋ], comer algo [koˈmeɾ ˈalɣo]. (4) The voiced stops /b d ɡ/ are [b d ɡ] after a pause or a nasal, and /d/ is also [d] after /l/; they are [β ð ɣ] everywhere else, across word boundaries too: la boca [la ˈβoka], los dedos [loh ˈðeðo], en Galicia [eŋ ɡaˈlisja]. (5) Vowels in contact across words (synalephа) are not elided, merged or glided: both are written, as in la_alumna [la aˈlumna] and y es [i ˈeh]. (6) Resyllabification is not shown beyond these rules.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapters on nasal assimilation, spirantization of voiced stops, and resyllabification across word boundaries; Zamora Vicente 1960, 'El andaluz', for -n velarisation and coda liquid neutralisation.

## Glides and diphthongs

Non-syllabic high vowels are written [j] and [w], in both rising and falling diphthongs and in triphthongs; i̯ and u̯ are not used. Examples: bien [ˈbjeŋ], bueno [ˈbweno], hay [ˈaj], causa [ˈkawsa], muy [ˈmuj], buey [ˈbwej]. Word-initial hie- and orthographic y/ll before a vowel are the consonant [ʝ]: hielo [ˈʝelo], yo [ˈʝo]. Word-initial hue- is [w] ([ˈweβo]), with no epenthetic [ɣ]. The conjunction y is [i]. Hiatus words with an accented i or u keep two syllables: día [ˈdia], país [paˈih].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on glides and diphthongs (glides as non-syllabic allophones of /i u/; hiatus vs diphthong).

## Other choices

### seseo vs ceceo

Follow seseo (the urban Sevilla norm). Write orthographic s, z and c before e/i alike as [s], and write no [θ]: casa [ˈkasa], caza [ˈkasa], cielo [ˈsjelo]. The notes allow either; ceceo ([θ] for all), found in Cádiz, rural Huelva and Málaga, is not used.

Source: Zamora Vicente 1960, 'El andaluz' (map and distribution of seseo/ceceo); Penny 2002, on the origin of seseo/ceceo.

### velar fricative and orthographic h

The phoneme /x/ (written j, or g before e/i) is [h]: jamón [haˈmoŋ], gente [ˈhente]. Orthographic h is silent: hombre [ˈombɾe]. The rural aspiration of Latin F- ([ˈhumo] for humo) is not written.

Source: Hualde 2005, dialect chapter on /x/ → [h] in Andalusia; Zamora Vicente 1960, 'El andaluz'.

### yeísmo

ll and y are the same phoneme, always written [ʝ], with no [ɟʝ] after a pause or nasal and no [ʎ]: calle [ˈkaʝe], yo [ˈʝo], conyuge [ˈkoɲʝuhe].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on palatals and yeísmo; Penny 2002, on the spread of yeísmo.

### ch

Written ch is deaffricated to [ʃ] everywhere (Sevilla/Cádiz): mucho [ˈmuʃo], noche [ˈnoʃe].

Source: Hualde 2005, dialect section on [ʃ] for /tʃ/ in western Andalusia; Zamora Vicente 1960, 'El andaluz'.

### coda liquids inside words

A word-internal coda /l/ before a consonant is neutralised to [ɾ], and coda /ɾ/ stays [ɾ]: alto [ˈaɾto], algo [ˈaɾɣo], carta [ˈkaɾta]. The trill /r/ (word-initial r, rr, and r after n, l, s) is [r]: rosa [ˈrosa], perro [ˈpero], los reyes [loh ˈreʝe]. Elsewhere r is the tap [ɾ].

Source: Zamora Vicente 1960, 'El andaluz' (confusion of -l/-r); Hualde 2005, chapters on rhotics and liquid neutralisation.

### intervocalic /d/

/d/ is deleted in the endings -ado and -ada, including participles and nouns: cansado [kanˈsao], cantada [kanˈta]. The pronunciation of -ada as [ˈa] follows Zamora Vicente's description of Andalusian contraction, which is stronger than the general lenition in the notes. In all other intervocalic positions /d/ is [ð]: todo [ˈtoðo], perdido [peɾˈðiðo].

Source: Zamora Vicente 1960, 'El andaluz' (loss of intervocalic -d-); Hualde 2005, chapter on /d/ weakening.

### other coda consonants

Inside a word, coda /s/ before a consonant is [h] (mismo [ˈmihmo], España [ehˈpaɲa]); the consonant after it is not written as aspirated or geminate. Other coda obstruents (p, k, b, g, t) are kept as written, in broad form: doctor [dokˈtoɾ] (prepausal [dokˈto]), apto [ˈapto].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on /s/ aspiration and coda weakening in southern Peninsular Spanish.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
