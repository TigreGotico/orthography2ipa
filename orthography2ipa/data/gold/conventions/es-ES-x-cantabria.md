# Transcription conventions: Cantabrian Spanish (Montañés) (es-ES-x-cantabria)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Stress is lexical and is placed by the spelling. A written accent marks the stressed vowel. With no written accent, a word ending in a vowel, n or s takes penultimate stress; any other word takes final stress. Write ˈ before the onset of the stressed syllable, using normal Spanish syllabification (one intervocalic consonant goes to the following syllable; the clusters pl bl pr br tr dr kl gl kr gr fl fr stay together). Every stressed word carries ˈ, including monosyllables: pan ˈpan, casa ˈkas̺a, canción kanˈθjon, reloj reˈlox, árbol ˈaɾβol. Words in -mente take ˌ on the adjective stress and ˈ on -men-: fácilmente ˌfaθilˈmente. Unstressed function words (see function_words) carry no mark. Long vowels, geminates and case endings do not apply: Spanish has no phonemic length and no case suffixes. Double letters are written as single segments, and rr is the trill r.

Source: Hualde 2005, The Sounds of Spanish, ch. 11 (word stress); Alarcos Llorach 1994, Gramática, §§ on accent (acento).

## Stressed and unstressed words

Unstressed words get no ˈ and keep their full vowels. These are: definite articles el la lo los las (el ɛ̃? no: el el, la la). Object and reflexive clitics me te se lo la le nos os les, also when written with a hyphen or attached by the engine. Prenominal possessives mi tu su mis tus sus nuestro (prenominal). All prepositions except según: a de en con por para sin sobre entre hasta desde hacia contra ante bajo tras. Conjunctions y e o u ni que pero mas si porque aunque cuando, and como when it is a conjunction. Relatives que quien cual donde cuando. Forms of address don doña san fray sor. Stressed words get ˈ. These are: indefinite articles and numerals un ˈun, una ˈuna, dos ˈdos̺. Demonstratives este ˈes̺te, ese ˈes̺e. Tonic pronouns yo ˈʝo, tú ˈtu, él ˈel, ella ˈeʝa, mí ˈmi, ti ˈti. Postnominal possessives mío ˈmio, suyo ˈs̺uʝo. Interrogatives and exclamatives qué ˈke, cómo ˈkomo, dónde ˈdonde. Negation no ˈno. Según s̺eˈɣun. All verbs, including auxiliaries and copulas: ha ˈa, he ˈe, es ˈes̺, está es̺ˈta. The regional ser/estar overlap is a matter of word choice. Transcribe the verb that is written. Example: el perro de mi casa el ˈpero ðe mi ˈkas̺a.

Source: Quilis 1993, Tratado de fonología y fonética españolas, ch. on palabras acentuadas e inacentuadas; Navarro Tomás 1918, Manual de pronunciación española, §§ 172–176; Hualde 2005, ch. 11.

## Pausal forms

Spanish has no pausal forms. Final vowels and consonants keep their connected-speech shape at a comma and at the end of a sentence. Case endings and nunation (tanwīn) do not apply. The only effect of a pause is on the choice between a stop and an approximant. A word at the start of the sentence, or right after a comma, semicolon, colon, dash, parenthesis or quotation mark, counts as post-pausal: its initial b d g are written as stops [b d ɡ]. Examples: Dame agua. ˈdame ˈaɣwa; ..., ganó ɡaˈno; ..., vino ˈbino.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 8 (voiced stops and approximants after pause); Navarro Tomás 1918, §§ on fricativization.

## Connected speech

Each word is written in its own place and in its own form. Its shape depends on the last segment of the preceding word in the same unpunctuated stretch. Three processes are written across word boundaries, and the same rules apply inside words. (1) Spirantization: /b d g/ are written β ð ɣ after a vowel or any consonant, with two exceptions: after a nasal they stay stops b d ɡ, and /d/ after l stays d. Examples: la boca la ˈβoka; un dedo ˈun ˈdeðo; el dedo el ˈdeðo; los gatos los̺ ˈɣatos̺; con gusto kon ˈɡus̺to; un vaso ˈum ˈbas̺o. (2) Nasal place assimilation: a word-final n is written m before p b m, and ŋ before k g x. Examples: un poco ˈum ˈpoko; en casa eŋ ˈkas̺a; con Juan koŋ ˈxwan. No other place assimilation is written. (3) The tap/trill choice depends on the context inside the word only; a word-initial r is always r: la rosa la ˈros̺a. The following processes are not written. Vowels across a boundary are not merged, elided or glided (no synalepha shown): la alegría la aleˈɣɾia; mi amigo mi aˈmiɣo; de otro de ˈotɾo. There is no resyllabification mark, and consonants stay with their own word: los otros los̺ ˈotɾos̺. Voicing of /s/ before voiced consonants is not written: mismo ˈmis̺mo. Casual -ado reduction is not written: cansado kanˈs̺aðo.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 8 (approximants), ch. 9 (nasal assimilation) and ch. 13 (synalepha/resyllabification, transcribed here without merger by convention).

## Glides and diphthongs

Unstressed i u next to another vowel in the same syllable are glides. Write them j w both before and after the vowel nucleus, never i̯ u̯. Examples: tierra ˈtjera, bueno ˈbweno, ciudad θjuˈðað, aire ˈajɾe, causa ˈkaws̺a, hoy ˈoj, rey ˈrej, muy ˈmuj. A written accent on i or u marks hiatus, and both vowels stay syllabic: país paˈis̺, día ˈdia, río ˈrio, baúl baˈul. Consonantal y, ll and syllable-initial hi- before a vowel are the palatal obstruent ʝ, distinct from the glide j: mayo ˈmaʝo, calle ˈkaʝe, yo ˈʝo, hielo ˈʝelo. The conjunction y is always i. In gue gui qu, u is silent; in güe güi it is w: guerra ˈɡera, pingüino piŋˈɡwino.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 10 (glides and diphthongs) and ch. 9 (palatal /ʝ/).

## Other choices

### /ʎ/ versus /ʝ/ (lleísmo/yeísmo)

Write full yeísmo: ll and y are both ʝ in onset position. Examples: llave ˈʝaβe, pollo ˈpoʝo, valle ˈbaʝe. This departs from the specification note, which calls yeísmo partial and keeps /ʎ/ for older speakers. Published descriptions give yeísmo as the current standard in Cantabria, with lleísmo only as a receding rural feature. The note also confuses distinción with lleísmo: distinción is the θ/s contrast, treated below.

Source: Penny 2002, A History of the Spanish Language, § on yeísmo; Hualde 2005, ch. 9 (palatals).

### θ versus s (distinción)

Distinción is kept. Spelling c before e i, and z, is θ; s is the apico-alveolar s̺, written with the subscript in every position as the specification note asks. Examples: cielo ˈθjelo, zapato θaˈpato, casa ˈkas̺a, caza ˈkaθa, voz ˈboθ. The letter x is ks̺ between vowels and s̺ before a consonant: examen ekˈs̺amen, extraño es̺ˈtɾaɲo.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 9 (fricatives, northern apical /s/); Penny 2002, § on the development of sibilants.

### Unstressed vowel raising (Asturleonese substrate)

Write the five-vowel Castilian system /a e i o u/ in every position. There is no raising of unstressed e o to i u (noche ˈnotʃe, not ˈnotʃi; perro ˈpero, not ˈperu) and no metaphony. This departs from the specification note. Final -u and metaphony are described for rural varieties such as Pasiego and Tudanca, not for the regional standard. They are also conditioned by morphology (for example, count versus mass nouns), so they cannot be applied consistently from spelling.

Source: Penny 2002, § on Leonese and Cantabrian dialect features; Penny 1978, Estudio estructural del habla de Tudanca; Hualde 2005, ch. 6 (vowels).

### Velar fricative /x/

Write j, and g before e i, as x everywhere: hijo ˈixo, gente ˈxente, reloj reˈlox. Eastern rural aspiration [h], including h from Latin F- as in jumo for humo, is not written. Written h is silent: humo ˈumo.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 9 (northern peninsular /x/); Penny 2002, § on F- > h.

### Word-final d

Write word-final d as ð, not as θ or zero: Madrid maˈðɾið, verdad beɾˈðað, usted us̺ˈteð.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 8 (final /d/ variants).

### Rhotics and other consonant symbols

Write the trill r for rr, for word-initial r, and for r after n l s: honra ˈonra, alrededor alreðeˈðoɾ, perro ˈpero. Write the tap ɾ elsewhere, including in codas: pero ˈpeɾo, mar ˈmaɾ, tres ˈtɾes̺. Other symbols: ch tʃ (mucho ˈmutʃo); ñ ɲ (año ˈaɲo); IPA ɡ for stop g; f is f; and b and v are the same phoneme (vaca ˈbaka, la vaca la ˈβaka).

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 9 (rhotics, affricate, palatal nasal) and ch. 8 (b/v).

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
