# Transcription conventions: Cuban Spanish (es-CU)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Primary stress is lexical and follows the standard spelling. A written accent marks the stressed vowel: [kaˈfe], [ˈarβol], [ˈmuʃiko] is wrong and the correct form is [ˈmusiko]. Without a written accent, stress falls on the penult when the word ends in a vowel, n or s: [ˈkasa], [ˈtjenen], [ˈlunes]. Otherwise it falls on the final syllable: [aˈβlaɾ], [papel] → [paˈpel]. Write ˈ immediately before the onset of the stressed syllable, and write it on every stressed word, monosyllables included: [ˈsol], [ˈpaŋ]. Unstressed function words (see function_words) get no mark. Write no secondary stress, except that -mente adverbs keep the stress of the adjective plus a stress on -men-, both written ˈ: [ˈklaɾaˈmente]. Stress stays where the standard places it after Cuban coda processes: cansado [kanˈsao], verdad [beɾˈða], usted [uhˈte]. Spanish has no phonemic long vowels, no geminates and no case endings. Write no length mark. Do not write the gemination that arises from western Cuban liquid assimilation (see other).

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on word stress (stress rules and orthographic accent); Navarro Tomás, Manual de pronunciación española, stress chapters.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Unstressed, with no ˈ: definite articles (el, la, los, las, lo); object and reflexive clitics (me, te, se, lo, la, le, nos, los, las, les); possessive determiners before the noun (mi, tu, su, nuestro); all prepositions except según; the conjunctions y, o, ni, pero, que, si, porque, como, aunque, mas; relative que, quien, donde, cuando, como; and tan, and titles such as don, doña. Examples: [el ˈniɲo], [se lo ˈdihe], [poɾ la ˈkaʝe], [mi ˈkasa]. Stressed, with ˈ: indefinite un, una, unos, unas [ˈuŋ], [ˈuna]; subject and prepositional pronouns (yo, tú, él, ella, mí, ti, usted) [ˈʝo], [paɾa ˈmi]; demonstratives; interrogatives and exclamatives with written accent [ˈke], [ˈdonde]; según [seˈɣuŋ]; numerals; quantifiers; negative no [ˈno]; and all verbs, auxiliaries included (haber, ser, estar). Example: ha comido [ˈa koˈmiðo], está hablando [ehˈta aˈβlando].

Source: Navarro Tomás, Manual de pronunciación española, chapter on stressed and unstressed words; Hualde 2005, chapter on stress (unstressed function words); Quilis 1993, Tratado de fonología y fonética españolas.

## Pausal forms

Pausal position is at the end of the sentence and before any comma or other punctuation that marks a pause. There, coda /s/ is written [h]: [ˈdoh], [loh ˈniɲoh]. Final /n/ is written [ŋ]: [ˈpaŋ], [ˈtjenen] → [ˈtjeneŋ]. Final /d/ is deleted: [beɾˈða]. Final vowels are written in full and unchanged: [ˈkasa], [ˈtaɾde]. Spanish has no case endings and no nunation (tanwīn), so nothing else changes.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapters on /s/ aspiration and nasal velarization in Caribbean Spanish; Terrell (1979) on Cuban /s/; Lipski 1994, Latin American Spanish, chapter on Cuba.

## Connected speech

Cross-word processes are written inside the word they affect, and each word keeps its own place. (1) Word-final /s/ before a vowel-initial word is [s]: [loh ˈotɾos] is wrong and the correct form is [los ˈotɾos], and [mas aˈɾiβa]. Before a consonant-initial word it is [h]: [loh ˈβeʝoh]. (2) Word-final /n/ before a vowel is [ŋ]: [eŋ ehˈpaɲa]. Before a consonant it takes the place of that consonant: [m] before p b m, as in [um ˈpeso]; [ŋ] before k g x, as in [eŋ ˈkasa]; [n] elsewhere, as in [eŋ ˈtoðo] is wrong and the correct form is [en ˈtoðo]. (3) A word-initial /b d g/ is written [β ð ɣ] after a vowel or after a non-nasal consonant other than l before d: [la ˈβoka], [loh ˈðeðoh]. It stays a stop [b d g] after a pause, after a nasal, and, for /d/ only, after l: [um ˈbeso], [el ˈdia]. (4) Vowels in contact across a boundary (sinalefa) are not merged, deleted or glided. Both vowels are written in their own words: [la aˈmiɣa], [ˈteŋgo ˈotɾo]. (5) Syllable boundaries are not written, and resyllabification is shown only through (1).

Source: Hualde 2005, chapters on spirantization, nasal assimilation and syllabification across words; Lipski 1994, chapter on Cuba; Terrell (1979).

## Glides and diphthongs

Non-syllabic high vowels in diphthongs are written [j] and [w], never i̯ or u̯. Rising diphthongs: [ˈtjera], [ˈbweno]. Falling diphthongs: [ˈajɾe], [ˈkawsa], [ˈoj], [ˈaj]. A high vowel with a written accent is syllabic and in hiatus: día [ˈdia], país [paˈih]. The conjunction y is [i] in all positions. The consonant spelled y or ll (yeísmo) is the palatal [ʝ], not a glide: [ˈkaʝe], [ˈʝo]. The sequence left by d-deletion stays two vowels without a glide: [kanˈsao].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapters on diphthongs and on palatal consonants; Quilis 1993.

## Other choices

### /x/ and /tʃ/

Write /x/ (j, g before e/i) as [h]: [haˈmah], [ˈhente]. Write ch as [tʃ] everywhere: [ˈmutʃo], [ˈnotʃe]. This follows the standard Havana norm and departs from the note's western deaffrication to [ʃ].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on dialectal variation in consonants; Lipski 1994, chapter on Cuba.

### Liquids

Keep coda /ɾ/ and /l/ distinct, as in the educated norm: [ˈkaɾne], [ˈalto], [koˈmeɾ]. Do not write lateralization ([koˈmel]), and do not write the western Cuban assimilation and gemination ([ˈkanne]). This departs from the note's liquid neutralisation, which the descriptions treat as socially marked. Write the tap as [ɾ] and the trill (initial r, rr, after n, l, s) as [r]: [ˈroxo] is wrong and the correct form is [ˈroho], and [ˈpero] is wrong where perro is meant, which is [ˈpero] with the trill [r]: [ˈpero] for pero and [ˈpero] for perro, distinguished as [ˈpeɾo] against [ˈpero].

Source: Guitart (1976), Markedness and a Cuban dialect of Spanish; Hualde 2005, chapter on rhotics and laterals; Lipski 1994, chapter on Cuba.

### Coda /s/ inside words

Write word-internal preconsonantal /s/ as [h]: [ˈmihmo], [ehˈtaŋ], [ˈehte]. Write it as [s] before a vowel: [ˈkasa].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on /s/ aspiration; Terrell (1979).

### /d/ deletion

Delete /d/ only in the -ado/-ada endings of past participles and participle-derived adjectives, and in word-final position: [kanˈsao], [eŋkanˈtaa] → write -ada as [ˈaa] only when the speaker norm deletes it; otherwise [aˈða]. For a uniform rule, delete it in -ado [ˈao] and keep it in -ada [ˈaða], and also delete word-final d [uhˈte], [ˈɾeðe] → [ˈɾe]. Keep every other intervocalic /d/ as [ð]: [ˈkaða], [ˈtoðo].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on spirantization and /d/ weakening; Lipski 1994, chapter on Cuba.

### Seseo and yeísmo

Write c before e/i and z as [s]: [ˈsjelo], [kaˈβesa]. Write ll and consonantal y as [ʝ] in all positions, with no affricate variant: [ˈʝama], [koŋ ˈʝeso].

Source: Fontanella de Weinberg 1992, chapter on Antillean Spanish; Penny 2002, chapter on American Spanish; Hualde 2005.

### Word-internal nasals

A preconsonantal nasal inside a word takes the place of the following consonant: [ˈkampo], [ˈteŋgo], [ˈkanta]. Word-final /n/ follows connected_speech and pausal_forms.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on nasal assimilation and velarization.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
