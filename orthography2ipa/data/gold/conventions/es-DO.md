# Transcription conventions: Dominican Spanish (es-DO)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Lexical stress is taken from the spelling of the full written word, before any deletion. A written accent marks the stressed vowel. Otherwise, words ending in a vowel, n or s stress the penultimate syllable, and all other words stress the final one. Write ˈ before the onset of the stressed syllable. The onset includes clusters and prenuclear glides: [ˈtjene], [ˈbweno], [ˈotɾo]. Mark every stressed word, including monosyllables: sol [ˈsol], tres [ˈtɾe]. Do not mark unstressed function words (see function_words). Deleting final /s/ or /d/ never moves stress: lunes [ˈlune], después [dehˈpwe], verdad [beɾˈða], jamás [haˈma]. Enclitics written joined to a verb leave stress on the host: dámelo [ˈdamelo]. Adverbs in -mente get ˈ on -men- and ˌ on the adjective's stressed syllable: rápidamente [ˌrapiðaˈmente]. Spanish has no phonemic long vowels, no geminates and no case endings, so these never arise.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on word stress (orthographic stress rules, stress in -mente adverbs).

## Stressed and unstressed words

Unstressed (no ˈ): definite articles el, la, lo, los, las. Prenominal possessives mi, tu, su, nuestro (with plurals). Object and reflexive clitics me, te, se, lo, la, le, nos, les, los, las. All simple prepositions except según: a, ante, con, de, desde, en, entre, hacia, hasta, para, por, sin, sobre. Conjunctions y, o, ni, pero, que, si, porque, aunque, como (non-interrogative). Relatives que, quien, donde, cuando, cual. Also tan, and the titles don and doña. Stressed (ˈ): subject and prepositional pronouns yo, tú, él, ella, usted, ustedes, mí, ti. Interrogatives and exclamatives qué, cómo, dónde, cuándo, quién. Demonstratives, numerals (including un/una as numeral or indefinite article), stressed possessives (mío, suyo), según, nouns, adjectives, adverbs, and every verb form, including the auxiliaries haber, ser and estar. Unstressed words are written in full, never reduced: para [paɾa], not [pa]. Examples: me lo dio [me lo ˈðjo]; la casa de Juan [la ˈkasa ðe ˈhwaŋ]; ¿qué quieres? [ˈke ˈkjeɾe]; ha comido [ˈa koˈmiðo]; yo sé [ˈʝo ˈse].

Source: Hualde 2005, section on stressed and unstressed words (following Quilis's classification).

## Pausal forms

Does not apply: Spanish has no pausal forms, case endings or nunation. Words take the same form at a comma, at the end of a sentence and inside the phrase. Final vowels are never dropped. Word-final rules (zero for final /s/ and /d/, [ŋ] for final /n/) apply identically in every position. Pause matters in only one place: a word-initial /b d ɡ/ right after a pause is written as a stop (see connected_speech).

Source: Hualde 2005 (no pausal morphology in Spanish); Lipski 1994, Latin American Spanish, Dominican Republic chapter (Dominican final /s/ loss and velar /n/ are not restricted to prepausal position).

## Connected speech

Each word stays in its own slot, and nothing is joined, elided or resyllabified in writing. Write both vowels of a vowel–vowel contact across words (synalefa is not shown): la ola [la ˈola]; mi amigo [mi aˈmiɣo]; casa entera [ˈkasa enˈteɾa]. Three cross-word processes are written. (1) Spirantization: a word-initial /b d ɡ/ (spelled b, v, d, g before a/o/u, gu) is written [β ð ɣ] when the preceding word in the same phrase ends in a vowel or in any consonant other than a nasal. This includes words whose final /s/ is deleted. It stays [b d ɡ] at sentence start, after a comma or other pause, and after a nasal. /d/ also stays a stop after l. Examples: la boca [la ˈβoka]; los dedos [lo ˈðeðo]; el dedo [el ˈdeðo]; el gato [el ˈɣato]; un beso [uŋ ˈbeso]; sentence-initial Dios [ˈdjo]. The same rule applies word-internally: desde [ˈdehðe], abogado [aβoˈɣao]. (2) Word-final /s/ is deleted before vowels, consonants and pause alike, with no linking [s]: los amigos [lo aˈmiɣo], más o menos [ˈma o ˈmeno]. (3) Word-final /n/ is [ŋ] everywhere, and before a vowel it does not relink: en agua [eŋ ˈaɣwa], son tres [ˈsoŋ ˈtɾe]. No other cross-word assimilation is written.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapters on the voiced obstruents /b d ɡ/ (spirantization contexts), on nasals (Caribbean velarization of final /n/ and blocked resyllabification) and on synalefa; Lipski 1994, Dominican Republic chapter (/s/ loss before vowels).

## Glides and diphthongs

Hualde's split applies. Prenuclear (rising) glides from unstressed i/u (also y, hi, hu) before a vowel in the same word are [j w]: tiene [ˈtjene], puerta [ˈpweɾta], cuidado [kwiˈðao], huevo [ˈweβo]. Postnuclear (falling) glides are [i̯ u̯]: aire [ˈai̯ɾe], hoy [ˈoi̯], rey [ˈrei̯], muy [ˈmui̯], causa [ˈkau̯sa], Europa [eu̯ˈɾopa]. An accented í or ú forms a hiatus and is written as a full vowel: día [ˈdia], río [ˈrio], país [paˈi]. Consonantal y and ll (yeísmo) are [ʝ]: yo [ˈʝo], calle [ˈkaʝe]. The conjunction y is always [i], even before a vowel: y aquí [i aˈki]. No glide formation is written across word boundaries.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on vowels and diphthongs (notation [j w] vs [i̯ u̯]) and section on yeísmo.

## Other choices

### coda /ɾ/ and /l/

Follow the general (Santo Domingo, educated) norm: coda /ɾ/ is [ɾ] and coda /l/ is [l]. This departs from the specification note, which cites the regional Cibaeño vocalization puerta [ˈpwei̯ta]. Neither Cibaeño vocalization nor southern lateralization ([ˈpwelta]) is written. Infinitive -r is kept: comer [koˈmeɾ], puerta [ˈpweɾta], alto [ˈalto].

Source: Lipski 1994, Dominican Republic chapter (Cibaeño vocalization and southern lateralization treated as regional); Fontanella de Weinberg 1992 on Caribbean liquids.

### word-internal coda /s/

Word-internal preconsonantal /s/ is aspirated [h], not deleted: este [ˈehte], mismo [ˈmihmo], buscar [buhˈkaɾ]. /s/ before a vowel inside a word and word-initial /s/ stay [s]: casa [ˈkasa], señor [seˈɲoɾ]. Word-final /s/ is zero (see connected_speech).

Source: Hualde 2005, section on /s/ aspiration and deletion; Lipski 1994, Dominican Republic chapter.

### /d/ deletion

Word-final /d/ is deleted: verdad [beɾˈða], usted [uhˈte], Madrid [maˈðɾi]. Intervocalic /d/ is deleted only in -ado/-ados: cansado [kanˈsao], pescados [pehˈkao]. Other intervocalic /d/ is [ð]: nada [ˈnaða], vida [ˈbiða].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on /d/ (weakening in -ado and final position); Lipski 1994, Dominican Republic chapter.

### segmental symbols

Use five vowels [a e i o u], with no open or close allophones. Seseo: c before e/i and z are [s] (cielo [ˈsjelo]). j and g before e/i are [h] (gente [ˈhente], jamás [haˈma]). ch is [tʃ], ñ is [ɲ], and h is silent. The trill [r] is used for rr, word-initial r and r after n, l, s (perro [ˈpero], rosa [ˈrosa], honra [ˈoŋra]); [ɾ] is used elsewhere. Write voiced velars with ɡ. Word-internal nasals assimilate to the following consonant: [m] before p/b/m, [ŋ] before velars, [n] elsewhere (campo [ˈkampo], tengo [ˈteŋɡo], cansado [kanˈsao]). Note that honra is a word-internal case: the internal n is [ŋ] by the general Dominican velar coda norm only word-finally, so write honra [ˈonra].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapters on consonants (seseo, rhotics, nasal assimilation); specification note for /x/ → [h].

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
