# Transcription conventions: Yucatec Mexican Spanish (es-MX-x-yucatan)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Primary stress is lexical and follows the written accent. A word with a written accent (á é í ó ú) is stressed on that vowel. A word without one ending in a vowel, -n or -s is stressed on the penult. Any other word without one is stressed on the final syllable. Examples: corazón [koɾaˈsom], teléfono [teˈlefono], dígamelo [ˈdigamelo], árbol [ˈaɾbol], pared [paˈɾed], examen [ekˈsamem], casas [ˈkasas]. Write ˈ before every stressed word, including stressed monosyllables: pan [ˈpam], tú [ˈtu], sí [ˈsi]. Place ˈ before the onset of the stressed syllable. The onset is maximal: obstruent+liquid clusters (pr br tr dr kr gr fr pl bl kl gl fl, and Mexican tl) and a prevocalic glide go with the following vowel. Examples: cuatro [ˈkwatɾo], Atlántico [aˈtlantiko], bien [ˈbjem]. Adverbs in -mente keep both stresses: ˈ on -mente and ˌ on the adjective, as in rápidamente [ˌrapidaˈmente]. Spanish has no phonemic vowel length, so never write ː. Double nn is written as two segments: innato [inˈnato]. The geminate rule otherwise does not apply. Case endings do not apply.

Source: Hualde, J.I. (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, ch. 13 (stress); RAE & ASALE (2010), Ortografía de la lengua española, ch. II §3 (acentuación gráfica).

## Stressed and unstressed words

The following are unstressed: write them with no ˈ and with full vowels, since Spanish has no vowel reduction. (a) Definite articles el la lo los las. (b) Prenominal possessives mi tu su mis tus sus. (c) Object and reflexive clitics me te se nos os lo la los las le les. (d) All simple prepositions except según: a de en con por para sin sobre desde hasta entre hacia. (e) Conjunctions y e o u ni que pero sino, conditional si, and porque. (f) Non-interrogative relatives and subordinators: que quien cuando donde como cual. (g) Clitic titles don doña san. The following are stressed and take ˈ. (a) Indefinite articles un una unos unas. (b) Subject pronouns and pronouns after a preposition: yo tú él ella usted mí ti sí. (c) Demonstratives and stressed possessives (mío, nuestro). (d) Interrogatives and exclamatives (qué quién cómo dónde cuándo cuál cuánto). (e) Numerals, según, no, muy, tan, and all other adverbs. (f) Every verb form, including the auxiliaries haber, ser and estar. Examples: el pan [el ˈpam]; un amigo [ˈum aˈmigo]; de la casa [de la ˈkasa]; me lo dio [me lo ˈdjo]; lo he visto [lo ˈe ˈbisto]; para mí [paɾa ˈmi]; con ella [kom ˈeʝa]; mi casa [mi ˈkasa]; es mío [ˈes ˈmio]; ¿por qué? [poɾ ˈke]; porque [poɾke]; cuando llegue [kwando ˈʝege]; ¿cuándo? [ˈkwando]; y [i]; o [o]; don Juan [dom ˈhwam].

Source: Navarro Tomás, T. (1918/1977), Manual de pronunciación española, ch. on 'Palabras acentuadas e inacentuadas'; Hualde, J.I. (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, ch. 13 (stressed vs unstressed words). Where Navarro Tomás treats nuestro and vuestro as unstressed, follow Hualde and stress them.

## Pausal forms

Not applicable as a separate pausal form: Spanish has no case endings, no nunation and no pausal deletion of final vowels. Write every word identically at the end of the sentence, at a comma and sentence-internally. Word-final /n/ is written [m] in all positions, so no pausal alternation arises: pan [ˈpam] in every position. Final /s/ is kept as [s] and final /d/ as [d]: verdad [beɾˈdad], más [ˈmas].

Source: Lipski, J.M. (1994), Latin American Spanish, pp. 279-282 (retained final /s/ in Yucatan); Michnowicz, J. (2008), Final -m in Yucatan Spanish, pp. 155-165. Michnowicz finds -m most frequent before pause. The spec extends it to all word-final positions for consistency, which this convention follows.

## Connected speech

Write each word in its own form and do not mark any cross-word process. (1) Do not write resyllabification: los otros [los ˈotɾos]. (2) Do not write synalepha or vowel fusion: keep both vowels, with no tie and no deletion, as in la otra [la ˈotɾa], va a ir [ˈba a ˈiɾ], mi hijo [mi ˈiho]. (3) Do not write the Yucatec glottal stop between vowels across words: la amiga [la aˈmiga], not [la ʔaˈmiga]. That stop is variable and declining (Michnowicz 2012 p.106, p.108-110). (4) Do not assimilate word-final /n/ to a following consonant: it is always [m]. Examples: en todo [em ˈtodo], un gato [ˈum ˈgato], en Mérida [em ˈmeɾida]. (5) Do not voice /s/ before a voiced consonant, within a word or across words: mismo [ˈmismo], los dedos [los ˈdedos]. (6) Do not spirantize /b d g/ after a vowel in the preceding word: la boca [la ˈboka], de dos [de ˈdos]. Inside a word, a coda nasal is written [m] before /p b m/ (including orthographic nv) and [n] elsewhere. Never write [ŋ ɱ n̪]. Examples: invierno [imˈbjeɾno], campo [ˈkampo], tango [ˈtango], cinco [ˈsinko].

Source: Hualde, J.I. (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, ch. 6 (nasal assimilation), ch. 8 (/s/ voicing), ch. 15 (synalepha), here applied as a broad, word-by-word transcription; Michnowicz, J. (2012), The Standardization of Yucatan Spanish, pp. 105-110 (glottal hiatus variable, occlusive /b d g/).

## Glides and diphthongs

Write the non-syllabic element of a diphthong as [j] or [w], never as [i̯] or [u̯]. An unaccented i/u (orthographic y in a word-final coda) next to another vowel is a glide. Rising diphthongs: bien [ˈbjem], cuando [ˈkwando], huevo [ˈwebo]. Falling diphthongs: aire [ˈajɾe], causa [ˈkawsa], hay [ˈaj], rey [ˈrej], hoy [ˈoj]. In iu/ui the second vowel is the nucleus: ciudad [sjuˈdad], muy [ˈmuj], ruido [ˈrwido]. An accented í/ú forms a hiatus: día [ˈdia], país [paˈis], baúl [baˈul]. Two of a e o are always in hiatus; do not write the Mexican casual gliding [ja]. Examples: teatro [teˈatɾo], pelear [peleˈaɾ], poeta [poˈeta]. Consonantal y/ll, and hi- before a vowel at the start of a word, are the yeísta fricative [ʝ], not [j]: ya [ˈʝa], llama [ˈʝama], hielo [ˈʝelo], reyes [ˈreʝes]. The conjunction y is [i] in all positions.

Source: Hualde, J.I. (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, ch. 7 (glides and diphthongs) and ch. 10 (/ʝ/ and yeísmo), adopting its broad [j w] notation; the [ʝ] symbol follows the spec's yeísmo convention.

## Other choices

### Voiced stops /b d g/

Write occlusive [b d g] in every position, including between vowels and word-finally. Never write [β ð ɣ]. Examples: lado [ˈlado], haba [ˈaba], agua [ˈagwa], nada [ˈnada], verdad [beɾˈdad], usted [usˈted]. This follows the traditional peninsular majority variant. Michnowicz reports that younger urban speakers increasingly use the pan-Hispanic approximants.

Source: Michnowicz, J. (2012), The Standardization of Yucatan Spanish, pp. 103-106 (Figure 4), 108-110; Lope Blanch, J.M. (1987), Estudios sobre el español de Yucatán. This differs from Hualde (2005), ch. 9, which writes spirants for general Spanish.

### Jota /x/

Write the glottal [h] for j, for g before e/i, and for x in México, Oaxaca and Texas. Examples: jefe [ˈhefe], gente [ˈhente], México [ˈmehiko], jamón [haˈmom]. Never write [x].

Source: Lipski, J.M. (1994), Latin American Spanish, pp. 279-282; Lope Blanch (1987). Highland Mexican velar [x] is not used.

### Word-final /n/

Write every word-final n as [m]: pan [ˈpam], también [tamˈbjem], son [ˈsom], un [ˈum], en [em], examen [ekˈsamem]. Word-internal n is not affected: cantar [kanˈtaɾ]. Lope Blanch's counts show [n] as the most frequent realisation (about 73%, with about 12% [m]). This convention instead follows the spec, which encodes [m] as the signature Yucatecan reflex that Michnowicz found increasing in apparent time.

Source: Michnowicz, J. (2008), Final -m in Yucatan Spanish, pp. 155-165; Michnowicz (2012), p.113; Lope Blanch (1987) for the frequencies.

### Voiceless stops

Write plain [p t k]. Do not mark glottalisation, ejective release or aspiration, which are variable and declining. Examples: casa [ˈkasa], tapa [ˈtapa].

Source: Michnowicz, J. (2012), p.106 (Figure 6), pp. 108-110; Lope Blanch (1987).

### Seseo, coda /s/, rhotics and other consonants

Seseo: c before e/i and z are written [s], as in cielo [ˈsjelo] and zapato [saˈpato]. Coda /s/ is always kept as [s]. Write the tap [ɾ] for single r between vowels and in codas: pero [ˈpeɾo], parte [ˈpaɾte]. Write the trill [r] for rr and for r at the start of a word or after n, l or s: perro [ˈpero], rosa [ˈrosa], honra [ˈonra]. Other spellings: ch [tʃ], ñ [ɲ]; h is silent; u is silent in gue gui que qui; ü is [w], as in pingüino [pinˈgwino]. Ordinary x is [ks]: éxito [ˈeksito], extra [ˈekstɾa], taxi [ˈtaksi].

Source: Hualde, J.I. (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chs. 8-10; Lipski (1994), pp. 279-282 (retained /s/).

### Maya loanwords and place names

In Yucatec Maya loanwords and place names, x is [ʃ] and tz is [ts]. Examples: Uxmal [uʃˈmal], Xcaret [ʃkaˈɾet], Chichén Itzá [tʃiˈtʃem iˈtsa]. Do not write Maya glottal stops or ejectives in these words.

Source: Lope Blanch, J.M. (1987), Estudios sobre el español de Yucatán; Lipski, J.M. (1994), pp. 279-282.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
