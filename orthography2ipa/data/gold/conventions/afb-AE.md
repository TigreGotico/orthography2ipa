# Transcription conventions: Emirati Arabic (afb-AE)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark primary stress with ˈ before the stressed syllable on every stressed word, including monosyllables (ˈbeːt). Unstressed function words (see function_words) get no mark. No secondary stress. Syllabify the whole written word, including attached proclitics and suffixes, then apply the first rule that fits. (1) A final superheavy syllable (CVːC, CVCC, CVːCC) takes stress: kiˈtaːb, maˈħall, ʔaˈbuːk. (2) Otherwise a heavy penult (CVː or CVC) takes stress: ˈmaktab, ˈbeːtah, muˈdarris, sajˈjaːra. (3) Otherwise the antepenult takes stress: ˈmadrasa, ˈʃadʒara, ilˈmadrasa. A two-syllable word with a light penult has penult stress: ˈkatab. Long vowels make a syllable heavy. Word-final long vowels in native words are written short and never take stress unless the word is a monosyllable (ˈlaː). A geminate closes the preceding syllable, so that syllable is heavy (muˈdarris). A final geminate makes the last syllable CVCC (maˈħall). The article and the proclitics w-, b-, l- are never stressed, even when the count would reach them. When a geminate straddles the stressed syllable, put the mark between its two halves (iʃˈʃams). The lect has no case endings, so none enter the count. Lexicalised adverbial -an counts as an ordinary syllable: ˈʃukran, ˈʔabadan.

Source: Eastern/Gulf weight-sensitive stress as described for Gulf Arabic by Holes (1990, Gulf Arabic, Routledge Descriptive Grammars, phonology chapter, section on stress) and Qafisheh (1977, A Short Reference Grammar of Gulf Arabic, based on Abu Dhabi speech, stress section); typology after Watson (2002, The Phonology and Morphology of Arabic, ch. 5). The specification's notes give no stress rule.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Unstressed, with no ˈ: the article il-/l-; the proclitics w-, b-, l- (and future b(a)-, which is written inside its verb anyway); simple prepositions fi, min, maʕ, ʕan, ʕind, ʕala, ʔila; conjunctions w, ʔaw, bas, ʔin, law; verbal negator ma; prohibitive la; relative illi. For example: fi lˈbeːt, min ˈweːn, ʕala ttˈtˤaːwla, illi ˈʃiftah. Stressed by the normal rule: independent pronouns (ˈʔana, ˈʔinta, ˈhuwa, ˈhiya); demonstratives (ˈhaːða, ˈhaːði, ˈðiːtʃ); interrogatives (ˈʃu, ˈweːn, ˈʃloːn, ˈmiːn); the auxiliary and copular verbs kaːn and ʔaku (ˈkaːn, ˈjikuːn); the nominal negators ˈmuː and ˈmub; ˈwiːja (with); ˈʕaʃaːn and ʕaˈʃaːn are also stressed, by the rule. Pronoun suffixes are part of their host and do not carry separate stress.

Source: Holes (1990, Gulf Arabic, phonology and particles chapters) and Qafisheh (1977), who treat prepositions, conjunctions and the article as proclitic and pronouns and demonstratives as full words. This is a project decision where the sources leave connected-speech prominence unspecified.

## Pausal forms

The lect has no separate pausal form, so the same form is written at the end of a sentence, at a comma and inside a phrase. Final case vowels are not pronounced: any case vowel the tashkeel marks on a noun or adjective is dropped (kitaːbun → kiˈtaːb). Tanwīn is dropped too, except the lexicalised adverbial -an, which is kept everywhere: ˈʃukran, ˈtˤabʕan, ˈʔabadan, ˈjamkin? not. Final short vowels that belong to dialect morphology are kept, for example the 1sg and 2sg perfect: ˈʃifti. Tāʾ marbūṭa is written -a when the word is free (ˈmadrasa) and -at when the word is in construct or carries a suffix (madraˈsatah, madˈrasat ilˈwalad). The form chosen depends on morphology, not on whether a pause follows.

Source: Holes (1990) and Qafisheh (1977): Gulf Arabic has no case inflection, and tāʾ marbūṭa alternates -a/-at by construct state. The pausal/contextual distinction of Classical and Modern Standard Arabic does not apply to this lect.

## Connected speech

Each word is written in its own place. Only two cross-word processes are written, both by changing the word they affect. (1) The helping vowel of the article and of hamzat al-waṣl is dropped after a vowel-final word: fi lˈbeːt, ʕala lˈbaħar. After a consonant-final word or at the start of an utterance it surfaces as i: ilˈbeːt, min ilˈbeːt. Wāw al-ʕaṭf attached to the article gives wil- (wilˈbeːt). (2) Sun-letter assimilation of the article applies inside the word: iʃˈʃams, fi ssˈsuːg, ittˈtˤaːwla. No other processes are written: not nasal place assimilation (min ˈbeːt, not mim), not voicing assimilation, not resyllabification across words, and no linking marks ‿. Write a written hamza at the start of a word as ʔ in every position (ˈʔana, w ʔaˈbuːk), but never write ʔ for hamzat al-waṣl.

Source: Qafisheh (1977) and Holes (1990, phonology chapter) on the article's helping vowel and sun-letter assimilation. Leaving other sandhi unwritten is a broad-transcription decision consistent with the specification.

## Glides and diphthongs

Glides are consonants and are written j and w; i̯ and u̯ are never used. Geminate glides are jj and ww: sajˈjaːra, ˈgawwa. Classical *ay and *aw (fatḥa plus yāʾ or wāw with sukūn) are monophthongised in native words and written eː and oː: ˈbeːt, ˈjoːm, ˈʃloːn. Write aj and aw only where the word keeps the diphthong: learned or Modern Standard Arabic vocabulary, for example ˈtˤajjib (geminate), and the verb ending -aw (ˈkatabaw). A glide next to a vowel stays a consonant: ˈhiya, ˈhuwa.

Source: Holes (1990, phonology chapter: monophthongisation of *ay, *aw in Gulf Arabic) and Qafisheh (1977), who write eː and oː for Abu Dhabi.

## Other choices

### jiim (ج)

Always write dʒ: ˈdʒaːr, ˈʔidʒi, raˈdʒdʒaːl. Write j only where the input itself spells ي. The Emirati [j] variant (ˈjiːt, rajˈjaːl) is not written, because it is a variable synchronic process.

Source: Szreder & Derrick (2023, JIPA 54(1):146-164, abstract): dʒ~j is a live synchronic alternation, inhibited near similar coronal obstruents. A broad transcription writes the phoneme /dʒ/. This is narrower than the specification's note of dʒ~j variation.

### qāf (ق)

Write ɡ (ˈɡaːl, ˈɡalb, ˈsuːɡ). Write dʒ only when the ق is immediately followed by i or iː in a native word, following the specification's inherited Gulf rule. Never write q, except in religious or Modern Standard Arabic loan terms that the tashkeel marks as Classical (ˈqurʔaːn).

Source: Specification note (Gulf *q → ɡ; affrication before high front vowels), supported by Alshammari (2026, p.1335, review of Gulf velar affrication). The ɡ~j variant of the note is not written, as with jiim.

### kāf (ك)

Write tʃ when the ك is immediately followed by i or iː in native vocabulary (ˈtʃibiːr). The 2fs pronoun suffix is always -itʃ or -tʃ (ˈbeːtitʃ, ˈʕindatʃ is not used; write ˈʕinditʃ). Write k everywhere else, and in learned, Modern Standard Arabic and English-loan words (kiˈtaːb if marked as Modern Standard Arabic, ˈkaːs).

Source: Specification note and Szreder & Derrick (2023): k~tʃ is a completed, lexicalised split. The before-i/iː rule is the specification's predictive approximation, which is adopted for consistency.

### interdentals and ḍād

Keep θ, ð and ðˤ. Both ض and ظ are written ðˤ: ˈðˤahr, ˈʔabjaðˤ. Emphatics are written tˤ, sˤ, ðˤ.

Source: Holes (1990) and Qafisheh (1977): the ḍ/ẓ merger to ðˤ in Gulf Arabic; the specification note on retained interdentals.

### vowel inventory and syllable structure

Short vowels a i u and long vowels aː iː uː eː oː only; raising or imāla of final -a is not written. Follow the tashkeel for vowel presence: do not add or remove syncope (ktaːb against kiˈtaːb) or gahawa-type epenthesis (ˈɡahwa against ˈɡahawa) beyond what is marked.

Source: Qafisheh (1977) phoneme inventory. Fidelity to the tashkeel follows the specification's input contract.

### loan phonemes

Write p, v and ŋ in integrated English and South-Asian loans as pronounced: ˈpaːrk, ˈvaːn, ˈʃoppiŋ.

Source: Specification note (c), on the Dubai effect.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
