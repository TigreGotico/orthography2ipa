# Transcription conventions: Hassaniya Arabic (Mauritanian) (mey-MR)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark primary stress with ˈ on every word of two or more syllables. Leave monosyllables unmarked. Count syllables after the vowel rules in 'other' have applied. Rule, applied from the end of the word: (1) a final superheavy syllable (CVːC, CVCC, or CVːCC) takes the stress; (2) otherwise a heavy penult (CVː or CVC) takes it; (3) otherwise the antepenult takes it if there is one; (4) otherwise the penult takes it. A long vowel makes its syllable heavy. A geminate closes the preceding syllable, so that syllable is heavy and the geminate is split across the boundary. Hassaniya has no case endings, so none are counted. Place ˈ immediately before the single consonant that begins the stressed syllable; any earlier consonants of a word-initial cluster stay before the mark. Examples: [ˈʃaːvu] 'they saw', [məkˈtuːb] 'written', [ttiliˈfuːn] 'the telephone', [ˈʕalləm] 'he taught', [ˈana] 'I', [ləkˈtaːb] 'the book', [əθˈnajn] 'two'. Monosyllables: [ɡaːl] 'he said', [ktəbt] 'I wrote', [dvəl] 'to spit'.

Source: The weight-sensitive stress algorithm for Bedouin-type Arabic dialects in Watson 2002 (The Phonology and Morphology of Arabic, chapter on syllable structure and stress). Taine-Cheikh 2007 (EALL, pp.240-250) treats stress as non-distinctive in Ḥassāniyya, so this is a predictable convention rather than a lexical one. Cohen & el Chennafi 1963 is cited only bibliographically.

## Stressed and unstressed words

The following get no stress mark even when they have two syllables: the article, prepositions, conjunctions, the negator, the relative pronoun and the interrogative particles. Examples: [mən] 'from', [vi] 'in', [ʕlaː] 'on', [ʕand] 'at', [w] or [u] 'and', [walla] 'or', [maː] 'not', [əlli] 'who/which'. Independent pronouns, demonstratives, auxiliaries such as [kaːn] 'was', and all content words carry stress by the stress rule. Clitics written attached to their host in the script are part of that word and are transcribed inside it, with stress counted on the whole word. Article: write it as a geminate of the first consonant before the coronals t d θ ð s z ʃ ʒ tˤ dˤ sˤ ðˤ r l n, e.g. [ttiliˈfuːn], [ʃʃams] 'the sun'. Before other consonants write [l], e.g. [lbajt] 'the house'. Before a word-initial cluster write [lə], e.g. [ləkˈtaːb]. The attached conjunction 'and' is [w] before a vowel and [u] before a consonant. A function word written as a separate word is still transcribed as its own word.

Source: Taine-Cheikh 2007, EALL pp.240-250 (morphology of the article and particles). Watson 2002 for the general Arabic pattern of unstressed proclitics and article assimilation to coronals.

## Pausal forms

Pausal forms do not apply. Ḥassāniyya has no case endings and no productive tanwīn, so every word is written in the same dialect form at the end of a sentence, at a comma and inside a sentence. Tāʾ marbūṭa is [a] when the word stands alone, e.g. [mdiːna] 'city'. In a construct phrase it is [ət] or [t], e.g. [mdiːnət]. A fossilised adverbial -an is kept as written, e.g. [ʔabadan] 'never'. Do not add or drop final vowels because of position.

Source: Taine-Cheikh 2007, EALL pp.240-250 (Ḥassāniyya as a caseless dialect with the feminine -a / construct -t alternation). This matches the general description of modern dialects in Holes 2004.

## Connected speech

No process across a word boundary is written. Each word keeps its citation form: no assimilation, elision, liaison or resyllabification between words, and every word is written in its own place. All conditioned processes are written only inside the word, including article assimilation, the voicing and devoicing of the labial spirant, and emphasis backing of /a/. Example: [ɡaːl] [ana] stays [ɡaːl ˈana] and is not written as a resyllabified form.

Source: A transcription convention for broad word-by-word IPA. Taine-Cheikh 2007 (§2.1) describes the relevant processes as word-internal conditioning.

## Glides and diphthongs

Write glides as the consonants [j] and [w], never as [i̯] or [u̯]. The four inherited diphthongs are kept and written [aj aw], with [ij uw] only where the word really contains a vowel followed by a glide. Do not write the variable monophthongs [eː oː]. Examples: [lajl] 'night', [əθˈnajn] 'two', [ʃajx] 'sheikh', [jawm] 'day'. Long /iː uː/ are written [iː uː], not [ij uw], e.g. [xfiːv] 'light (in weight)', [məkˈtuːb]. A geminate glide is written [jj] or [ww].

Source: Taine-Cheikh 2007 §2.1.3, which states that the four Classical diphthongs are preserved, with /aj aw/ only variably tending to [eː oː].

## Other choices

### labial spirant ف

Inherited ف is [v], e.g. [ʃaːvu], [dvəl]. Write it [f] when it is directly next to a voiceless consonant in the same word, e.g. [xfiːv], and when it is geminate, written [ff]. Recent European loans keep [f], e.g. [ttiliˈfuːn]. This follows the published description and departs from the spec note, which writes [v] even next to voiceless consonants.

Source: Taine-Cheikh 2007 §2.1.1.3: 'except in contact with a voiceless consonant or when it is geminate'.

### qāf and ġayn

ق is [ɡ] in inherited words, e.g. [ɡaːl] 'he said'. It is [q] in Classical borrowings, e.g. [qərˈʔaːn]. غ is [q], following the merger found for most speakers, e.g. [qaːli] 'expensive', and is always [qq] when geminate.

Source: Taine-Cheikh 2007 §2.1.1.1-2.1.1.2; Watson 2002 on Bedouin *q → [ɡ].

### interdentals, ǧīm and emphatics

ث is [θ], ذ is [ð], and both ض and ظ are [ðˤ]. ج is [ʒ]. ط is [tˤ], ص is [sˤ], and ض in the few words where it is a stop is [dˤ]. /a aː/ next to an emphatic in the same word are written [ɑ ɑː]. The lexical emphatic sonorants /rˤ lˤ zˤ/ cannot be predicted from the spelling, so they are written plain [r l z]. This is a known under-representation that agrees with the spec note.

Source: Taine-Cheikh 2007 §2.1.1.1-2.1.1.3; Watson 2002 pp.268-286 on emphasis backing.

### short vowels and ʔimāla

Short /i u/ are written [ə] (the closed-syllable merger). Short /a/ is written [a]. The centralising ʔimāla of /a/ to [æ] is variable and is not written. Long vowels are [iː uː aː]. Geminate consonants are written as doubled letters, not with ː.

Source: Taine-Cheikh 2007 §2.1.2 (citing Cohen 1970 for the i/u merger).

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
