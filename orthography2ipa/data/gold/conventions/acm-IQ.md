# Transcription conventions: Iraqi Arabic (Baghdad / gilit) (acm-IQ)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark primary stress with ˈ before the stressed syllable on every word of two or more syllables. Monosyllables get no mark. Syllables are light (CV), heavy (CVː, CVC) or superheavy (CVːC, CVCC). A geminate counts as two consonants, so it closes the syllable before it. Apply the rule in this order. (1) If the final syllable is superheavy, stress it: /kiˈtaːb/ 'book', /gaˈʕadt/ 'I sat'. (2) Otherwise, stress the rightmost heavy syllable that is not final: /ˈʔaːni/ 'I', /ˈʕallam/ 'he taught', /ˈmadrasa/ 'school', /muˈdarrisa/ 'teacher (f.)', /ˈʃiftha/ 'I saw her', /madraˈsatna/ 'our school'. (3) If no non-final syllable is heavy, stress the first syllable: /ˈkitab/ 'he wrote', /ˈʃajara/ 'tree'. A final short vowel that lengthens before a suffix takes the stress by rule (2): /ʃifˈnaːha/ 'we saw her'. There are no case endings in the dialect. Learned MSA words keep their MSA segments but take the same rule: /taqˈriːban/.

Source: Erwin 1963, A Short Reference Grammar of Iraqi Arabic, ch. 1 (stress); the specification did not consult this work, and this convention follows the rule as it is usually summarised. Consistent with Blanc 1964 ch. 3 forms. The rule follows the eastern-Arabic tradition (final superheavy, else the last heavy syllable, else the first syllable), not the Classical antepenult cap.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Stress marks show lexical stress only, and the same word gets the same mark in every sentence. Monosyllabic function words are unstressed and unmarked. These include: prepositions /min/, /fiː/, /ʕind/; conjunctions /w/, /ʔaw/, /bas/; negators /ma/, /mu/, /la/; the future particle /raħ/; the progressive /da/; and the relative /illi/ when it is monosyllabic in speech, but write /illi/ as below. Polysyllabic function words are written with their lexical stress by the stress rule, because a broad transcription marks one form per word. Examples: relative /ˈilli/, /ˈʕala/ 'on', /ˈwijja/ 'with', /ˈhuwwa/ 'he', /ˈhijja/ 'she', /ˈʔaːni/ 'I', /ˈhaːða/ 'this', /ˈʃinu/ 'what'. Proclitics written joined in the orthography belong to their host and never carry the stress: the article il-, b-, l-, w-, da-. Examples: /ilˈwalad/, /bilˈbeːt/. Enclitic pronouns count as part of the host for the stress rule: /ˈbeːtna/, /ʃifˈnaːha/.

Source: Erwin 1963 (clitics and particles, not consulted by the specification); Blanc 1964 ch. 3 for the forms of the particles; Holes 2004 (pronoun and particle inventory).

## Pausal forms

Nowhere. Iraqi Arabic has no case endings, no nunation and no pausal/contextual alternation. Every word is written in one form in every position, whether at the end of the sentence, at a comma or inside the sentence. Final short vowels are kept as they are: /ˈʔaːni/, /ˈmadrasa/. The feminine -a/-at alternation is grammatical (construct state), not pausal. Write /ˈmadrasa/ alone and /madraˈsatna/ or /madrasat/ in construct, wherever the word stands. Frozen MSA adverbs in -an are written with -an as part of the word: /taqˈriːban/.

Source: Blanc 1964 ch. 3; Holes 2004 (dialects lack i'rab and pausal forms).

## Connected speech

Write every word in its citation form, that is, the form it has after a pause. Do not write assimilation, elision, liaison, epenthesis or resyllabification across a word boundary. Only processes inside the word are written. (a) The article assimilates to a following coronal: /iʃˈʃams/, /idˈdiːn/, /ilˈwalad/. (b) Kaf affrication and emphatic backing apply inside the word. (c) Epenthetic /i/ breaks a three-consonant cluster inside the word: /ˈgilit/ 'I said'. The article is always written /il-/, with no initial ʔ, even after a vowel-final word, where speech often drops the i. Write /ˈʃifna ilˈwalad/, not *[ˈʃifna lˈwalad].

Source: Blanc 1964 §3 (gilit forms such as /gilit/ with epenthesis); Erwin 1963 (article assimilation and helping vowels, not consulted by the specification). The citation-form choice is made here for consistency.

## Glides and diphthongs

Write glides as consonants /j w/, never as i̯ u̯. Inherited diphthongs *ay *aw are monophthongised in Baghdadi gilit; write them as /eː oː/: /beːt/ 'house', /joːm/ 'day', /ʃloːn/ 'how'. Where /aj aw/ survive, write them as vowel + /j/ or /w/. This happens before a geminate, in suffixes and in some learned words: /ˈʃaːfaw/ 'they saw', /ʔajˈjaːm/ 'days'. Geminate glides are written doubled: /ˈhuwwa/, /ˈhijja/.

Source: Blanc 1964 ch. 3 (Muslim Baghdadi ē ō for *ay *aw); Watson 2002 (glides as consonants).

## Other choices

### qaf reflex

Write ق as /g/ in inherited words: /gaːl/ 'he said', /ˈgilit/ 'I said', /ˈgaʕad/ 'he sat'. Do not write the [dʒ] variant before front vowels; in urban Muslim Baghdad it is lexically restricted and rural in flavour. Here the convention departs from the specification's IQ_GILIT_AFFRIC_G allophone rule. Learned MSA and religious words keep /q/: /ilqurˈʔaːn/, /taqˈriːban/.

Source: Blanc 1964 §3.26 pp. 26–28; Jasim 2020 §2.4.

### kaf affrication

Write /tʃ/ only in words whose standard Baghdadi form has it, including lexicalised cases with no surface front vowel: /tʃaːn/ 'he was', /tʃibiːr/ 'big', /tʃalib/ 'dog'. Elsewhere write /k/. European loans always keep /k/: /ˈkiːlu/. The second-person feminine suffix is /-itʃ/: /ˈbeːtitʃ/ 'your (f.) house'.

Source: Blanc 1964 §3.25 p. 25; Jasim 2020 §2.9.2.

### ض and ظ

Both are written /ðˤ/, because they merge in Baghdadi: /ðˤuhur/ 'noon', /ʔarðˤ/ 'ground'. Interdentals /θ ð/ are kept.

Source: Blanc 1964 §3.2.

### vowel inventory

The short vowels are /a i u/ and the long vowels /aː iː uː eː oː/. Blanc's short e and o are folded into /i u/: /tʃibiːr/, not /tʃebiːr/. Next to an emphatic, write /a aː/ as [ɑ ɑː]: /tˤɑːlib/, /sˤɑff/. Word-final long vowels are shortened except in monosyllables and before suffixes: /ˈʔaːni/, /maː/ only as a full lexical monosyllable. Geminates are written as doubled consonants; long vowels are written with ː.

Source: Blanc 1964 ch. 3; Jasim 2020 (tafxim of low vowels).

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
