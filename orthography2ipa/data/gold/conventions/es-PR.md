# Transcription conventions: Puerto Rican Spanish (es-PR)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark primary stress with ˈ before the onset of the stressed syllable in every stressed word, including stressed monosyllables. Unstressed function words (see function_words) get no mark. Place stress by the standard Spanish rule as the spelling shows it. A written accent marks the stressed syllable. Otherwise a word ending in a vowel, n or s is stressed on the penult, and any other word on the final syllable. Examples: casa [ˈkasa], comen [ˈkomeŋ], comer [koˈmel], árbol [ˈalβol], sol [ˈsol]. Lexical stress is decided on the spelled form before the coda rules apply, so verdad [belˈða] and usted [uhˈte] keep final stress after /d/ is dropped. Syllabify inside the word only. A single intervocalic consonant goes to the onset, and onset clusters are C+ɾ and C+l: otro [ˈotɾo], hablar [aˈβlal]. Adverbs in -mente keep two stresses: ˌ on the adjective's stressed syllable and ˈ on -men-, as in rápidamente [ˌʁapiðaˈmente]. Enclitic pronouns written on the verb do not move its stress: dímelo [ˈdimelo]. Spanish has no phonemic long vowels and no case endings, so those do not apply. There are no lexical geminates. A sequence of two identical consonants from different segments is written twice: comerlo [koˈmello].

Source: Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chapter on stress (ch. 14: stress rules, -mente adverbs, stressed vs. unstressed words).

## Stressed and unstressed words

Unstressed words carry no ˈ: definite articles (el, la, lo, los, las); prenominal possessives (mi, tu, su, nuestro, mis, tus, sus); object and reflexive clitics (me, te, se, lo, la, le, nos, los, las, les); simple prepositions (a, ante, bajo, con, contra, de, desde, en, entre, hacia, hasta, para, por, sin, sobre, tras); the conjunctions y, e, o, u, ni, pero, que, si, aunque, mas; unaccented relatives (que, quien, donde, cuando, como, cual); and forms of address before a name (don, doña, san, santo). Examples: [el ˈpeʁo], [de ˈpwelto ˈʁiko], [me lo ˈðio], [pol la ˈkaʝe], [paɾa ˈmi]. Stressed words carry ˈ: indefinite articles (un [ˈuŋ], una [ˈuna]); demonstratives (este [ˈehte]); tonic and prepositional pronouns (yo [ˈʝo], tú [ˈtu], él [ˈel], ella [ˈeʝa], usted [uhˈte], mí [ˈmi], ti [ˈti]); postnominal possessives (mío [ˈmio]); and interrogatives and exclamatives with an accent (qué [ˈke], cómo [ˈkomo], dónde [ˈdonde]). The following are also stressed: all verb forms including auxiliaries and copulas (ha [ˈa], he [ˈe], es [ˈeh], está [ehˈta]), the negation no [ˈno], numerals, sí [ˈsi], más [ˈmah] and muy [ˈmui̯]. Unstressed words are never reduced to colloquial short forms: para is [paɾa], not [pa].

Source: Hualde (2005), ch. 14, list of stressed and unstressed word classes (following the Navarro Tomás / Quilis classification).

## Pausal forms

Does not apply. Spanish has no pausal forms, no case endings and no nunation. A word is written the same way at a comma, at the end of a sentence and inside a phrase, and the coda rules (see other) apply in all positions. A pause matters only to connected_speech: /b d g/ after a pause are stops.

Source: Hualde (2005); no Spanish description posits pausal forms.

## Connected speech

Each word stays in its own slot. Synalepha, resyllabification and linking are not written. Vowels meeting across words stay full vowels, with no glides or deletion: mi amigo [mi aˈmiɣo], la otra [la ˈotɾa]. Word-final consonants are not moved into the next word. Word-final /s n ɾ/ keep their coda forms [h ŋ l] even before a vowel-initial word: los amigos [loh aˈmiɣoh], en agua [eŋ ˈaɣwa], por eso [pol ˈeso]. The one cross-word process written is the stop/approximant choice for /b d g/ at the start of a word, decided by the end of the preceding word in the same sentence. /b d g/ are stops [b d ɡ] after a pause (sentence start or any punctuation) and after a nasal, and /d/ is also a stop after [l]: [uŋ ˈbeso], [el ˈdeðo], [ˈbamoh]. Elsewhere they are approximants [β ð ɣ]: after a vowel, glide, [h], [l] (for /b g/) or [ɾ]: [la ˈβoka], [loh ˈðeðoh], [de ˈɣato]. Inside words the same rule holds: [ˈkampo], [ˈteŋɡo], [ˈkalβo], [ˈaldea]. No other assimilation is written across word boundaries. Word-final /n/ is [ŋ] before any consonant: [uŋ ˈpeʁo], [koŋ ˈβino]. There is no voicing of [h] before voiced consonants.

Source: Hualde (2005), chapters on the voiced obstruents (spirantization of /b d g/), on nasals (Caribbean velarization of word-final /n/ in all positions), and on resyllabification and synalepha.

## Glides and diphthongs

Write a rising (pre-nuclear) glide as [j] or [w]: tierra [ˈtjeʁa], bueno [ˈbweno], cuatro [ˈkwatɾo], Juan [ˈhwaŋ]. Write a falling (post-nuclear) glide as [i̯] or [u̯]: aire [ˈai̯ɾe], hoy [ˈoi̯], muy [ˈmui̯], causa [ˈkau̯sa], reina [ˈrei̯na], which with the PR trill is [ˈʁei̯na]. Spelled hie- and hue- at the start of a word are [ʝe] and [we]: hielo [ˈʝelo], huevo [ˈweβo]. An accented i or u is a full vowel in hiatus: día [ˈdia], país [paˈih], baúl [baˈul]. Sequences of non-high vowels (ea, eo, oa, ae, ao) are written as two full vowels, never glided: teatro [teˈatɾo], poeta [poˈeta]. The conjunction y is [j] directly before a vowel-initial word and [i] elsewhere: [j eŋˈtonseh], [ˈpaŋ i ˈβino]. Consonantal y and ll are the fricative [ʝ], not a glide (see other).

Source: Hualde (2005), chapter on vowel sequences, diphthongs and glides ([j w] vs. [i̯ u̯]; hiatus vs. diphthong).

## Other choices

### coda /ɾ/ (lateralization)

Published descriptions treat PR lateralization as frequent but variable. Following the spec note, write it categorically. Every coda /ɾ/, word-internal or word-final, is [l], also before a vowel-initial word: comer [koˈmel], puerta [ˈpwelta], carne [ˈkalne], verdad [belˈða], por eso [pol ˈeso]. An onset /ɾ/, single or in a cluster, stays a tap: pero [ˈpeɾo], tres [ˈtɾeh], hablar [aˈβlal]. Coda /l/ stays [l]. PR has no rhotacism and no gemination of coda liquids before consonants of the Cuban type ([ˈkatta]).

Source: Spec note; Hualde (2005), section on Caribbean liquid neutralization; Fontanella de Weinberg (1992), chapter on Caribbean Spanish (Puerto Rico).

### /r/ (trill)

Descriptions report apical [r] beside velar/uvular variants. Following the spec note, write the PR 'erre velarizada' as [ʁ] in every trill position, with no [χ] or [x] variants: word-initially, after n, l or s, and for intervocalic rr. Examples: rosa [ˈʁosa], perro [ˈpeʁo], honra [ˈonʁa], alrededor [alʁeðeˈðol], Puerto Rico [ˈpwelto ˈʁiko].

Source: Spec note; Hualde (2005), section on rhotic variation (velar/uvular trill of Puerto Rico); Fontanella de Weinberg (1992).

### coda /s/

Every coda /s/ is [h], word-internal and word-final, before consonants, vowels and pause. It is never deleted and never voiced: mismo [ˈmihmo], los ojos [loh ˈohoh], más [ˈmah]. Spelled coda x is [kh]: examen [ekˈhameŋ]. Onset x is [ks]: taxi [ˈtaksi]. Seseo applies throughout: z, ce and ci are [s], as in cielo [ˈsjelo] and luz [ˈluh].

Source: Hualde (2005), section on aspiration and deletion of /s/ in Caribbean dialects; Fontanella de Weinberg (1992); Penny (2002) on seseo.

### coda /n/

Word-final /n/ is [ŋ] in all positions: pan [ˈpaŋ], son [ˈsoŋ], en el [eŋ el]. Word-internal coda /n/ assimilates in place: [m] before bilabials, [ŋ] before velars, [n] elsewhere. Examples: campo [ˈkampo], tengo [ˈteŋɡo], canto [ˈkanto]. Vowel nasalization is not written.

Source: Hualde (2005), section on velarization of /n/ in Caribbean Spanish.

### /x/

Spelled j, and g before e or i, are the glottal [h]: jamón [haˈmoŋ], gente [ˈhente], México [ˈmehiko]. Spelled h is silent.

Source: Spec note; Hualde (2005), section on /x/ in Caribbean dialects.

### /d/ intervocalic and word-final

Intervocalic /d/ is kept as [ð], including in -ado and -ido: cansado [kanˈsaðo]. The colloquial [kanˈsao] is not used. Word-final /d/ is deleted: verdad [belˈða], usted [uhˈte], Madrid [maˈðɾi].

Source: Hualde (2005), section on /d/ weakening (word-final deletion general in Latin America; intervocalic deletion stylistically variable).

### yeísmo

Spelled ll and consonantal y are both [ʝ] in every position. Do not write [ɟʝ], [dʒ] or [ʎ]: calle [ˈkaʝe], yo [ˈʝo], llamar [ʝaˈmal], conyuge [ˈkoŋʝuhe]. Spelled ch is [tʃ]: mucho [ˈmutʃo].

Source: Hualde (2005), section on yeísmo; Penny (2002) on yeísmo in American Spanish.

### English loanwords

Unassimilated English words are transcribed with PR Spanish phonemes and the coda rules, with stress on the syllable stressed in English: parking [ˈpalkiŋ], show [ˈtʃou̯], email [iˈmei̯l].

Source: Spec note on English contact; Fontanella de Weinberg (1992), Puerto Rico section on anglicisms.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
