# Transcription conventions: Syrian Arabic (Damascene) (apc-SY)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark primary stress with ˈ before the stressed syllable on every content word, monosyllables included (ˈbeːt 'house', ˈweːn 'where'). Count from the word end and take the first rule that fits. (1) A final superheavy syllable takes the stress: CVːC or CVCC (kiˈtaːb 'book', ʔaˈkalt 'I ate'). (2) A final long vowel takes the stress only when it is the long vowel of a suffix such as the object form of -u (ʃaːˈfuː 'they saw him' against ˈʃaːfu 'they saw'). (3) Otherwise the penult takes the stress if it is heavy, that is CVː or CVC (ˈkaːnu 'they were', madˈrasti 'my school'). (4) Otherwise the antepenult takes the stress (ˈmadrase 'school', ˈʃaʒara 'tree'). Stress never falls further back than the antepenult; a two-syllable word with a light penult takes initial stress (ˈkatab). A geminate closes the syllable before it, which makes that syllable heavy (ˈmʕallem 'teacher', ʔadˈdeːʃ 'how much'). The suffix vowel -e / -a never takes stress. Case endings do not apply: Damascene has no case inflection. Write no secondary stress.

Source: Cowell 1964, A Reference Grammar of Syrian Arabic, phonology chapter on stress (Damascene antepenult rule, not the Cairene penult rule). Cowell's text was not consulted directly; the rule is given here as the standard Levantine description.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Write these without a stress mark, in every position: the article il- (inside its host word); the prepositions b-, l-/la, ʕa/ʕala, min, fi, maʕ, ʕand when it means 'at'; the conjunctions w-, ʔaw, bas 'but', ʔinnu 'that', ʔiza 'if', la 'so that'; the verbal particles b- (indicative), ʕam (progressive), raħ/laħ (future); the negator ma; the vocative ja; and the object/possessive suffixes, which are part of the host word. Mark stress on everything else: independent pronouns (ˈʔana, ˈʔinti, ˈhuwwe), demonstratives (ˈhaːda, ˈhaːji), interrogatives (ˈʃuː, ˈmiːn, ˈweːn, ˈleːʃ), the negators ˈmuː and ˈmaː when they stand alone as an answer, and kaːn in all uses, auxiliary included (ˈkaːn ʕam jiʔra). Example: ma ˈbaʕref 'I don't know', raħ ˈruːħ ʕa ˈbeːt? (preposition and particles unstressed).

Source: Cowell 1964 (proclitic particles and pronouns, as reported in standard Levantine descriptions); clitic status of b-, ʕam and raħ as in Levantine grammars generally.

## Pausal forms

Does not apply: Damascene has no case endings, no nunation and no pausal/contextual alternation. Write every word in the same form at the end of a sentence, at a comma and in mid-sentence. The feminine ending is -e/-a everywhere and never -t at pause (ˈmadrase). A lexicalised tanwīn adverb written in diacritized input keeps its -an in all positions as a fixed lexical form (ˈʔabadan 'never', ˈʃukran 'thanks'). Final short vowels are never deleted at pause.

Source: Cowell 1964 (absence of case; -e/-a feminine); Almbark & Hellmuth 2015 §1 (dialect vowel system without MSA case vowels).

## Connected speech

Write each word in its citation form; apply no assimilation, elision, liaison or epenthesis across a space. The word that carries the article keeps il- in every position, also after a vowel-final word (ˈfi ilˈbeːt is written fi ilˈbeːt, not fi lˈbeːt). The article has no glottal stop. Inside the word, il- assimilates fully to a following coronal (t d s z ʃ ʒ r l n tˤ dˤ sˤ zˤ): iʃˈʃams 'the sun', irˈraʒʒaːl. It stays il before other consonants: ilˈʔamar 'the moon' (qamar with qaf → ʔ), ilˈbeːt. Write no helping vowel between clusters that meet across words. The construct state is lexical, not a boundary process: a feminine noun followed by its genitive takes -it, written on the first word (madraset → ˈmadrasit ilˈbeːt is not used; write madˈrasit ilˈbeːt).

Source: Cowell 1964 (article assimilation to coronals, construct -it); common Levantine practice of word-by-word broad transcription.

## Glides and diphthongs

Write glides as consonants j and w, never i̯ or u̯. Write the old diphthongs /aj aw/ as monophthongs eː oː when the glide closes the syllable (ˈbeːt, ˈjoːm, ˈweːn, ˈloːn). Keep aj, aw when the glide is geminate or carries its own vowel (ˈʔawwal, ħajaˈwaːn, jaˈwadd). Write the nisba feminine as -ijje with a geminate glide (ʕaraˈbijje). Write the glide before a vowel as j/w after i or u, not as a long vowel plus vowel (ʔixtiˈjaːr, ˈħaːjit). Word-final -aːj stays aːj (ˈʃaːj 'tea').

Source: Almbark & Hellmuth 2015 §1.1 (eː oː from /aj aw/); Fadda 2016 p.30 (no monophthongization before a glide); spec note on the وَيْ/يَوْ keys.

## Other choices

### qaf and interdentals

Write native qaf as ʔ (ˈʔaːl 'he said'). Write q only in learned MSA words (ilˈqurʔaːn). Write native θ ð ðˤ as t d dˤ (ˈtneːn 'two', ˈdahab 'gold', ˈdˤahr 'back'). In learned loans write s z zˤ (ˈsaːnawi, ˈzikr, zˤaːˈhira). Write ج as ʒ (ˈʒamal). Write گ as ɡ in loans only (ˈtfinge).

Source: Cowell 1964 (urban Damascene reflexes and learned s/z); spec notes; WikiPron apc gold for گ.

### short vowels and feminine ending

Write the short vowels as i a u only (ˈbeːtu 'his house', ˈkitbit is not used: write ˈkatbit). Write no e, o or ə except in the feminine ending. That ending is -e after most consonants (ˈmadrase) and -a after emphatics, gutturals (ħ ʕ x ɣ ʔ h) and r (ˈsˤaħħa, ˈʃaʒara). Long vowels are iː eː aː oː uː.

Source: Almbark & Hellmuth 2015 §3-4 (three short phonemes; e o ə allophonic); Cowell 1964 for the -e/-a split of the feminine ending.

### final unstressed long vowels

Write a word-final vowel short when it is unstressed (ˈkaːnu, ˈʔinti, ˈʔabi). Write it long only when it is stressed (ʃaːˈfuː). This departs from spec outputs such as ˈʔabwiː.

Source: Cowell 1964 (final vowels are short unless stressed).

### emphasis backing

Write /a aː/ as ɑ ɑː when they stand immediately next to tˤ dˤ sˤ zˤ in the same word (ˈsˤɑːr, ˈtˤɑːwle). Write a aː elsewhere, including next to ʔ, q and r.

Source: Spec notes (inherited Levantine allophone rule); Watson 2002 on emphasis spreading.

### gemination

Write a geminate as a doubled consonant (ˈmʕallem, ʔadˈdeːʃ, ˈħammaːm).

Source: Cowell 1964.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
