# Transcription conventions: Moroccan Arabic (Darija) (ary-MA)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Do not mark stress anywhere: no ˈ or ˌ in any word. Moroccan Arabic word stress is not contrastive and its placement is unstable. The prominence speakers hear is largely phrase-level, often on the final or heavy syllable of the phrase. Full vowels, geminates and the epenthetic ə therefore receive no stress marking. Moroccan has no case endings, so they never bear on stress. Examples: ktəb 'he wrote', kaːjəktəb 'he is writing', ʕaɡɡuːn 'stutterer', ʔaɡaːdiːr 'Agadir'. This matches the specification's own gold rows, which carry no stress marks.

Source: Harrell 1962, A Short Reference Grammar of Moroccan Arabic, phonology chapter (stress described as non-distinctive). Benkirane 1998, 'Intonation in Western Arabic (Morocco)', in Hirst & Di Cristo (eds.), Intonation Systems (phrase-level prominence). Boudlal 2001, Constraint Interaction in the Phonology and Morphology of Casablanca Moroccan Arabic, PhD thesis, Rabat (argues against lexical word stress). Some descriptions do posit a word-stress rule (e.g. stress on the last heavy syllable); this convention follows the no-lexical-stress tradition.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Because stress is never marked, function words differ from content words only in taking their reduced, cliticised forms. Write each orthographic word in one piece. Proclitics written attached in Arabic script stay inside their host word with no vowel. Examples: article l- (lkəlb 'the dog'); prepositions f- 'in', b- 'with', l- 'to' (fəddaːr 'in the house', bəlʕarbijja); conjunction w- 'and' (wəlwəld); genitive d- (dəlməʁrib); aspect prefixes kaː-/ta- and future ɣa-/ɣaːdi (kaːnəmʃi, ɣaːdi nəmʃi). Negation ma- … -ʃ is written in the one word as ma…ʃ, e.g. maːkaːjənʃ 'there isn't' if spelled as one word. If ما is spelled separately, write ma as its own word and the host with -ʃ. Enclitic pronouns are part of their host: ktaːbu 'his book', ʃaftha 'she saw her', ʕandək 'you have'. Free-standing short words take these forms: و → w (u before a consonant cluster in careful speech is not written), من → mən, على → ʕla, في → fi, ديال → djaːl, هاد → haːd, اللي → lli, باش → baːʃ. Independent pronouns take full forms: ʔana, ntaːja/nta, ntiːja/nti, huwwa, hijja, ħna, ntuːma, huːma.

Source: Harrell 1962, chapters on prepositions, pronouns and verbal particles. Ennaji et al. 2004, A Grammar of Moroccan Arabic, pp. 2-7 and the morphology chapters (clitics, article assimilation). Heath 2020 (in Lucas & Manfredi), p. 216, for the reduced vowel system that leaves proclitics vowelless.

## Pausal forms

Does not apply: Moroccan Arabic has no case endings, no nunation (tanwīn) and no pausal/context alternation, so a word is written the same at the end of a sentence, at a comma or inside a phrase. Classical-style endings are never added, even when the spelling shows harakāt or tanwīn: a final ‹ـًا› in a borrowed formula is read as the Moroccan pronunciation, e.g. ʃukran.

Source: Harrell 1962 (no case or nunation in the dialect). Watson 2002, ch. 2, which contrasts dialects with Classical pausal phonology. Ennaji et al. 2004, pp. 2-7.

## Connected speech

Write every word in its isolation (citation) form. Do not transcribe any process across a word boundary: no assimilation, no elision of a final or initial vowel, no linking consonant and no resyllabification. Each IPA word corresponds to exactly one input word, e.g. ʃaf lwəld 'he saw the boy', not ʃafl wəld. Word-internal processes ARE written. (1) The article l- assimilates to a following coronal (t d s z ʃ ʒ n r l tˤ sˤ dˤ) as a geminate: ʃʃəms 'the sun', ddaːr 'the house', ʒʒməl 'the camels', nnaːs 'the people'. It stays l before other consonants: lqəmr 'the moon', lbaːb 'the door'. (2) Epenthetic ə is written inside the word where the published forms have it: kəlb, smən, ktəb, kaːjəktbu. (3) Voicing and place assimilation between word-internal consonants is NOT written; a broad transcription keeps the phonemes, e.g. ktəb, not ɡdəb.

Source: Ennaji et al. 2004, pp. 3-7 (clusters kəlb, smən, smra; schwa epenthetic between consonants; article assimilation). Harrell 1962 (definite article). Heath 2020, p. 216 (syncope in weak positions). The whole-word, one-to-one constraint is the engine's segmentation rule.

## Glides and diphthongs

Glides are always written with the consonant symbols j and w, never i̯ or u̯, and are geminated as jj or ww where long: bajjaʕ, kaːjən, ʃwija 'a little', wəld, huwwa, hijja. Old diphthongs are monophthongised in the koiné: *ay → iː, *aw → uː. So write biːt 'room', ziːt 'oil', juːm 'day', zuːʒ 'two', luːn 'colour', not bajt/jawm. Write a vowel+glide sequence (aj, aw, aːj, aːw) only where the Moroccan word itself has one, e.g. ʔataːj 'tea', kaːjən, ʃaːf → ʃuːf (imperative uː, not aw).

Source: Harrell 1962 (monophthongisation of *ay *aw; semivowels y, w as consonants). Ennaji et al. 2004, p. 2 consonant inventory (/j/, /w/ as consonants). Heath 1987, Ablaut and Ambiguity: Phonology of a Moroccan Arabic Dialect, for glide/vowel alternations.

## Other choices

### vowel inventory and length

Use full vowels a i u, the reduced/epenthetic ə, and e o only in French loans (e.g. telefon, radjo, bisklit). Write the full vowels that the spelling shows with a mater (ا و ي) as long aː iː uː, except word-finally, where they are written short: daːr 'house', kbiːr 'big', ʃuːf 'look!', ʔaɡaːdiːr, but friːɡu 'fridge', ʃra 'he bought', ʒa 'he came', ktaːbi 'my book'. Every other vowel is either ə or nothing, following the Moroccan clusters (smən, ktəb, kəlb). Write a rounded reduced vowel as u where the standard form has it (kull, xubz). This follows the specification's gold rows (aː iː uː, final short u). Noamane 2020 and Ennaji et al. 2004 describe the full vowels as phonologically non-long. The length mark here only distinguishes full vowels from ə; it is not a claim of contrastive length.

Source: Noamane 2020, p. 49 (/i u a/ plus epenthetic /ə/). Heath 2020, p. 216. Ennaji et al. 2004, pp. 2-7. Harrell 1962 (rounded short ŭ in kŭll, xŭbz). Length marking follows the specification's ary_arab_broad gold rows.

### qāf and /ɡ/

Read ‹ق› as q, the urban/sedentary koiné reflex: qaːl 'he said', qraː, lqəmr. Read ‹ڭ›, ‹ݣ›, ‹گ› and ‹ڨ› as ɡ: ɡaːl, ɡəlt, nɡuːl, ɡaːʕ, ʔaɡaːdiːr. Never convert a ‹ق› spelling to ɡ, even in words that Bedouin/rural speech pronounces with ɡ; the spelling decides.

Source: Watson 2002, p. 17 §2.1.9 (q sedentary, ɡ Bedouin). Ennaji et al. 2004, p. 2 and Noamane 2020, p. 45 (both /q/ and /ɡ/ phonemic). Kew 2003, L2/03-176 §3 (ݣ = /ɡ/). Saadane & Habash 2015, §4.1 p. 70 (ڨ = [ɡ]). The specification's gold rows (ڭ = [ɡ]).

### jīm and interdentals

Read ‹ج› (and ‹چ›) as ʒ, never dʒ: ʒbəl 'mountain', ʒaːʒ 'glass', ʒʒməl 'the camels'. Read ‹ث› as t, ‹ذ› as d, and ‹ظ›, ‹ض› as dˤ: tlaːta 'three', diːb 'wolf', dˤhər 'back', mrəːdˤ → mriːdˤ 'sick'. ‹چ› in /tʃ/ loans is also written ʒ, as the specification's note requires; the inventory has no tʃ.

Source: Watson 2002, p. 16 (jīm = /ž/) and p. 15 §2.1.4 (interdentals merged with stops). Ennaji et al. 2004, p. 2 (no /dʒ/, no interdentals). Noamane 2020, p. 45.

### emphatics and hamza

Write emphasis only on the etymological emphatic consonants: tˤ sˤ dˤ, plus rˤ, lˤ only where the lexeme is distinctively emphatic, e.g. rˤbəʕ is not written; default r. Do not mark emphasis spreading on vowels or neighbouring consonants: tˤajjəb, sˤbaːħ, bəːdˤ → biːdˤ. Medial and final hamza is lost (raːs 'head', biːr 'well', ma 'water' → ma). Write ʔ only before a word-initial vowel: ʔana, ʔaɡaːdiːr, ʔataːj.

Source: Harrell 1962 (emphasis; loss of hamza). Ennaji et al. 2004, p. 2 (emphatic series). The word-initial ʔ follows the specification's gold rows (ʔ a ɡ aː d iː r).

### feminine ending (tāʾ marbūṭa)

Write ‹ة› as -a in the free form and as -t before a pronoun suffix or in a construct with a following noun, when the spelling shows the construct form: mdiːna 'city', mdiːntna 'our city', məraːt 'wife of'.

Source: Harrell 1962 (feminine -a/-t). Ennaji et al. 2004, morphology chapter on gender.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
