# Transcription conventions: Uruguayan Portuguese (Riverense / DPU) (pt-UY)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark primary stress with ˈ before the stressed syllable of every content word, including monosyllables: [ˈpe], [ˈsɔl]. Write no secondary stress. Place ˈ before the whole syllable onset, clusters included: prato [ˈpɾatu]; dia [ˈdia]. Stress is lexical and falls on one of the last three syllables. A written accent (á é ê í ó ô ú â ã õ) marks the stressed vowel: café [kaˈfɛ], ônibus [ˈonibus], está [esˈta]. Without an accent: (a) a word ending in unstressed ⟨a e o⟩, optionally followed by ⟨s⟩, or in ⟨am em ens⟩ takes penultimate stress: casa [ˈkazɐ], livros [ˈlivɾus], falam [ˈfalɐ̃w̃], homem [ˈɔmẽj̃]. (b) Every other word takes final stress: words ending in ⟨l r z i u im um⟩, in a nasal vowel or in a diphthong: papel [paˈpɛl], mulher [muˈjɛ], aqui [aˈki], falou [faˈlo], irmão [iɾˈmɐ̃w̃]. A word keeps its stress position when an unstressed final vowel or ⟨r⟩ is not written, as in mulher [muˈjɛ]. Only stressed syllables have [ɛ ɔ]. Portuguese has no phonemic long vowels, no geminates and no case endings, so none are written. Orthographic ⟨rr ss⟩ are single consonants: carro [ˈkaro], passo [ˈpasu].

Source: Mattoso Câmara (1970) Estrutura da língua portuguesa, ch. on accent; Barbosa & Albano (2004) 'Brazilian Portuguese', JIPA 34(2), stress section. DPU is inherited from pt-BR per Carvalho (1998: 646, Figure 1).

## Stressed and unstressed words

These words are unstressed proclitics or enclitics and get no ˈ. Their vowels are reduced. Definite articles: o [u], a [a], os [us], as [as]. Indefinite articles: um [ũ], uma [umɐ], uns [ũs]. Prepositions: de [di], em [ẽj̃], com [kõ], sem [sẽj̃], por [poɾ], para [paɾɐ], pra [pɾa], a [a]. Contractions: do [du], da [dɐ], no [nu], na [nɐ], ao [aw], pelo [pelu]. Conjunctions: e [i], que [ki], se [si], mas [mas], ou [o], nem [nẽj̃], porque [poɾki]. Clitic pronouns: me [mi], te [ti], se [si], lhe [ji], o [u], a [a], nos [nus], lhes [jis]. In te [ti] and de [di] the stop stays dental. A hyphenated enclitic is its own unstressed word: dá-me [ˈda] [mi]. These words carry ˈ: subject and tonic pronouns (eu [ˈew], tu [ˈtu], ele [ˈeli], ela [ˈɛlɐ], nós [ˈnɔs], você [voˈse], mim [ˈmĩ], ti [ˈti]), possessives, demonstratives, não [ˈnɐ̃w̃], sim [ˈsĩ], adverbs, and every verb form, auxiliaries (ser, estar, ter, ir, haver) included: é [ˈɛ], tá [ˈta], vai [ˈvaj], tem [ˈtẽj̃]. A function word under contrastive focus still gets no ˈ, so that every row is the same.

Source: Mattoso Câmara (1970) on atonic forms (vocábulos átonos); Bisol (2005) 'O clítico e seu hospedeiro', in Bisol (ed.) Introdução a estudos de fonologia do português brasileiro. The dental [t d] in te and de follows Carvalho (1998: 646, 648).

## Pausal forms

Does not apply: Portuguese has no pausal forms, case endings or nunation. A word at a comma or at the end of a sentence is written exactly as it is elsewhere. The only effect is that final ⟨s⟩ stays [s] before a pause: casas. [ˈkazɐs].

Source: Mattoso Câmara (1970); Barbosa & Albano (2004). No pausal morphology is described for pt-BR or DPU (Carvalho 1998).

## Connected speech

Each word is written in its own place with its own form. Exactly one cross-word process is written: word-final /s/ (⟨s z x⟩) becomes [z] before a word beginning with a vowel or a voiced consonant, and stays [s] before a voiceless consonant or a pause. Examples: os amigos [uz aˈmigus]; duas mulheres [ˈduaz muˈjɛɾis]; mas tem [mas ˈtẽj̃]. The same rule applies inside a word: mesmo [ˈmezmu]. Nothing else is written. Vowels meeting across a boundary are not elided or merged: de água [di ˈaɡwɐ], que eu [ki ˈew]. Word-final ⟨r⟩ is not restored before a vowel: falar isso [faˈla ˈisu]. Final ⟨s⟩ is never palatalised to [ʃ] and never aspirated. Word-final [ti di] before a vowel stay dental, with no affrication and no glide formation: noite inteira [ˈnojti ĩˈtejɾɐ]. Resyllabification is not shown.

Source: Bisol (2005) and Barbosa & Albano (2004) for /s/ sandhi voicing in pt-BR. The alveolar unaspirated coda /s/ is inherited per the spec note, since Carvalho (1998) documents /s/-aspiration only for border Spanish. Dental stops follow Carvalho (1998: 646, 648), citing Rona (1965: 40) and Hensey (1972: 60).

## Glides and diphthongs

Write the non-syllabic part of a diphthong as [j] or [w], never as i̯ or u̯: pai [ˈpaj], mau [ˈmaw], leite [ˈlejti], céu [ˈsɛw], água [ˈaɡwɐ], quando [ˈkwɐ̃du]. Nasal diphthongs carry a tilde on both parts: não [ˈnɐ̃w̃], mãe [ˈmɐ̃j̃], bem [ˈbẽj̃], põe [ˈpõj̃]. Written vowel sequences that are hiatuses stay two vowels: dia [ˈdia], rua [ˈrua], país [paˈis]. Orthographic ⟨lh⟩ is written as the glide [j] in onset position: mulher [muˈjɛ], filho [ˈfiju], trabalho [tɾaˈbaju], colher [koˈjɛ]. This is the focused rural variant, which Rona describes as categorical. The urban-Brazilian [ʎ] is not used. ⟨ou⟩ is the monophthong [o]: falou [faˈlo], outro [ˈotɾu]. ⟨ei⟩ before ⟨r⟩, ⟨x⟩ or ⟨j⟩ is [e]: primeiro [pɾiˈmeɾu], deixa [ˈdeʃɐ]. Elsewhere ⟨ei⟩ is [ej]: lei [ˈlej].

Source: Carvalho (1998: 646-647), /mujɛ/ and coié, citing Rona (1965: 23) for categorical ⟨lh⟩ → [j]. Barbosa & Albano (2004) for the j/w notation and the ou/ei monophthongisation.

## Other choices

### Dental stops before [i]

/t d/ stay dental [t d] before [i], whether the [i] is underlying or a raised final ⟨e⟩. Never write [tʃ dʒ]: tia [ˈtia], dia [ˈdia], tarde [ˈtaɾdi], gente [ˈʒẽti], tira [ˈtiɾɐ]. The palatalised forms are the diffusing urban-Brazilian variant and are not used.

Source: Carvalho (1998: 646, 648-650), local [ti]/[tira] vs urban [tʃi]/[tʃira], citing Rona (1965: 40) and Hensey (1972: 60).

### Rhotics

Word-initial ⟨r⟩, ⟨rr⟩, and ⟨r⟩ after ⟨n l s⟩ are written as the alveolar trill [r]: rua [ˈrua], carro [ˈkaro], honra [ˈõrɐ]. Intervocalic ⟨r⟩, ⟨r⟩ in clusters and word-internal coda ⟨r⟩ are written as the tap [ɾ]: caro [ˈkaɾu], prato [ˈpɾatu], porta [ˈpɔɾtɐ]. Word-final ⟨r⟩ is not written, in verbs and nouns alike: falar [faˈla], mulher [muˈjɛ], colher [koˈjɛ]. This departs from the spec note's coda tap for word-final position only, following Carvalho's transcriptions.

Source: Trill: Wikipedia 'Uruguayan Portuguese' (secondary, as in the spec note). Final-r deletion: Carvalho (1998: 646-647), /mujɛ/ and coié.

### Coda /l/

Coda ⟨l⟩ is the lateral [l], not vocalised to [w]: Brasil [bɾaˈzil], mal [ˈmal], alto [ˈaltu]. This is the secondary-sourced feature noted in the spec.

Source: Wikipedia 'Uruguayan Portuguese' (secondary), consistent with the Spanish adstrate (Elizaincín 1992).

### Unstressed vowels

Word-final unstressed ⟨e⟩ (+s) is [i], ⟨o⟩ (+s) is [u], and ⟨a⟩ (+s) is [ɐ]: leite [ˈlejti], livros [ˈlivɾus], casa [ˈkazɐ]. Pretonic and non-final post-tonic mid vowels are written as close-mid [e o], with no raising and no [ɛ ɔ]: menino [meˈninu], bonito [boˈnitu]. The unstressed monosyllables listed under function words are the only other exception. A vowel before an onset nasal consonant is written oral: cama [ˈkamɐ]. A vowel before a coda ⟨m n⟩ or under a tilde is written nasal, without the nasal consonant: campo [ˈkɐ̃pu], tempo [ˈtẽpu], lã [ˈlɐ̃].

Source: Barbosa & Albano (2004); Mattoso Câmara (1970). Inherited from pt-BR per the spec note, since DPU pretonic vocalism is not quantified in Carvalho (1998).

### Palatal nasal and sibilants

⟨nh⟩ is [ɲ]: minha [ˈmiɲɐ]. ⟨ch⟩ and ⟨x⟩ (as /ʃ/) are [ʃ]; ⟨j⟩, and ⟨g⟩ before ⟨e i⟩, are [ʒ]: chá [ˈʃa], gente [ˈʒẽti]. Coda ⟨s⟩ is alveolar [s]/[z], never [ʃ]. Intervocalic ⟨s⟩ is [z]: casa [ˈkazɐ].

Source: Barbosa & Albano (2004), inherited from pt-BR per the spec note.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
