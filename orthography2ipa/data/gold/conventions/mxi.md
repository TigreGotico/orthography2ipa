# Transcription conventions: Mozarabic (Andalusi Romance) (mxi)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Primary stress falls on the syllable that carried the Latin stress, as in the rest of Ibero-Romance. The romanization carries no accents, so apply this rule. (1) A word ending in a vowel, or in a vowel plus -s or -n, is stressed on the penult: bono [ˈbono], bokella [boˈkeʎa], sabes [ˈsabes], welyos [ˈweʎos]. (2) A word ending in any other consonant is stressed on the last syllable. This covers infinitives, -el/-ar forms and apocopated forms, whose apocope removed a post-tonic vowel: matar [maˈtar]. (3) A word of three or more syllables is stressed on the antepenult only where the edition marks it with an accent. Always follow an accent printed by the editor. (4) Andalusi Arabic loans follow Corriente's Andalusi Arabic stress: stress the last syllable that has an etymologically long vowel, otherwise the penult, so habib [ħaˈbib]. Write ˈ before the stressed syllable of every word of two or more syllables. Place it before the whole onset, treating stop or f plus r/l as an onset. Never mark monosyllables (fač [fatʃ], kuand [kwand]) or unstressed function words. Long vowels: there is no phonemic length in the Romance layer, and Andalusi Arabic loans lost length in favour of stress, so length is never written: [ħaˈbib], not [ħaˈbiːb]. Geminates: Romance doubled letters are not length. ⟨rr⟩ is [r] and ⟨ll⟩ is [ʎ] (see other). A geminate in an Arabic loan, where the romanization doubles it, is written as a doubled consonant. Case endings: none. Romance lost case, and the Arabic loans of the kharjas appear without iʿrāb, so no case vowel is ever added.

Source: Latin-stress continuity as in Penny 2002 ch. 2. Default rules follow the stress reconstruction of García Gómez/Galmés kharja editions, as discussed by Barbato & Minervini 2024 pp. 157-163, who stress that it is editorial. Arabic-loan stress follows Corriente 1997 (introduction on Andalusi Arabic phonology: stress replacing length).

## Stressed and unstressed words

These are unstressed in connected speech and never take ˈ, even when they have two syllables: definite articles (el, lo, la, los, las, and Arabic al); prepositions (de, a, en/in, kon, por/pora, sin, fasta); conjunctions and complementizers (e/i, ke, si, mas, ma, komo, kuando/kuand when they are conjunctions); unstressed object and reflexive clitics (me, te, se, le, lo, la, nos, vos); proclitic possessives (mio/mi, tu, su before a noun); and forms of aver/ser used as auxiliaries. Examples: la [la], de [de], komo [komo], pora [poɾa], al [al]. These carry stress: tonic and prepositional pronouns (yo, tu, mib, tib); possessives after a noun or standing alone; interrogative ke/komo/kuando, which are marked only if polysyllabic, as [ˈkomo]; negation non/no; and full lexical verbs, including aver/ser used as main verbs. A clitic split off by a hyphen or apostrophe is transcribed as its own unstressed word, for example al-habib → [al ħaˈbib].

Source: Proclitic/enclitic classes follow the general Ibero-Romance pattern described in Penny 2002 §3.5 (pronouns, articles), which Galmés de Fuentes 1983 assumes for Mozarabic. Arabic al- is unstressed as in Corriente 1997.

## Pausal forms

Does not apply. There are no pausal forms: nothing changes at the end of a sentence or at a comma. Final vowels are written exactly as the romanization gives them, and apocope is written only where the edition shows it (kuand [kwand], but bono [ˈbono] at a pause too). There are no case endings, and Arabic loans appear without tanwīn: habib [ħaˈbib], never [ħabibun].

Source: Romance lacks pausal morphology. The kharja Arabic loans are cited without iʿrāb in García Gómez/Corriente readings (Corriente 1997; Barbato & Minervini 2024 pp. 160-163).

## Connected speech

Write every word in its citation form. Across word boundaries, write no synalepha or vowel elision, no nasal or voicing assimilation (en bono → [en ˈbono], not [em]), no linking and no resyllabification. The records are consonantal scripts and give no evidence for sandhi. The only exception is elision the edition itself shows with an apostrophe, such as d' or k'. The split piece is its own word and is transcribed as written without its vowel: d' → [d], k' → [k]. Arabic al- before a coronal is assimilated only where the romanization writes it, so ar-raqib → [ar raˈqib], while al-habib → [al ħaˈbib].

Source: Barbato & Minervini 2024 pp. 157-163 on the fragmentary, consonant-script nature of the evidence. Galmés de Fuentes 1983 describes word forms, not sandhi.

## Glides and diphthongs

Write non-syllabic glides as [j] and [w], never as i̯ or u̯. ⟨w⟩ is [w], and unstressed ⟨u⟩ next to a vowel is [w]: welyos [ˈweʎos], kuand [kwand]. Unstressed ⟨i⟩ next to a vowel is [j]. ⟨y⟩ not followed by a vowel is [j], as in rey [rej] and falling diphthongs [aj ej oj aw ew]. ⟨y⟩ followed by a vowel is the palatal consonant [ʝ] (see other). ⟨ly⟩ is [ʎ] and ⟨ny⟩ is [ɲ], not l+j or n+j. Diphthongs are written only where the romanization shows them. A non-diphthongized Ĕ/Ŏ stays a monophthong (bene [ˈbene], bono [ˈbono]). A form that the edition gives with ie/ue, such as Toledo-type forms, is [je]/[we].

Source: Barbato & Minervini 2024 pp. 157-160 and Galmés de Fuentes 1983 pp. 57-61 (absence of tonic diphthongization; metaphonic welyos).

## Other choices

### vowel system

Write a five-vowel system [a e i o u] and never [ɛ ɔ]. The consonantal scripts cannot show a mid-vowel height contrast, and the romanization does not mark one. This picks the 5-vowel option of the note's '5/7-vowel system'. Examples: bene [ˈbene], filyo [ˈfiʎo].

Source: Corriente 1997 (Romance loans read with five vowels); Barbato & Minervini 2024 pp. 157-160 on the 5/7 uncertainty.

### ⟨y⟩ before a vowel (< ge,i, j)

Always [ʝ], never [ɟ], word-initially and between vowels alike: yana [ˈʝana], yermanellas [ʝeɾmaˈneʎas]. The note allows [ʝ]/[ɟ]; this picks one.

Source: Galmés de Fuentes 1983 (palatal reflexes of G-, J-); Barbato & Minervini 2024 pp. 160-163.

### ⟨č⟩ (< ce,i, ty, -cl-)

[tʃ], not [ts] or [θ]: fač [fatʃ].

Source: Galmés de Fuentes 1983 (Mozarabic palatal affricate from ce,i); Penny 2002 on Mozarabic č.

### ⟨ll⟩

[ʎ], the same as ⟨ly⟩, for Latin -LL-: bokella [boˈkeʎa], yermanellas [ʝeɾmaˈneʎas]. Never a geminate [lː] and never [l]. Corriente reads the diminutive -ell- with plain [l]. That reading is not followed.

Source: Galmés de Fuentes 1983 pp. 81-82 (lateral palatalization).

### rhotics

⟨r⟩ between vowels and in codas is [ɾ]. ⟨rr⟩ and word-initial ⟨r⟩ are [r]: farina [faˈɾina], matar [maˈtaɾ].

Source: General Ibero-Romance pattern (Penny 2002 ch. 2), assumed by Galmés de Fuentes 1983.

### stops and fricatives

Write voiceless intervocalic stops as voiceless, exactly as romanized: matar [maˈtaɾ], bokella [boˈkeʎa]. Do not voice them. Write ⟨b d g⟩ as [b d g] in every position, with no [β ð ɣ] spirantization, because there is no evidence for it. ⟨f⟩ is [f] word-initially (filyo [ˈfiʎo]). ⟨s⟩ is [s] and ⟨š⟩ is [ʃ], each exactly as the edition romanizes it; Romance s is not re-converted to [ʃ] even where Arabic script used šīn.

Source: Galmés de Fuentes 1983 pp. 80, 92-97; Barbato & Minervini 2024 pp. 160-163.

### Arabic-loan consonants

⟨ḥ⟩ is [ħ]. Plain ⟨h⟩ in an Arabic loan (habib) is also [ħ], since it stands for ḥāʾ there; this departs from the note's examples, which write plain h. ⟨ḫ⟩ is [x], ⟨ʿ⟩ is [ʕ], ⟨ʾ⟩ is [ʔ], ⟨q⟩ is [q], ⟨ṭ ḍ ṣ⟩ are [tˤ dˤ sˤ], and ⟨ġ⟩ is [ɣ]. Examples: habib [ħaˈbib], al-habib [al ħaˈbib].

Source: Corriente 1997 (Andalusi Arabic consonant inventory and kharja readings); Vicente 2020 §2.1.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
