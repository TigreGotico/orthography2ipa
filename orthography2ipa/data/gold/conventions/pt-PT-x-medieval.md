# Transcription conventions: Old Portuguese (pt-PT-x-medieval)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Stress is lexical and inherited from Latin. Mark primary stress with ˈ before the stressed syllable, including its onset consonants, on every word that carries stress, monosyllables included. Leave unstressed function words unmarked (see function_words). Do not mark secondary stress. Placement: if the word has a written acute or circumflex, stress that vowel. Otherwise, stress the penult in words ending in a vowel, in -s, or in verbal -am/-em, and stress the final syllable in words ending in another consonant (l, r, z), in a nasal vowel or nasal diphthong (-ã, -om, -ão, -im, -um), or in a stressed diphthong. Old spelling marks accents unreliably, so where the rule and the etymology conflict, follow the etymological (modern-cognate) stress. Examples: ⟨cavaleiro⟩ [kavaˈlejɾu], ⟨senhor⟩ [seˈɲoɾ], ⟨coraçom⟩ [koɾaˈsɐ̃w̃], ⟨mãos⟩ [ˈmɐ̃w̃s], ⟨fezerom⟩ [feˈzeɾɐ̃w̃], ⟨rei⟩ [ˈrej]. There is no phonemic vowel length. Spellings with double vowels from lost intervocalic l/n (⟨maa, teer, door, poboo, vee⟩) are written as one contracted vowel by this period: [ˈma], [ˈteɾ], [ˈdoɾ], [ˈpɔbu], [ˈve]. True hiatus is kept as two syllables: ⟨boa⟩ [ˈboa], ⟨dia⟩ [ˈdia]. There are no geminates. ⟨ss⟩ is single voiceless [s] (against intervocalic ⟨s⟩ [z]), ⟨rr⟩ is the trill [r], and ⟨ll mm nn ff pp tt⟩ are single consonants. The lect has no case endings, so that question does not apply.

Source: Williams (1962) From Latin to Portuguese, stress and vowel-contraction sections; Teyssier (1980) Histoire de la langue portugaise, ch. on Middle Portuguese (crasis of hiatus vowels completed by the 15th c.); Mateus & d'Andrade (2000) ch. 6 on stress placement.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Unstressed (no ˈ): monosyllabic articles, prepositions, conjunctions, object and reflexive clitics, and the relative/complementizer que. These are: o a os as [u a us as]; um ũa [ũ ˈũa], where the indefinite counts as stressed when it is a numeral but is unstressed as an article, so write [ũ] [ua]; de da do [de da du]; a [a]; em [ẽj̃]; per por [peɾ poɾ]; com sem [kõ sẽj̃]; e [e]; ou [ow]; nem [nẽj̃]; que [ke]; se [se]; and the clitics me te se lhe lhes nos vos o a os as lho [me te se ʎe ʎes nos vos u a us as ʎu]. Stressed (ˈ): all nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs; tonic pronouns eu tu el/ele ela nós vós mim ti si [ˈew ˈtu ˈel ˈɛla ˈnɔs ˈvɔs ˈmĩ ˈti ˈsi]; demonstratives and possessives (meu [ˈmew], este [ˈeste]); the negator nom/não [ˈnɐ̃w̃]; and polysyllabic prepositions and conjunctions, on their lexical stress (pera [ˈpeɾa], contra [ˈkõtɾa], sobre [ˈsobɾe], porque [poɾˈke]). Auxiliaries ser, seer, aver/haver, estar, teer are stressed like full verbs: ⟨ha⟩ [ˈa], ⟨é⟩ [ˈɛ]. In mesoclitic and enclitic forms split by hyphens, the clitic is unstressed and the verbal pieces keep stress: ⟨dar-lho-ei⟩ [ˈdaɾ ʎu ˈej], ⟨disse-lhe⟩ [ˈdise ʎe].

Source: Williams (1962) sections on pronouns and articles; Mattos e Silva (2006) O português arcaico: fonologia, morfologia e sintaxe, on clitics and atonic forms; Cunha & Cintra (1984) ch. on pronomes átonos for the modern continuation.

## Pausal forms

Does not apply: Old Portuguese has no pausal morphology, case endings or nunation. Every word is written in the same form at a sentence end, at a comma and sentence-internally. The only position-dependent effect is the voicing of final sibilants (see connected_speech), and before a pause a final ⟨s z⟩ is voiceless [s]: ⟨cousas.⟩ [ˈkowzas].

Source: Williams (1962); Teyssier (1980). Neither describes pausal alternants for this period.

## Connected speech

Write each word in its full citation form, in its own place. Do not write vowel elision, crasis or resyllabification across word boundaries, even where verse scansion implies synalepha (Gil Vicente, Cancioneiro Geral). Spellings that the source itself contracts are transcribed as written. An apostrophe splits words, so ⟨d'el-rei⟩ gives [d ˈel ˈrej]. One cross-word process is written: a word-final sibilant ⟨s z⟩ is [z] before a word beginning with a vowel or a voiced consonant, and [s] before a voiceless consonant or a pause. This changes only the final segment of the first word: ⟨os homens⟩ [uz ˈɔmẽj̃s], ⟨as donas⟩ [az ˈdonas], ⟨os paaços⟩ [us ˈpasus]. Coda ⟨s⟩ is not palatalised to [ʃ ʒ], because that change belongs to the 17th–18th c. No nasal or place assimilation is written across boundaries.

Source: Teyssier (1980), Middle Portuguese chapter (coda-s palatalisation dated later); Fernão de Oliveira (1536) Grammatica da lingoagem portuguesa, on final s; Williams (1962) on sandhi.

## Glides and diphthongs

Write non-syllabic high vowels as [j w], and in nasal diphthongs as [j̃ w̃]. Never write i̯ u̯. Oral diphthongs: [aj ej ɛj oj ɔj uj aw ew ɛw iw ow]. Examples: ⟨pai⟩ [ˈpaj], ⟨rei⟩ [ˈrej], ⟨papéis⟩ [paˈpɛjs], ⟨herói⟩ [eˈɾɔj], ⟨céu⟩ [ˈsɛw], ⟨mau⟩ [ˈmaw]. ⟨ou⟩ is kept as the diphthong [ow] (⟨cousa⟩ [ˈkowza], ⟨outro⟩ [ˈowtɾu]). This departs from the note's monophthongisation, which was only incipient and is completed later. Nasal diphthongs: final ⟨-ão, -am, -om⟩ (stressed or unstressed) → [ɐ̃w̃]; ⟨-ães⟩ → [ɐ̃j̃s]; ⟨-ões, -oes⟩ → [õj̃s]; final ⟨-em, -ẽ, -ens⟩ → [ẽj̃(s)]; ⟨mui⟩ → [ˈmũj̃]. Written hiatus (⟨uí⟩, ⟨aí⟩, ⟨ia⟩, ⟨oa⟩) is two syllables with no glide: ⟨saía⟩ [saˈia]. Following Base X, an accent on ⟨i u⟩ after a vowel marks hiatus, and an acute or circumflex in ⟨éi ói éu ôi êi⟩ marks open or closed diphthong quality.

Source: Teyssier (1980) on the convergence of -ão/-am/-om and on ou; Williams (1962) sections on diphthongs and nasal endings; Mateus & d'Andrade (2000) ch. 3 for the j/w glide notation; Acordo Ortográfico (1990) Bases VIII–X for the reading of accented digraphs.

## Other choices

### Unstressed vowel reduction

Unstressed final ⟨-o, -os⟩ → [u, us], and the unstressed article ⟨o, os⟩ → [u, us]. All other unstressed vowels keep full quality [i e a o u]: final ⟨-e⟩ is [e] and pretonic e/o are [e o] (⟨menino⟩ [meˈninu], ⟨grande⟩ [ˈgɾɐ̃de], ⟨fermoso⟩ [feɾˈmozu]). Unstressed ⟨a⟩ is [a]. This departs from the note's [ə]: centralisation of unstressed e to [ɨ] is dated to the 18th c., while final -o as [u] is old.

Source: Teyssier (1980), chronology of atonic vowels; Williams (1962) on final vowels.

### Sibilants

Following the note, write two sibilant pairs after deaffrication: ⟨c(e,i) ç ss s-initial, s+voiceless C⟩ → [s]; ⟨z, intervocalic s⟩ → [z]; ⟨x⟩ → [ʃ]; ⟨j, g(e,i)⟩ → [ʒ]. The apical/predorsal distinction (⟨paço⟩ vs ⟨passo⟩), which Oliveira (1536) still reports for some speakers, is not written. ⟨ch⟩ stays the affricate [tʃ] (⟨chamar⟩ [tʃaˈmaɾ]), because deaffrication to [ʃ] is completed in Lisbon only later.

Source: Teyssier (1980), sibilant chapter; Maia (1986); Fernão de Oliveira (1536).

### Other consonants

⟨lh⟩ [ʎ], ⟨nh⟩ [ɲ], ⟨h⟩ silent. ⟨r⟩ is the tap [ɾ], but word-initial ⟨r⟩, ⟨rr⟩, and ⟨r⟩ after ⟨n l s⟩ are the alveolar trill [r] (not uvular). ⟨b⟩ [b] and ⟨v/u⟩ consonantal [v] are kept distinct (central-southern norm). ⟨l⟩ is [l] in all positions. ⟨g, gu⟩ is [g] and ⟨qu⟩ before e/i is [k].

Source: Williams (1962); Teyssier (1980); Mateus & d'Andrade (2000) for symbol values.

### Nasal vowels

Vowel + ⟨m n⟩ in coda, and tilde vowels, are written as nasal vowels [ĩ ẽ ɐ̃ õ ũ] with no following nasal consonant. Nasal a is [ɐ̃]: ⟨campo⟩ [ˈkɐ̃pu], ⟨tempo⟩ [ˈtẽpu], ⟨irmã⟩ [iɾˈmɐ̃]. This follows the vowel-phoneme analysis stated in the note, not the VN analysis of Mateus & d'Andrade (2000).

Source: Teyssier (1980); Williams (1962); contrast Mateus & d'Andrade (2000) ch. 2.

### Stressed mid-vowel quality

Spelling does not distinguish open and closed stressed e/o except through accents (é ó vs ê ô) and the accented digraphs. Elsewhere, use the etymological value, which normally matches the modern European Portuguese cognate: ⟨terra⟩ [ˈtɛra], ⟨porta⟩ [ˈpɔɾta], ⟨medo⟩ [ˈmedu], ⟨todo⟩ [ˈtodu]. Unstressed mid vowels are always closed [e o].

Source: Williams (1962) on stressed vowels and metaphony; Mattos e Silva (2006).

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
