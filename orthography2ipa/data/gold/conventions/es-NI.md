# Transcription conventions: Nicaraguan Spanish (es-NI)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Write primary stress with ˈ before the stressed syllable of every stressed word (see function_words), monosyllables included: sol [ˈsol], pan [ˈpaŋ]. Unstressed words get no mark. Stress is lexical, so take it from the spelling. A word ending in a vowel, -n or -s has penultimate stress: casa [ˈkasa], hablan [ˈaβlaŋ]. Any other word has final stress: comer [koˈmeɾ], ciudad [sjuˈða]. A written accent overrides both rules: árbol [ˈaɾβol], café [kaˈfe], música [ˈmusika]. Keep the stress of a final -d even when the -d is deleted: verdad [beɾˈða]. Voseo forms are oxytone: tenés [teˈneh], hablás [aˈβlah], vení [beˈni], sos [ˈsoh]. Enclitics written solid with the verb do not move its stress: decime [deˈsime], dámelo [ˈdamelo]. Adverbs in -mente keep two stresses; write ˌ on the stressed syllable of the adjective and ˈ on -men-: rápidamente [ˌrapiðaˈmente]. Mark no other secondary stress. Spanish has no phonemic vowel length, no geminates and no case endings, so those parts of the topic do not apply.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on word stress (stress rules, -mente compounds); voseo stress follows Fontanella de Weinberg 1992, chapter on voseo in Central America.

## Stressed and unstressed words

These words are unstressed and written without ˈ. Definite articles: el, la, los, las, neuter lo. Object and reflexive clitics, whether written apart or hyphenated: me, te, se, nos, lo, la, los, las, le, les. Prenominal possessives: mi, tu, su, mis, tus, sus. All simple prepositions except según: a, ante, con, de, desde, en, entre, hacia, hasta, para, por, sin, sobre, tras. Conjunctions and complementizers: y, e, o, u, ni, que, pero, sino, si, aunque, porque. Relatives without a written accent: que, quien, cual, donde, cuando, como. Prenominal titles: don, doña, san. Examples: la casa [la ˈkasa], de mi tío [de mi ˈtio], te lo doy [te lo ˈdoj]. These words are stressed: subject and prepositional pronouns (yo, vos, él, ella, usted, ustedes, nosotros, ellos, mí, sí), indefinite articles un, una, unos, unas, demonstratives, nuestro and vuestro, numerals, según, accented interrogatives and exclamatives (qué, cómo, dónde, cuándo), all adverbs, and every verb form, auxiliaries and copulas included. Examples: un perro [ˈum ˈpero], vos sos [ˈboh ˈsoh], ha comido [ˈa koˈmiðo], qué querés [ˈke keˈɾeh].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on word stress, section on stressed vs. unstressed words. That section is followed for stressed indefinite articles. Treating auxiliary haber as stressed is this convention's choice, made for consistency.

## Pausal forms

Spanish has no pausal forms in the Arabic sense: no final short vowels, case endings or tanwīn are dropped. So no special pausal form applies at a comma or at the end of a sentence, and every word keeps the form it has before a vowel. Final vowels stay full: casa [ˈkasa]. Final /s/ is [h]: dos [ˈdoh]. Final /n/ is [ŋ]: pan [ˈpaŋ]. Final -d is deleted: usted [uhˈte]. A phrase-initial voiced obstruent is a stop: Dios [ˈdjoh] (see connected_speech).

Source: Hualde 2005, chapters on obstruents and nasals and on dialectal variation; Fontanella de Weinberg 1992, chapter on Central American Spanish.

## Connected speech

Write sandhi as changes on the edge of the affected word only; never join or drop words. (1) Nasal place assimilation: a word-final /n/ before a word starting with a consonant is written m before p, b, m: un beso [ˈum ˈbeso]. It is n before a dental, alveolar or palatal consonant (t, d, s, l, ɾ, r, n, tʃ, ʝ) and before f: con todo [kon ˈtoðo]. It is ŋ before k, g, h, w, before a vowel and before a pause: en casa [eŋ ˈkasa], en eso [eŋ ˈeso]. (2) Word-final /s/ is [h] before a consonant, before a vowel and before a pause: los amigos [loh aˈmiɣoh], es bueno [ˈeh ˈβweno]. (3) /b d g/ are stops [b d g] after a pause and after a nasal; /d/ is also a stop after /l/. Everywhere else they are approximants [β ð ɣ], across word boundaries too: la boca [la ˈβoka], el dedo [el ˈdeðo], un gato [ˈuŋ ˈgato], mucho gusto [ˈmutʃo ˈɣuhto]. (4) An initial r is the trill [r] after a word ending in n, l or s/h, and also elsewhere: los ríos [loh ˈrioh]. (5) Resyllabification of final consonants before a vowel, synalepha and vowel elision across words are not written. Each word keeps all its vowels, no gliding is written across a boundary, and no syllable marks are used: la otra [la ˈotɾa], de agua [de ˈaɣwa], y ella [i ˈeʝa].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapters on nasal assimilation, spirantization of /b d g/, and syllabification across word boundaries; Fontanella de Weinberg 1992 on /s/ weakening in Central America. Prevocalic aspiration of word-final /s/ in Nicaragua follows Lipski 1994 (Latin American Spanish, chapter on Nicaragua).

## Glides and diphthongs

Write an unstressed i or u next to another vowel in the same word as [j] or [w], both before and after the nucleus: tierra [ˈtjera], bueno [ˈbweno], ciudad [sjuˈða], aire [ˈajɾe], auto [ˈawto], hoy [ˈoj], muy [ˈmuj], ley [ˈlej]. Do not use i̯ u̯. This departs from Hualde 2005, who writes [i̯ u̯] after the nucleus; [j w] are used everywhere here for consistency. A written accent on i or u marks hiatus, and each vowel is then written in full: día [ˈdia], país [paˈih], baúl [baˈul]. Spelled hie- and hue- at the start of a word are written [ʝe] and [we]: hielo [ˈʝelo], huevo [ˈweβo]. The conjunction y as a word on its own is always [i]. Consonantal y and ll are written [ʝ]: calle [ˈkaʝe], yo [ˈʝo]. Gliding across word boundaries is never written.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on glides and diphthongs (followed for the analysis; the symbols are unified as j w).

## Other choices

### Coda /s/ inside the word

Write /s/ in a syllable coda as [h]: este [ˈehte], mismo [ˈmihmo], buscar [buhˈkaɾ]. Keep syllable-initial /s/ as [s], also between vowels: casa [ˈkasa], señor [seˈɲoɾ]. Never delete /s/ completely.

Source: Fontanella de Weinberg 1992, chapter on Central American Spanish; Hualde 2005, chapter on /s/ variation.

### Velar fricative

Write j, and g before e or i, as [h]: gente [ˈhente], México [ˈmehiko], ojo [ˈoho]. Never write [x] or [χ].

Source: Fontanella de Weinberg 1992, chapter on Central America; Hualde 2005, chapter on dialectal variation in /x/.

### Word-final -d

Delete word-final -d and keep the word's stress: verdad [beɾˈða], usted [uhˈte], salud [saˈlu]. Keep intervocalic /d/ inside the word as [ð], including in -ado: cansado [kanˈsaðo].

Source: The specification note on -d deletion; Hualde 2005, chapter on /d/ weakening.

### Word-final /n/

Write word-final /n/ as velar [ŋ] before a vowel and before a pause, and assimilate it before a consonant as given in connected_speech. Word-internal coda /n/ only assimilates in place: campo [ˈkampo], tengo [ˈteŋgo], canto [ˈkanto]. The note does not mention final velarization; it is added here because standard descriptions of Nicaraguan give it.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on nasals (velarization in Central America and the Caribbean); Lipski 1994, chapter on Nicaragua.

### Seseo and yeísmo

Write c before e or i, z and s all as [s]: cielo [ˈsjelo], zapato [saˈpato]. Write ll and y alike as [ʝ]: pollo [ˈpoʝo], cayó [kaˈʝo]. Never write [θ] or [ʎ].

Source: Penny 2002, chapter on American Spanish (seseo, yeísmo); Fontanella de Weinberg 1992.

### Rhotics and liquids

Write the trill [r] for rr, for word-initial r, and for r after n, l or s: perro [ˈpero], rojo [ˈroho], honra [ˈonra]. Write the tap [ɾ] elsewhere, coda r included: pero [ˈpeɾo], comer [koˈmeɾ]. Keep coda /ɾ/ and /l/ distinct; Caribbean-style neutralization is not written: puerta [ˈpweɾta], alto [ˈalto].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on rhotics and laterals; Fontanella de Weinberg 1992, chapter on Central America (absence of liquid neutralization there).

### Vowels and affricate

Write five vowels /a e i o u/ with no length or laxing marks: perro [ˈpero], todos [ˈtoðoh]. Write ch as [tʃ]: mucho [ˈmutʃo]. Write ñ as [ɲ]: niño [ˈniɲo]. Write voiceless stops without aspiration: [p t k].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapters on vowels and on obstruents.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
