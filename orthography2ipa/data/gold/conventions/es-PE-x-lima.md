# Transcription conventions: Peruvian Spanish (Lima) (es-PE-x-lima)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Spanish has lexical stress. It is free but predictable from the spelling: a written acute accent marks the stressed vowel. Without an accent, words ending in a vowel, n or s are stressed on the penultimate syllable, and all other words on the final syllable. Mark primary stress with ˈ before the stressed syllable on every stressed word, including monosyllabic content words. Unstressed function words (see function_words) get no mark. Examples: casa ˈkasa, canción kanˈsjon, cantar kanˈtaɾ, árbol ˈaɾβol, sol ˈsol, pan ˈpan. Place ˈ before the whole onset of the stressed syllable. Syllabify with maximal onsets: the clusters pr br tr dr kr ɡr fr pl bl kl ɡl fl tl stay together, and all other consonant sequences split. Examples: otro ˈotɾo, hablar aˈβlaɾ, atlas ˈatlas, constante konsˈtante, transporte tɾansˈpoɾte. A glide belongs to its syllable: bueno ˈbweno, nieve ˈnjeβe. Adverbs in -mente keep the stress of the adjective as secondary ˌ, with primary stress on -men-: rápidamente ˌrapiðaˈmente. Enclitics written together with the verb do not move its stress: dámelo ˈdamelo, diciéndole diˈsjendole. Long vowels, geminates and case endings do not exist in this lect, so no rule is needed for them.

Source: Hualde 2005, The Sounds of Spanish, ch. 15 (word stress, stressed and unstressed words) and ch. 9 (syllabification); RAE orthographic accent rules as summarised there.

## Stressed and unstressed words

The stress a word carries depends on its word class, not on emphasis in the sentence. Unstressed words keep their full vowels: Spanish has no vowel reduction, so there are no weak forms. They are written without ˈ. UNSTRESSED: (1) definite articles el la lo los las; (2) all simple prepositions except según: a ante bajo con contra de desde en entre hacia hasta para por sin sobre tras; (3) conjunctions y e o u ni que pero mas si porque aunque pues; (4) unaccented relatives que quien cual cuyo donde cuando como cuanto; (5) clitic pronouns me te se le les lo la los las nos os; (6) prenominal possessives mi mis tu tus su sus; (7) forms of address before names: don doña san fray sor. Examples: de la casa de la ˈkasa, que me lo dé ke me lo ˈde, mi amigo mi aˈmiɣo, con el perro kon el ˈpero. STRESSED: indefinite articles un una unos unas; demonstratives; subject and prepositional pronouns yo tú él ella usted nosotros ellos mí ti sí; stressed possessives nuestro vuestro mío tuyo suyo; accented interrogatives and exclamatives qué quién cuál dónde cuándo cómo cuánto; numerals; no and sí; según; muy; all adverbs, nouns and adjectives. Every verb form is stressed, including the auxiliaries haber, ser and estar and the form hay. Examples: un libro ˈun ˈliβɾo, ¿qué quieres? ˈke ˈkjeɾes, he visto ˈe ˈβisto, ha llegado ˈa ʝeˈɣaðo, no es ˈno ˈes.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 15 (inventory of stressed vs. unstressed words), following the traditional classification of Navarro Tomás and Quilis that Hualde adopts. Auxiliaries are treated as stressed, as in that tradition.

## Pausal forms

Pausal forms do not apply: Spanish has no pausal forms, case endings or nunation. Every word is written the same before a comma, at the end of the sentence and in the middle of a phrase. Final vowels and final /s/ are never dropped or changed at a pause: casas ˈkasas, café kaˈfe. Final /d/ is written ð everywhere, including before a pause: verdad beɾˈðað, Madrid maˈðɾið. A pause matters only for the word that follows it, which takes its post-pausal onset (see connected_speech).

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 9–10 (no pausal morphology; word-final /d/ as approximant). Final /s/ is conserved in Lima per Fontanella de Weinberg 1992 (Peruvian coastal Spanish).

## Connected speech

Each word stays in its own place. Processes across a word boundary change only the segments at the edges of the words; words are never joined. (1) /b d ɡ/ alternation applies across word boundaries. Write the stops b d ɡ at the start of the sentence, after a pause, and after a nasal. Write d after l as well. Write the approximants β ð ɣ everywhere else, including after vowels, r, s, and after l for b and ɡ. A pause means the start of the sentence or a position after any comma, semicolon, colon, full stop, question or exclamation mark, parenthesis or dash. Examples: la boca la ˈβoka, un beso ˈum ˈbeso, el dedo el ˈdeðo, el banco el ˈβaŋko, las ganas las ˈɣanas, por donde poɾ ˈðonde, ¡Vamos! ˈbamos. (2) Nasal place assimilation is written within and across words. A nasal is m before p b m, ŋ before k ɡ x, and n elsewhere (no ɱ or n̪). Examples: un perro ˈum ˈpero, con ganas koŋ ˈɡanas, en Francia en ˈfɾansja, tengo ˈteŋɡo. (3) Synalepha, i.e. vowel contact across words, is not written. Both vowels stay in full, even identical ones, and no glide is written across the boundary: la amiga la aˈmiɣa, de este de ˈeste, mi hijo mi ˈixo. (4) Resyllabification of a final consonant before a vowel is not shown, because no syllable marks are written: los otros los ˈotɾos. (5) Voicing of /s/ before a voiced consonant is not written: mismo ˈmismo, los mismos los ˈmismos. (6) Word-initial r is the trill r whatever precedes it: la rosa la ˈrosa.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 8 (spirantization of /b d ɡ/), ch. 10 (nasal assimilation, /s/ voicing), ch. 9 and 16 (synalepha and resyllabification). The broad level of detail (no ɱ n̪, no [z]) is this set's own simplification.

## Glides and diphthongs

In rising diphthongs, i.e. before the nucleus, write the high vowels as j and w: bien ˈbjen, bueno ˈbweno, ciudad sjuˈðað, cuando ˈkwando, huevo ˈweβo. In falling diphthongs, i.e. after the nucleus, write them as i̯ and u̯: aire ˈai̯ɾe, causa ˈkau̯sa, hay ˈai̯, muy ˈmui̯, rey ˈrei̯, seis ˈsei̯s, deuda ˈdeu̯ða. The conjunction y is always i: y él i ˈel. Write hiatus with two full vowels where an accent mark breaks the sequence (día ˈdia, país paˈis, búho ˈbuo). Also keep hiatus between two non-high vowels (poeta poˈeta, real reˈal, toalla toˈaʝa): Lima's standard pronunciation keeps it. Consonantal y and ll, and initial hi plus vowel, are the palatal obstruent ʝ, not the glide j: yo ˈʝo, calle ˈkaʝe, hielo ˈʝelo.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 9 (glides and diphthongs; j w vs. i̯ u̯ distinction, hiatus vs. synaeresis) and ch. 11 (palatal obstruent /ʝ/).

## Other choices

### yeísmo

ll and consonantal y merge as ʝ in every position, including after a nasal, l or pause. No ʎ, no ɟʝ, no ʒ/ʃ. Examples: llamar ʝaˈmaɾ, cónyuge ˈkonʝuxe, el yate el ˈʝate. This agrees with the specification note.

Source: Fontanella de Weinberg 1992 (yeísmo in coastal Peruvian Spanish); Hualde 2005, ch. 11.

### seseo

z and c before e or i are s; there is no θ. Examples: cielo ˈsjelo, zapato saˈpato, hacer aˈseɾ.

Source: Fontanella de Weinberg 1992; Penny 2002, ch. on American Spanish (seseo).

### coda /s/

Coda and final /s/ are always s, never aspirated [h] or deleted. Examples: estos ˈestos, después desˈpwes, mis libros mis ˈliβɾos. This agrees with the note on conservative Lima /s/. It differs from the descriptions of northern coastal and Caribbean varieties.

Source: Fontanella de Weinberg 1992 (Lima vs. other coastal varieties); Hualde 2005, ch. 10.

### velar fricative

j, and g before e or i, are the velar x, not glottal h. Examples: hijo ˈixo, gente ˈxente, México ˈmexiko.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 10 (/x/ realisations; velar in Peru); Fontanella de Weinberg 1992.

### rhotics

Write the trill r for rr, for word-initial r, and for r after n, l or s: perro ˈpero, Enrique enˈrike, alrededor alreðeˈðoɾ. Write the tap ɾ elsewhere, including in coda and word-final position. Coda r is not neutralised to a trill or fricative: caro ˈkaɾo, carta ˈkaɾta, amor aˈmoɾ.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 11 (rhotics).

### intervocalic /d/ in -ado

Write ð. It is not deleted, since the standard Lima media pronunciation keeps it: cansado kanˈsaðo, nada ˈnaða.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 8; Fontanella de Weinberg 1992 (Peruvian coastal norm).

### coda obstruents and x

Write learned coda clusters as spelled; they are not reduced. The letter x between vowels is ks. Examples: actor akˈtoɾ, examen ekˈsamen, obtener oβteˈneɾ, extraño eksˈtɾaɲo. Coda b d ɡ follow the approximant rule. The letter h is silent, v is b/β, ch is tʃ, ñ is ɲ, and qu/gu before e or i are k/ɡ: chico ˈtʃiko, año ˈaɲo, guerra ˈɡera.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 8 and 10 (coda obstruents, orthography–sound correspondences).

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
