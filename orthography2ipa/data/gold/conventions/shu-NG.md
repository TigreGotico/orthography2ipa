# Transcription conventions: Nigerian Arabic (Shuwa) (shu-NG)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark one primary stress (ˈ) before the stressed syllable of every content word, monosyllables included. Place it as follows: (1) a final superheavy syllable (CVVC, CVCC, including a final geminate) takes stress: kiˈtaːb, ˈamm. (2) Otherwise a heavy penult (CVV or CVC) takes stress: ˈgallab, ˈkaːtib, maˈdiːna. (3) Otherwise stress the antepenult, or the first syllable of a disyllable: ˈkatab 'he wrote', ˈmaktaba. A geminate closes its first syllable (gal.lab), so that syllable is heavy. Long vowels are written with ː and make a syllable heavy. Lexical exception: the 1sg perfect keeps final stress as a trace of lost -t, katabt > kaˈtab 'I wrote', against ˈkatab 'he wrote'. Case endings do not exist in the vernacular and never count; if the source spells them, drop them before stressing. Lexical tone is not marked.

Source: Owens 1993 (A Grammar of Nigerian Arabic), phonology chapter on stress; Owens 2006 pp.13-14 for the 1sg perfect final-stress evidence. This is the general Eastern/Bedouin weight-sensitive rule, applied as Owens describes it for Nigerian Arabic.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Monosyllabic function words are written without ˈ in connected speech. These are the article al (part of its host word, never stressed itself), the prepositions fi, le/li, min, ma, bi, the conjunctions wa, aw, fa, the negator ma, the relative al, and short object or possessive forms written as separate words. Polysyllabic function words take their lexical stress by the stress rule: ˈala 'on', ˈlaːkin 'but', ˈana 'I', ˈinta 'you'. Independent pronouns are always stressed, monosyllables included: ˈhu 'he', ˈhi 'she'. Demonstratives are always stressed: ˈda, ˈdi, ˈdool. The auxiliary kaːn/ˈkaːn 'was' is stressed. Negative existential maˈfi is stressed on its final syllable. When an article or suffix is attached, the stress of the whole word follows the stress rule: al-ˈbeːt, beːˈtak.

Source: Owens 1993, chapters on pronouns, particles and prepositions; prosodic treatment follows the general clitic analysis in Watson 2002.

## Pausal forms

Nowhere. Nigerian Arabic has no case endings, no tanwīn and no Classical pausal alternation, so every word is written in the same vernacular form at sentence end, at a comma and sentence-internally. If the source text is vowelled with Classical endings (-u, -a, -i, -un, -an, -in), drop them everywhere: ⟨كِتَابٌ⟩ kiˈtaːb. Tāʾ marbūṭa is written -a (maˈdiːna). It is written -at only before an attached suffix (madiːˈnatak) or as the head of a synthetic genitive: ˈbagarat ar-ˈraːdʒil.

Source: Owens 1993 (vernacular morphology without case or nunation); Holes 2004 and Versteegh 2001 on the loss of case and pausal forms in the dialects.

## Connected speech

Each word is written in its own citation form, and no process across word boundaries is transcribed. There is no cross-word assimilation (min ˈbaːb, not mim ˈbaːb) and no elision of a word-initial vowel after a vowel. The article is always written al, or assimilated inside its own word. There is no liaison or resyllabification, and no spread of emphasis or ɑ-backing across a boundary. Inside a word the obligatory processes are written: (a) article assimilation to coronals (sun letters): ar-ˈraːdʒil, as-ˈsuːg, an-ˈnaːs, against al-ˈbeːt; (b) emphasis on the vowels adjacent to an emphatic in the same word (see other).

Source: Owens 1993 (article allomorphy and assimilation); Watson 2002 for emphasis spreading as word-internal. Cross-word sandhi is left out to keep words aligned with the tool's segmentation.

## Glides and diphthongs

Consonantal glides are always written j and w, never i̯ or u̯: ˈwalad, jaˈmiːn, ˈsawwa, aˈjjaːm. The historical diphthongs *aj and *aw are written as the monophthongs eː and oː in vernacular words: beːt 'house', leːl 'night', joːm 'day', loːn 'colour', keːf 'how', weːn 'where'. NOTE: this follows the published descriptions and departs from the specification note, which keeps [aj aw] (bajt, kajf, wajn) and remaps ⟨وَيْ⟩ and ⟨يَوْ⟩ to [waj] and [jaw]. Owens 1993 and Kaye 1976 both give the eː/oː forms for Nigerian and Chadian Arabic. Where a sequence survives as a diphthong (learned words, or before a geminate glide), write it as vowel plus j or w: ˈdawla.

Source: Owens 1993 (lexicon and phonology: beet, yoom); Kaye 1976 (monophthongisation of *aj, *aw to eː, oː across the Chad–Sudan group); Abu-Absi & Sinaud 1968 (five qualities /i e a o u/ with length).

## Other choices

### qāf

ق is written ɡ in all vernacular words: ɡaːl 'he said', ˈɡalib. It is written q only in clearly learned, Classical or religious words, such as qurˈʔaːn, and is never ʔ.

Source: Owens 1993; Kaye 1976.

### interdentals and jīm

ث is written t, ذ is written d, and ظ is written dˤ: ˈtalaːta, ˈdahab, dˤulm. ج is written dʒ, not ɟ: ˈdʒamal.

Source: Owens 2006 p.25 (loss of interdentals); Owens 1993.

### emphatics and vowel backing

ص, ض and ط are written sˤ, dˤ and tˤ. The emphatics lˤ, rˤ and mˤ are written only in words with lexical emphasis, as in the pairs gallab/galˤlˤab, karra/karˤrˤa, amm/amˤmˤ, and in alˤˈlˤaːh. Short and long a adjacent to an emphatic in the same word are written ɑ and ɑː: sˤɑːˈbuːn, ˈtˤɑjjib. Implosives [ɓ ɗ ɗˤ] are not written; use b, d, dˤ.

Source: Owens 2006 pp.19, 25; Watson 2002 on pharyngealisation.

### gahawa (low-vowel insertion after gutturals)

After x, ɣ, ħ, ʕ or h closing a syllable after a, insert a: ˈɡahawa 'coffee', aˈhamar 'red', ˈbaxar 'back'. Write the vernacular output, not the Classical shape.

Source: Owens 1993; Owens 2006 p.25; Prochazka 2026 pp.310, 318.

### hamza

Do not write glottal stop before a word-initial vowel: ˈakal, ˈana. Hamza after a vowel is lost, and the vowel is lengthened where the vernacular has a long vowel: raːs 'head', biːr 'well'. Write ʔ only in learned words: qurˈʔaːn.

Source: Owens 1993; Kaye 1976.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
