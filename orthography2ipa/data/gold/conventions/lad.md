# Transcription conventions: Ladino (Judeo-Spanish) (lad)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Ladino stress follows the Spanish type: it is lexical, falls on one of the last three syllables, and is marked with ˈ before the stressed syllable in every word of two or more syllables. Monosyllables never get a mark. Default rule: words ending in a vowel, -n or -s are stressed on the penult, as in [ˈkaza] 'house', [ˈdʒudjo] 'Jew' and [ˈavlan] 'they speak'. Words ending in any other consonant are stressed on the last syllable, as in [kanˈtar] 'sing' and [koˈɾason] 'heart'. Where the Latin-script spelling has an accent, or the word is a known exception, stress the syllable that carries it. Judezmo verb stress differs from Spanish: preterite 1sg [kanˈti], preterite 1pl [kanˈtimos], imperfect 1pl [kanˈtavamos]. Ottoman Turkish loans keep final stress, as in [koˈlaj] 'easy' and [bojaˈdʒi] 'painter'. Hebrew and Aramaic loans take the stress of established Judezmo pronunciation, not of Modern Israeli Hebrew. Words with the ending ⟨-ía⟩ have stress on the i in hiatus: [aˈvia], [ˈdia]. Long vowels: none, as there is no vowel length. Geminates: none, as there are no geminates; written ⟨rr⟩ is the trill /r/, not a geminate. Case endings: not applicable, as Ladino has no case.

Source: Hualde, J.I. & Şaul, M. (2011), 'Istanbul Judeo-Spanish', Journal of the International Phonetic Association 41(1), section on stress. Verb forms follow Penny 2002, pp. 276–280. General Romance stress typology follows Schmid 2013.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Follow the Spanish-type split between stressed and unstressed words; Judezmo patterns with Spanish here. Because monosyllables are never marked, this rule matters only for words of two or more syllables. UNSTRESSED, so no ˈ even when polysyllabic: definite articles (el, la, los, las, lo); object and reflexive clitics (me, te, se, lo, la, le, mos, vos, los, las, les), including clitics split off by a hyphen; prepositions (para [paɾa], sovre [sovɾe], entre [entɾe], dezde [dezde], asta [asta]); conjunctions and relatives (porke [poɾke], kuando and komo when they are conjunctions or relatives); and prenominal mi, tu, su. STRESSED, so polysyllables take ˈ: indefinite articles ([ˈuna], [ˈunos]); subject and prepositional pronouns ([ˈeja], [ˈeʎos] is not used, write [ˈejos], [moˈzotɾos]); demonstratives ([ˈeste], [aˈkel]); interrogatives ([ˈkuando?], [ˈkomo?], [ˈonde]); possessives muestro and vuestro; numerals; adverbs; and all verb forms, including auxiliaries and copulas ([aˈvia], [ˈesta], [ˈeɾa]). Examples: [de la ˈkaza]; [paɾa el ˈiʒo]; [me lo ˈdize]; [ˈeja ˈkanta].

Source: The Spanish word-class classification of Navarro Tomás (1918, Manual de pronunciación española) and Quilis (1993, Tratado de fonología y fonética españolas). Its application to Judezmo is consistent with Hualde & Şaul 2011, whose sample text is transcribed this way. Ladino-specific forms such as mos and muestro follow Penny 2002, pp. 276–280.

## Pausal forms

Not applicable: Ladino has no pausal forms, case endings or nunation, so every word is written in the same form at a comma, at the end of a sentence and inside a sentence.

Source: Hualde & Şaul 2011; Schmid 2013 (no pausal phenomena described).

## Connected speech

Write no cross-word processes. Each word appears in its citation form and keeps its own stress mark. Specifically: no sinalefa (vowel merger across a boundary); no resyllabification marks; no linking symbols; no voicing of word-final /s/ before a voiced sound; no nasal place assimilation across a boundary. Examples: [la ˈalma], [los ˈombɾes], [en ˈboka], [es ˈbweno]. Inside a word, write only what the standard phonological form has. Write /n/ as [m] only before p or b, where the spelling has m: [ˈkampo]. Write [z] before a voiced consonant only where the lexical form has it: [ˈmizmo]. Write [ŋ] nowhere. Contractions written as one word (al, del) are transcribed as one word: [al], [del].

Source: A broad phonemic convention. The processes themselves are described as optional, phonetic-level phenomena in Hualde & Şaul 2011, sections on consonant allophony and the sample text. Writing citation forms keeps to the tool's rule that one IPA word corresponds to each orthographic word.

## Glides and diphthongs

Write non-syllabic high vowels as consonantal j and w, never as i̯ u̯. Rising diphthongs: [ˈtjera] 'land', [ˈbweno] 'good', [ˈgwevo] 'egg', [ˈdʒudjo]. Falling diphthongs: [ˈajɾe] 'air', [muj] 'very', [ˈkaje] 'street'. Orthographic ⟨y⟩ before or after a vowel is [j]: [jo] 'I', [ˈkaje]. Never write [ʝ] or [ɟʝ]. In hiatus, both vowels are full vowels and the stressed one is marked: [ˈdia], [ˈrio], [aˈvia]. Do not form glides across word boundaries.

Source: Hualde & Şaul 2011, vowel and diphthong section, which uses j and w. Yeísmo, the merger of ll with y, follows Penny 2002, pp. 276–280.

## Other choices

### b versus v (disagrees with note 4)

Write /b/ and /v/ as distinct phonemes, as the Latin-script spelling does. ⟨b⟩ is [b]: [ˈboka] 'mouth', [ˈbweno]. ⟨v⟩ is labiodental [v]: [ˈvida] 'life', [ˈavlar] is not correct, write [avˈlaɾ] 'speak', [kaˈvajo] 'horse'. Do not write spirant [β], [ð] or [ɣ]; /b d ɡ/ are always written b d ɡ in this broad transcription. The note's claim that b and v have fully merged is not followed: Judezmo keeps a /b/~/v/ contrast inherited from medieval Castilian.

Source: Penny 2002, pp. 276–280; Hualde & Şaul 2011, consonant inventory.

### ll / palatal lateral (disagrees with note 5)

Standard Istanbul and Balkan Judezmo has yeísmo, so write [j] for etymological ll: [kaˈvajo], [ˈkaje], [ˈejos]. Do not write ʎ. The note's preserved /ʎ/ is not followed.

Source: Penny 2002, pp. 276–280; Hualde & Şaul 2011.

### velar fricative /x/ (qualifies note 7)

The note says Ladino has no /x/. Inherited Romance words indeed have none: Spanish j/g corresponds to [ʒ] or [dʒ], as in [ˈiʒo] or [ˈfiʒo] 'son' and [muˈʒeɾ] 'woman'. However, Hebrew and Turkish loans spelled ⟨h⟩ have [x], so write it: [xaˈxam] 'rabbi', [xaˈveɾ] 'friend'. Elsewhere ⟨h⟩ is silent.

Source: Hualde & Şaul 2011, consonant inventory (marginal /x/); Harris 1994.

### affricates and sibilants (qualifies note 2)

Map the spelling to sibilants and affricates as follows. ⟨s⟩ is [s] and ⟨z⟩ is [z]; the two contrast between vowels: [ˈkaza] 'house' vs [paˈsaɾ] 'pass'. ⟨sh⟩ is [ʃ], ⟨j⟩ is [ʒ], ⟨ch⟩ is [tʃ] and ⟨dj⟩ is [dʒ]: [ʃaˈbat], [ˈdʒente] (written djente), [ˈnotʃe]. Medieval /ts/ has merged with /s/ ([ˈsjelo] 'sky'). Write [ts] only where a loanword has it, so the note's claim that /ts/ is preserved is not followed.

Source: Penny 2002, pp. 16–18 and 276–280; Hualde & Şaul 2011.

### rhotics

Write the trill [r] for word-initial ⟨r⟩, for ⟨rr⟩, and for ⟨r⟩ after n, l or s: [ˈrio], [ˈpero] vs [ˈpeɾo] (perro 'dog' is [ˈpero], pero 'but' is [peɾo]). Write the tap [ɾ] everywhere else, including at the end of a word and before a consonant: [kanˈtaɾ], [ˈvedɾe] 'green'. The kanˈtar given under stress above is to be read as [kanˈtaɾ].

Source: Hualde & Şaul 2011, consonant inventory.

### initial f- and lexical variants (qualifies note 6)

Do not apply initial f- by rule. Write the word as the text spells it, since Judezmo has both [f] and zero for Latin F-: [ˈfiʒo] or [ˈiʒo], [aˈzeɾ] 'do', [eɾˈmozo] 'beautiful'. Apply the same principle to Judezmo lexical forms such as [ˈmwestɾo] 'our' and [ˈvedɾe] 'green'.

Source: Penny 2002, pp. 276–280; Harris 1994.

### mid vowels and final-vowel raising

Write the five vowels /i e a o u/ only. Do not write ɛ or ɔ, and do not write the Salonika or Balkan raising of final -o and -e to [u] and [i]: write [ˈkaza], [ˈbweno], [ˈnotʃe]. This follows the Istanbul standard.

Source: Hualde & Şaul 2011, vowel section; the Balkan raising is described in Harris 1994.

### palatal nasal

⟨ny⟩ is [ɲ]: [ˈaɲo] 'year'. Where ⟨ni⟩ before a vowel is a sequence of n plus glide, write [nj].

Source: Hualde & Şaul 2011.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
