# Transcription conventions: Uruguayan Spanish (es-UY)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark primary stress with ˈ immediately before the stressed syllable of every stressed word, monosyllables included: casa [ˈkasa], café [kaˈfe], sol [ˈsol], árbol [ˈaɾβol], música [ˈmusika]. Place stress by the orthographic accent rules. A written accent marks the stressed vowel. Otherwise, a word ending in a vowel, -n or -s is stressed on the penult, and any other word on the final syllable. Voseo verb forms follow the same rules: cantás [kanˈtas], tenés [teˈnes], comé [koˈme], decime [deˈsime]. Syllabify inside the word only, putting the onset consonant after ˈ: problema [pɾoˈβlema], hablar [aˈβlaɾ]. A cross-word resyllabification never moves the mark into another word. Secondary stress is not marked, except in adverbs in -mente, which have ˌ on the adjective base and ˈ on -mente: rápidamente [ˌrapiðaˈmente]. Unstressed function words (see function_words) carry no mark. Spanish has no phonemic long vowels, no geminates and no case endings, so those parts of the topic do not apply. Doubled vowel letters are two syllables: leer [leˈeɾ], creer [kɾeˈeɾ]. Doubled consonant letters stand for single segments: perro [ˈpero], llama [ˈʒama].

Source: Hualde 2005, The Sounds of Spanish, ch. 13 (stress placement and the double stress of -mente adverbs); RAE/ASALE orthographic accent rules.

## Stressed and unstressed words

These words are unstressed and get no ˈ: the definite articles el, la, los, las, lo; the object and reflexive clitics me, te, se, lo, la, le, nos, los, las, les; prepositions other than según; the conjunctions y, e, o, u, ni, que, pero, mas, si, porque, aunque, pues; the relatives and relative adverbs que, quien, cual, cuyo, donde, cuando, como (when not interrogative or exclamative); the prenominal possessives mi, tu, su, mis, tus, sus; and the titles don, doña, san, sor, fray. Examples: el perro [el ˈpero], me lo dijo [me lo ˈðixo], a la casa [a la ˈkasa], en mi pueblo [em mi ˈpweβlo]. These words are stressed and get ˈ: the indefinite articles un, una, unos, unas [ˈun], [ˈuna]; the subject pronouns yo [ˈʒo], vos [ˈbos], tú [ˈtu], él [ˈel], ella [ˈeʒa], usted [usˈteð], nosotros [noˈsotɾos], ustedes [usˈteðes]; the prepositional pronouns mí, ti, sí; the demonstratives; the postnominal possessives mío, tuyo, nuestro; numerals; según [seˈɣun]; tan; the interrogatives and exclamatives qué, quién, cuál, dónde, cuándo, cómo, cuánto; and every verb form, auxiliaries included: ha [ˈa], hemos [ˈemos], está [ehˈta], fue [ˈfwe]. Enclitics joined without a hyphen belong to their host word: dámelo [ˈdamelo]. A clitic written as a separate word is unstressed.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 13 (stressed and unstressed words); Navarro Tomás, Manual de pronunciación española, §§ on palabras acentuadas e inacentuadas, as summarized in Hualde 2005.

## Pausal forms

Not applicable. Spanish has no pausal forms, no case endings and no nunation. A word is written the same way before a pause and inside a phrase, except for the context-sensitive allophones described under connected_speech and other: stops after a pause, and [s] rather than [h] before a pause.

Source: Hualde 2005 (no pausal morphology in Spanish).

## Connected speech

Each word is written in its own place. A cross-word process changes the segment at the edge of a word, judged from the neighbouring word, and never deletes or moves segments into another word. A sentence start or a comma, colon, semicolon or dash counts as a pause. (1) Spirantization. /b d ɡ/ are written as stops [b d ɡ] after a pause and after a nasal, and /d/ also after /l/. Everywhere else they are [β ð ɣ], including when the preceding word ends in a vowel, liquid or /s/. Examples: la boca [la ˈβoka], un beso [um ˈbeso], el dedo [el ˈdeðo], los gatos [loh ˈɣatos], sentence-initial Gracias [ˈɡɾasjas]. (2) Nasal place assimilation. Word-final /n/ is written [m] before p b β m and [ŋ] before k ɡ ɣ x, and n elsewhere, including before f: en Paysandú [em pajsanˈdu], un gato [uŋ ˈɡato], con fuerza [kon ˈfweɾsa]. (3) /s/ before a consonant is written [h], and this applies across word boundaries too. Before a vowel or a pause it stays [s]: los perros [loh ˈperos], los amigos [los aˈmiɣos], es así [es aˈsi], vamos. [ˈbamos]. See other. (4) Vowel sequences across words (synalepha) are not written. Both vowels stay in full, as syllabic vowels, and stay in their own words: la amiga [la aˈmiɣa], mi hijo [mi ˈixo], y ella [i ˈeʒa]. (5) Word-final r before a vowel is the tap [ɾ], not a trill: por eso [poɾ ˈeso]. Word-initial r is always the trill [r], whatever precedes it: la rosa [la ˈrosa]. (6) Resyllabification and liaison are not marked. There are no syllable dots and no linking ties.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 8 (spirantization of /b d ɡ/), ch. 9 (nasal assimilation), ch. 10 (/s/ weakening), ch. 14 (synalepha and resyllabification), with Fontanella de Weinberg 1992 on Río de la Plata /s/ aspiration.

## Glides and diphthongs

Non-syllabic high vowels are written with the glide symbols [j] and [w], never i̯ u̯. This holds in rising diphthongs, as in tiene [ˈtjene], bueno [ˈbweno], cuatro [ˈkwatɾo], ciudad [sjuˈðað], and in falling diphthongs, as in aire [ˈajɾe], auto [ˈawto], hoy [ˈoj], muy [ˈmuj], rey [ˈrej]. Every ⟨i⟩ or ⟨u⟩ next to another vowel in the same word is a glide unless it bears a written accent: día [ˈdia], país [paˈis], continúa [kontiˈnua]. ⟨u⟩ in gue, gui, que, qui is silent: guerra [ˈɡera], queso [ˈkeso]. ⟨ü⟩ is [w]: pingüino [piŋˈɡwino]. The conjunction y is always the vowel [i], never a glide or consonant: y ahora [i aˈoɾa]. Word-initial hi before a vowel and ⟨y⟩ before a vowel are not glides but the Rioplatense fricative [ʒ]: hielo [ˈʒelo], yerba [ˈʒeɾβa], yo [ˈʒo]. Word-initial hu before a vowel is [w]: hueso [ˈweso].

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 7 (glides and diphthongs, j w notation); Fontanella de Weinberg 1992 (Rioplatense [ʒ] for consonantal y/hi-).

## Other choices

### ll/y (žeísmo vs šeísmo)

⟨ll⟩, consonantal ⟨y⟩ and initial hi before a vowel are written uniformly as voiced [ʒ]: calle [ˈkaʒe], lluvia [ˈʒuβja], mayo [ˈmaʒo], yo [ˈʒo]. The spec note mentions a devoicing trend toward [ʃ] among younger speakers. That trend is not transcribed: [ʃ] is never used for these letters, so all rows stay consistent.

Source: Fontanella de Weinberg 1992, El español de América, section on the Río de la Plata (žeísmo and its devoicing); Hualde 2005, ch. 8.

### Coda /s/ aspiration

In standard Montevideo speech, /s/ at the end of a syllable before a consonant is aspirated, so it is written [h] both inside words and across word boundaries: mismo [ˈmihmo], este [ˈehte], está [ehˈta], los dos [loh ˈðos]. Before a vowel and before a pause it is written [s]: más o menos [mas o ˈmenos], ¿Vos sabés? [ˈbos saˈβes]. It is never deleted and never written [z]. ⟨x⟩ is [ks] between vowels and [h] before a consonant: examen [ekˈsamen], explicar [ehpliˈkaɾ].

Source: Fontanella de Weinberg 1992 (Río de la Plata /s/ aspiration); Hualde 2005, ch. 10.

### Seseo and the velar fricative

⟨c⟩ before e or i, ⟨z⟩ and ⟨s⟩ are all /s/ (seseo), with no [θ]: cielo [ˈsjelo], zapato [saˈpato]. ⟨j⟩, and ⟨g⟩ before e or i, are written velar [x], not [h]: gente [ˈxente], ojo [ˈoxo].

Source: Hualde 2005, chs. 8–9; Penny 2002, ch. 5 (seseo in American Spanish).

### Word-final /d/ and intervocalic -ado

Word-final /d/ is kept and written [ð]: verdad [beɾˈðað], usted [usˈteð]. The colloquial Rioplatense loss of this /d/ is not transcribed. Intervocalic /d/ in -ado is written in full: cansado [kanˈsaðo].

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 8 (weakening of /d/), choosing the careful standard over the variable deletion.

### Rhotics

The trill [r] is written for ⟨rr⟩, for word-initial r, and for r after n, l or s/h: perro [ˈpero], rosa [ˈrosa], honra [ˈonra], Israel [ihraˈel]. All other r is the tap [ɾ], including in tr clusters, which have no assibilation: tres [ˈtɾes], mar [ˈmaɾ].

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 10.

### Silent h and vowels

⟨h⟩ is silent, except in hie- and hue- as given under glides: hablar [aˈβlaɾ]. ⟨ch⟩ is [tʃ]: mucho [ˈmutʃo]. The five vowels are written a e i o u with no laxing or reduction in unstressed syllables. Portuguese-influenced border (DPU) features are not used.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 6; spec note on DPU.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
