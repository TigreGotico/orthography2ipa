# Transcription conventions: Venezuelan Llanero Spanish (es-VE-x-llanero)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark primary stress with ˈ before the stressed syllable. Stress is lexical and is read from the spelling. (1) A written accent marks the stressed vowel: canción [kanˈsjoŋ], árbol [ˈaɾbol], sábana [ˈsabana]. (2) With no written accent, a word ending in a vowel, n or s takes penultimate stress: casa [ˈkasa], llanos [ˈʝanoh], cantan [ˈkantaŋ]. (3) With no written accent, a word ending in any other consonant takes final stress: caballo [kaˈbaʝo], verdad [beɾˈdad], amor [aˈmoɾ]. Written y and the u of gu/qu are not vowels for this rule: estoy [ehˈtoi̯], ¿qué? [ˈke]. Stressed monosyllables (content words and the stressed function words listed under function_words) get ˈ: sol [ˈsol], yo [ˈʝo], ¿qué? [ˈke]. Unstressed function words get no mark. Enclitics written solid with the verb do not move the spelled stress: dámelo [ˈdamelo]. Adverbs in -mente take primary stress on -men- and a secondary ˌ on the adjective's stress: rápidamente [ˌrapidaˈmente]. No other secondary stress is marked, and syllable boundaries are not marked. The lect has no long vowels, no geminates and no case endings, so these parts of the topic do not apply.

Source: Hualde, J. I. (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, Cambridge UP, chapter on word stress; Quilis, A. (1993), Tratado de fonología y fonética españolas, Gredos, chapter on accent. Standard Spanish stress rules, which Llanero shares.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Unstressed (no ˈ): definite articles el la lo los las [el la lo loh lah]. All prepositions except según: a de en con por para sin sobre desde hasta [a de eŋ koŋ poɾ paɾa siŋ sobɾe dehde ahta]. Conjunctions y e o u ni pero que si (conditional), porque, aunque: y [i], pero [peɾo]. Object and reflexive clitics me te se lo la le nos los las les, whether proclitic or hyphen-separated. Prenominal possessives mi tu su mis tus sus nuestro: mi casa [mi ˈkasa]. Relatives and subordinators que quien donde como cuando when not interrogative or exclamative. Stressed (ˈ): indefinite articles un una unos unas [ˈuŋ ˈuna]. Subject pronouns and prepositional pronouns: yo tú él ella usted mí ti sí [ˈʝo ˈtu ˈel]. Demonstratives, numerals and postposed possessives (mío, suyo). Interrogatives and exclamatives qué cuál quién dónde cómo cuándo cuánto [ˈke ˈdonde]. según, and every verb form including the auxiliaries haber and ser: ha [ˈa], he [ˈe], han [ˈaŋ], es [ˈeh], hemos cantado [ˈemoh kanˈtado]. All adverbs and all nouns, adjectives and verbs.

Source: Navarro Tomás, T. (1918), Manual de pronunciación española, sections on unaccented and accented words (palabras inacentuadas / acentuadas); Quilis, A. (1993), Tratado, list of tonic and atonic words, which treats verbs including auxiliaries as stressed; Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chapter on stressed and unstressed words.

## Pausal forms

Not applicable. Spanish has no pausal forms, case endings or nunation. Every word is written in the same form at the end of a sentence, at a comma and inside a phrase. Coda /s/ is written [h] and final /n/ is written [ŋ] in all positions, as set out under other.

Source: Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish; Lipski, J. M. (1994), Latin American Spanish, Longman, chapter on Venezuela. No pausal morphology is described for any variety of Spanish.

## Connected speech

Each word keeps its own segments in its own place. Resyllabification of a final consonant before a vowel (los amigos) is not shown: [loh aˈmiɣoh] is not written; write [loh aˈmigoh]. Word-final s stays [h] and word-final n stays [ŋ] before a vowel as well: en el agua [eŋ el ˈagwa], las aguas [lah ˈagwah]. Vowel contact across words (synalepha, fusion of identical vowels) is not written; both vowels stay full and no glide is created across the boundary: la amiga [la aˈmiga], de un [de ˈuŋ], mi hijo [mi ˈiho]. No elision is written. No nasal place assimilation is written across words, because final n is always [ŋ]: un poco [ˈuŋ ˈpoko]. Word-initial r is always the trill [r] in every context: el río [el ˈrio]. Inside a word, n is written [ŋ] before k and g: banco [ˈbaŋko], tengo [ˈteŋgo]. Spelled m stays [m]. No other assimilation is written.

Source: Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chapters on resyllabification, synalepha and nasal assimilation, used only as a description of the processes; the decision not to write cross-word processes is a convention of this specification, required by the one-IPA-word-per-word segmentation. Velar final n before vowels follows Lipski (1994), chapter on Venezuela.

## Glides and diphthongs

Use [j w] for glides before a vowel (rising diphthongs and triphthong onsets): tierra [ˈtjera], bueno [ˈbweno], agua [ˈagwa], ciudad [sjuˈdad], buey [ˈbwei̯]. Use [i̯ u̯] for glides after a vowel (falling diphthongs), including word-final y: aire [ˈai̯ɾe], causa [ˈkau̯sa], hoy [ˈoi̯], muy [ˈmui̯], rey [ˈrei̯]. Treat a written accent on i or u as a hiatus and write two full vowels: día [ˈdia], país [paˈih], baúl [baˈul]. The conjunction y is [i]. Consonantal y and ll before a vowel are [ʝ] (yeísmo): yo [ˈʝo], calle [ˈkaʝe]. Initial hie- is [ʝe]: hierba [ˈʝeɾba]. Initial hue- is [we]: hueso [ˈweso]. No glides are formed across word boundaries.

Source: Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chapter on diphthongs and hiatus, which uses j w versus i̯ u̯; yeísmo per the specification note, after Lipski (1994).

## Other choices

### coda /s/

The note models only seseo, so this is a departure from the note. Write every syllable-final /s/ as [h], word-internally and word-finally, before consonants, before vowels and at a pause: los llanos [loh ˈʝanoh], este [ˈehte], más [ˈmah]. Never write it as zero, even though deletion occurs in speech. Write onset /s/ as [s], including c before e or i and z (seseo): cielo [ˈsjelo], zapato [saˈpato].

Source: Lipski (1994), Latin American Spanish, chapter on Venezuela: aspiration of syllable-final /s/ throughout Venezuela including the Llanos. Seseo per the specification note.

### word-final /n/

Write word-final n as [ŋ] in every context: pan [ˈpaŋ], son [ˈsoŋ], cantan [ˈkantaŋ]. Inside a word, follow the rule given under connected_speech. This goes beyond the note.

Source: Lipski (1994), chapter on Venezuela: velarization of word-final /n/.

### /x/

Write the sound spelled j, and g before e or i, as glottal [h]: caja [ˈkaha], gente [ˈhente], México [ˈmehiko]. Spelled h is silent: hombre [ˈombɾe]. Spelled x elsewhere is [ks]: taxi [ˈtaksi].

Source: Lipski (1994), chapter on Venezuela: /x/ realized as glottal [h].

### voiced stops and intervocalic /d/

This is a broad transcription. Write /b d g/ as [b d g] in all positions and do not write the spirants [β ð ɣ]: abogado [aboˈgado], lado [ˈlado]. Write v as [b]: vaca [ˈbaka]. Always write intervocalic d, including in -ado and -ada: cansado [kanˈsado], not [kanˈsao]. Weakening and deletion of d are left unwritten.

Source: Phonemic representation as in Quilis (1993), Tratado; d-weakening in Venezuela is noted in Lipski (1994) but is not written, as a broad-transcription convention of this specification.

### rhotics and laterals

Write the trill [r] for rr, for word-initial r, and for r after n, l or s: perro [ˈpero], río [ˈrio], honra [ˈonra]. Write the tap [ɾ] everywhere else, including in the coda: pero [ˈpeɾo], mar [ˈmaɾ]. Keep coda r and l distinct; Caribbean lambdacism or rhotacism is not written: alma [ˈalma], carta [ˈkaɾta]. Write ch as [tʃ] and ñ as [ɲ]: muchacho [muˈtʃatʃo], año [ˈaɲo].

Source: Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chapter on rhotics. Lipski (1994), chapter on Venezuela, treats r/l neutralization as a coastal Caribbean feature rather than a feature of the interior plains.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
