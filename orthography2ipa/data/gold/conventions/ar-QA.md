# Transcription conventions: Qatari Arabic (ar-QA)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark primary stress with ˈ before the stressed syllable in every word of two or more syllables. Leave monosyllables and unstressed function words (see function_words) unmarked. Syllabify first: a single consonant between vowels starts the next syllable, and a cluster or geminate is split between syllables, so a geminate always closes the syllable before it. Then apply these steps in order. (1) If the final syllable is superheavy (CVːC, CVCC, or a long vowel before a suffix), stress it: kaˈtabt, ʃaˈfoːh, ʕaˈleːk. (2) Otherwise, if the penult is heavy (CVː or CVC, including the first half of a geminate), stress it: maˈdiːna, muˈdarris, ˈmaktab, ˈdʒaːmʕa. (3) Otherwise stress the antepenult: ˈmadrasa, ˈʃadʒara. Never stress further back than the antepenult. Case endings are not pronounced (see pausal_forms), so they never count in stress assignment. Lexicalised adverbial -an counts as an ordinary closed syllable: ˈʃukran, ˈtˤabʕan. Long vowels make a syllable heavy wherever they surface. Word-final long vowels are written short, so a final open syllable is never stressed by length: ˈmaʃa.

Source: Holes, C. (1990). Gulf Arabic (Routledge Descriptive Grammars), phonology chapter, section on word stress. Cross-checked with Watson, J. C. E. (2002). The Phonology and Morphology of Arabic, OUP, chapter on stress (Eastern/Gulf pattern). Bukshaisha (1985, PhD Edinburgh) describes the same pattern for Qatari.

## Stressed and unstressed words

These words are unstressed and carry no ˈ mark, even when they have two syllables: the article il-/l- (always part of its host word); the proclitics b(i)-, l(i)-, w(a)-, f(i)-; independent prepositions fi, min, ʕan, maʕ, ʕala, ila, leːn; conjunctions w, aw, bas, inn, lo; the relative illi; verbal negation ma; future/aspect particles; auxiliary kaːn when it precedes a main verb. Write them as, for example, ʕala lbeːt, min ilbeːt, illi ˈʃifta. These words are stressed and marked by the stress rule when they are polysyllabic: independent pronouns (ˈana, ˈinta, ˈhuwa, ˈhumma), demonstratives (ˈhaːða, ˈhaːði), interrogatives (ˈweːn, ˈʃloːn, ˈmenu), the predicate negator mub/mu, and kaːn used as a full verb. Pronoun suffixes (-ah, -ha, -na, -kum, -hum, -itʃ, -ik) are part of their host word, and they shift stress only through the stress rule: ˈkitaːbah but kitaːˈbitʃ? Do not use that last form; write ktaːbitʃ with stress by the rule, ˈktaːbitʃ.

Source: Holes, C. (1990). Gulf Arabic, chapters on pronouns, particles and the article. The stressed/unstressed split follows the general Arabic dialect treatment in Watson (2002), chapter on stress (clitics fall outside the stress domain).

## Pausal forms

Qatari has no case inflection, so the same reduced form is written everywhere: at sentence end, at a comma, and in sentence-medial connected speech alike. Do not write the final short case vowels -u, -a, -i or the nunation -un, -an, -in, even if the tashkeel marks them: kitaːbun → ktaːb, fi lbeːti → fi lbeːt. The exception is lexicalised adverbs in -an, which keep -an everywhere: ˈʃukran, ˈabadan, ˈtˤabʕan. Tāʼ marbūṭa is written -a in absolute form (maˈdiːna, ˈsayyaːra). It is written -at before a following genitive or pronoun suffix (maˈdiːnat iddooħa, sayˈyaːratah). Word-final long vowels are written short in every position: ˈmaʃa, ˈʃuːfi. There is no separate pausal rule at commas or sentence end beyond this.

Source: Holes, C. (1990). Gulf Arabic, phonology and nominal morphology chapters (absence of case and tanwīn, feminine -a/-at, final vowel shortening). Johnstone, T. M. (1967). Eastern Arabian Dialect Studies, OUP (Qatar sections).

## Connected speech

Each word keeps its own place, and only these processes are written. (1) Article and hamzat al-waṣl alternation. A word-initial i- of the article or of a waṣl form is dropped when the preceding word in the same phrase ends in a vowel: ʕala lbeːt, fi ssuːg. It is kept after a consonant-final word or after a pause: min ilbeːt, ilbeːt ˈkibiːr. (2) The article assimilates to a following coronal (t d θ ð tˤ ðˤ s z sˤ ʃ n l r) inside its own word: iʃʃams, issuːg, innaːs. It does not assimilate to dʒ or to tʃ from affrication: ildʒaːmʕa, iltʃalb. Do not write any other assimilation across word boundaries. That excludes n-to-labial/velar place assimilation, voicing assimilation, and gemination across words. Do not write linking consonants or resyllabification symbols. Write no hyphens or ties inside or between IPA words.

Source: Holes, C. (1990). Gulf Arabic, phonology chapter (article allomorphy, elision of initial i- after vowels, sun-letter assimilation). Watson (2002), chapter on syllable structure, for the waṣl/epenthesis treatment.

## Glides and diphthongs

Consonantal glides are written j and w, never i̯ or u̯; geminate glides are written jj and ww: ˈsajjaːra, ˈgawwa. The old diphthongs *ay and *aw are written as the monophthongs eː and oː in native vocabulary: beːt, leːn, joːm, ʃoːf. Write aj and aw only in learned or Classical words where the diphthong is kept, and before a geminate glide or a vowel: ˈdawla is written ˈdoːla in colloquial use, so prefer the monophthong unless the word is clearly learned. Long high vowels are iː and uː, never ij or uw.

Source: Holes, C. (1990). Gulf Arabic, phonology chapter (monophthongisation of *ay, *aw in Gulf dialects including Qatar). Johnstone (1967), Qatari sections.

## Other choices

### velar affrication

Write /k/ as [tʃ] and /ɡ/ as [dʒ] only in a stem, and only adjacent to /i/ or /iː/: diːtʃ 'rooster', tʃibiːr 'big', tʃiðiː 'like this'. Do not affricate next to a non-high vowel (kalb is ˈkalb, not tʃalb), next to an emphatic, or near a similar coronal (OCP). Do not affricate in verbs, participles, verbal nouns or broken plurals: katab, kaːtib, kitaːba, kutub stay with k. This follows the published account rather than the base rule, which the specification notes does not model these blocks. The 2nd person feminine singular suffix is written -itʃ: ktaːbitʃ, ʕaleːtʃ.

Source: Mustafawi, E. (2006). An Optimality Theoretic Approach to Variable Consonantal Alternations in Qatari Arabic, PhD, Univ. of Ottawa; and Mustafawi (2007), both as reported in Alshammari (2026), p.1335. The -itʃ suffix follows Holes (1990).

### qāf, jīm, ḍād and interdentals

Write *q as ɡ in native words (ɡaːl, ɡalb, suːɡ) and as q in learned or religious words (qurʔaːn, qaːnuːn). Write *ǧ as dʒ (dʒaːmʕa, raːdʒil), not the Bedouin j variant. Retain the interdentals θ, ð and ðˤ: θalaːθ, haːða. Write both ض and ظ as ðˤ: ðˤaːbitˤ, ˈðˤuhur.

Source: Holes (1990), phonology chapter. Al-Balushi (2016), phonology sections, for Eastern Arabian interdental and emphatic retention.

### hamza

Do not write ʔ word-initially; vowel-initial words start with the vowel: ana, ummi. Medial and final hamza in native words are dropped, with lengthening of the vowel: raːs, biːr, ʃaːf, maːj. Write ʔ only in learned words where the hamza is pronounced: ˈmasʔala, suˈʔaːl.

Source: Holes (1990), phonology chapter (loss of hamza in Gulf dialects).

### short vowels

Use the vowel inventory a i u aː iː uː eː oː. Write the short vowels the tashkeel gives, with sukūn meaning no vowel. In native words, drop a short high vowel in an open unstressed syllable before CV(ː), the Bedouin-type syncope: kitaːb → ktaːb, kabiːr → tʃibiːr (with i). Do not add vowel raising (imāla) of final -a; it stays a.

Source: Holes (1990), phonology chapter on syncope. Johnstone (1967) for the stronger Bedouin character of Qatari.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
