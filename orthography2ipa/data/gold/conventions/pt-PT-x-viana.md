# Transcription conventions: Alto-Minhoto Portuguese (pt-PT-x-viana)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Primary stress is lexical, as in all European Portuguese. Write ˈ before the stressed syllable of every lexically stressed word, monosyllables included: pão [ˈpɐ̃w̃], vez [ˈbes]. Do not write ˈ on the unstressed function words listed under function_words. Do not mark secondary stress. Place ˈ before the whole syllable onset, clusters included: outro [ˈowtɾu], prato [ˈpɾatu]. A coda consonant stays with the preceding syllable: festa [ˈfɛʃtɐ]. Place stress from the spelling. (1) A written acute or circumflex marks the stressed vowel: café [kɐˈfɛ], fácil [ˈfasiɫ], você [boˈse]. (2) Otherwise, words ending in a, e, o, am, em or ens, with or without a final s, stress the penult: casa [ˈkaz̺ɐ], cidade [siˈdadɨ], falam [ˈfalɐ̃w̃]. (3) All other words stress the final syllable. These are words ending in i, u, l, r, z, x, a consonant, ã, ão, im, um, om or a diphthong, with or without a final s: falar [fɐˈlaɾ], rapaz [rɐˈpas], papel [pɐˈpɛɫ], irmão [iɾˈmɐ̃w̃]. A written tilde does not itself mark stress, so órfão is stressed on the first syllable by rule (1). The stressed vowel takes its full quality and every unstressed vowel is reduced, as under other. Portuguese has no phonemic long vowels, so length is never written. It has no geminates: ⟨ss⟩ and ⟨rr⟩ are spellings of single consonants, as in passo [ˈpas̺u] and carro [ˈkaru]. It has no case endings.

Source: Mateus & d'Andrade (2000), The Phonology of Portuguese, ch. 6 on word stress. The spelling rules restate the standard orthographic stress rules, which hold unchanged for the Northern dialects (Cintra 1971 lists no stress isogloss).

## Stressed and unstressed words

These words are unstressed. They take no ˈ and are written with reduced vowels. Definite articles: o [u], a [ɐ], os [us̺], as [ɐs̺], and the enclitic forms lo, la, los, las [lu lɐ lus̺ lɐs̺]. Monosyllabic prepositions and their contractions with articles: a [ɐ], à [a], ao [aw], aos [aws̺], de [dɨ], do [du], da [dɐ], dos [dus̺], das [dɐs̺], em [ɐ̃j̃], no [nu], na [nɐ], nos [nus̺], nas [nɐs̺], num [nũ], por [puɾ], com [kõ], sem [s̺ɐ̃j̃]. Also para [pɐɾɐ], pelo [pɨlu], pela [pɨlɐ], pelos [pɨlus̺], pelas [pɨlɐs̺]. Conjunctions and complementisers: e [i], ou [ow], que [kɨ], se [s̺ɨ], mas [mɐs̺], nem [nɐ̃j̃], the relative que [kɨ]. Clitic object pronouns, whether free or joined by a hyphen: me [mɨ], te [tɨ], se [s̺ɨ], lhe [ʎɨ], lhes [ʎɨs̺], nos [nus̺], vos [bus̺], o [u], a [ɐ], os [us̺], as [ɐs̺]. Example: chama-se [ˈtʃɐmɐ s̺ɨ]. All other words are stressed and take ˈ. This includes um [ˈũ], uma [ˈumɐ], numa [ˈnumɐ], numerals, demonstratives (este [ˈeʃtɨ]), possessives (meu [ˈmew]), subject pronouns (eu [ˈew], ele [ˈelɨ], nós [ˈnɔs̺]), and prepositional pronouns (mim [ˈmĩ], si [ˈs̺i]). It includes all verb forms, auxiliaries among them: é [ˈɛ], foi [ˈfoj], tem [ˈtɐ̃j̃], está [ɨʃˈta], ser [ˈs̺eɾ]. It includes não [ˈnɐ̃w̃], the interrogatives and adverbs quando [ˈkwɐ̃du], como [ˈkomu], onde [ˈõdɨ], and all nouns, adjectives and adverbs.

Source: Mateus & d'Andrade (2000) on clitics and the prosodic word (ch. 6). The reduced forms follow their unstressed-vowel reduction rules (ch. 2). The apical [s̺] in se, sem, os and similar words follows Cintra (1971:93, note 29): 's inicial e final'.

## Pausal forms

Does not apply: Portuguese has no pausal morphology, final short case vowels or tanwīn. A pause still conditions the word-final sibilant, as set out under connected_speech. A pause is the end of the sentence or any punctuation mark (comma, colon, semicolon, dash, quotation mark, bracket). A word before a pause has its citation form: os [us̺], luz [ˈlus].

Source: Mateus & d'Andrade (2000) on sandhi of word-final fricatives. Cintra (1971:93, note 29) on final [s̺].

## Connected speech

Only two processes are written across word boundaries, and each changes only the word on its own side. Both apply within a phrase, including across hyphens. Neither applies across a pause as defined under pausal_forms.
(1) Voicing of a word-final sibilant. Final ⟨s⟩ is apical. It is [s̺] before a pause or a voiceless consonant: os pés [us̺ ˈpɛs̺]. It is [z̺] before a vowel or a voiced consonant: os olhos [uz̺ ˈɔʎuz̺], mais nada [ˈmajz̺ ˈnadɐ]. Final ⟨z⟩, and final ⟨x⟩ when pronounced as a sibilant, are laminal. They are [s] before a pause or a voiceless consonant: vez [ˈbes], faz tudo [ˈfas ˈtudu]. They are [z] before a vowel or a voiced consonant: faz isso [ˈfaz ˈis̺u]. The Lisbon palatal [ʃ ʒ] is not used word-finally.
(2) Word-final ⟨l⟩ is [ɫ] before a pause or a consonant: mal [ˈmaɫ]. It is [l] before a vowel-initial word: mal amado [ˈmal ɐˈmadu].
Nothing else is written. There is no vowel elision, crasis, glide formation or nasal assimilation across words, and each word keeps its own vowels: de amor [dɨ ɐˈmoɾ], o homem [u ˈɔmɐ̃j̃]. Word-final ⟨r⟩ stays [ɾ] in all positions.

Source: Mateus & d'Andrade (2000) on external sandhi of /s/ and coda /l/ (chs. 2 and 3). Their [ʃ ʒ] are replaced by the apical [s̺ z̺] that Cintra (1971:93, note 29) gives for final ⟨s⟩ in the transmontano-alto-minhoto group. Álvarez Pérez (2014:36-38, §4) gives ⟨z⟩ as the laminal series. Choosing apical rather than palatal final ⟨s⟩ follows Cintra against the inherited Lisbon sandhi of the specification.

## Glides and diphthongs

Write non-syllabic vowels as the consonant symbols j and w, never as i̯ or u̯. Falling diphthongs: pai [ˈpaj], meu [ˈmew], rei [ˈrej], dois [ˈdojs̺], outro [ˈowtɾu]. Nasal diphthongs carry a tilde on both elements: mão [ˈmɐ̃w̃], mãe [ˈmɐ̃j̃], põe [ˈpõj̃], muito [ˈmũj̃tu]. Final ⟨-em⟩ and ⟨-am⟩ are written [ɐ̃j̃] and [ɐ̃w̃]: tem [ˈtɐ̃j̃], falam [ˈfalɐ̃w̃]. An unstressed ⟨i⟩ or ⟨u⟩ next to a vowel in the same word is written as a glide: água [ˈaɡwɐ], história [iʃˈtɔɾjɐ], quatro [ˈkwatɾu]. A stressed ⟨í⟩ or ⟨ú⟩, or any stressed i or u in hiatus, stays syllabic: saída [s̺ɐˈidɐ], país [pɐˈis̺]. Nasal vowels are written with a tilde and no following nasal consonant: campo [ˈkɐ̃pu], tempo [ˈtẽpu], fim [ˈfĩ].

Source: Mateus & d'Andrade (2000, ch. 3) on glides and nasal diphthongs. On ⟨ei⟩ and ⟨ou⟩, see other.

## Other choices

### Sibilants (four-sibilant system)

Two series contrast. The apical series is written s̺ z̺. ⟨s⟩ at the start of a word, after a consonant, or at the end of a word is [s̺]: sol [ˈs̺ɔɫ], pensar [pẽˈs̺aɾ]. ⟨ss⟩ is [s̺]: passo [ˈpas̺u]. Intervocalic ⟨s⟩ is [z̺]: casa [ˈkaz̺ɐ], coser [kuˈz̺eɾ]. The laminal series is written plain s z. ⟨c⟩ before e or i, and ⟨ç⟩, are [s]: paço [ˈpasu], cidade [siˈdadɨ]. ⟨z⟩ is [z]: cozer [kuˈzeɾ]. Intervocalic ⟨x⟩ pronounced [z] is laminal: exame [iˈzɐmɨ]. The retroflex-like [ʂ ʐ] surface variant is not written. Word-internally before a consonant, ⟨s⟩, ⟨z⟩ and ⟨x⟩ are [ʃ] before a voiceless consonant and [ʒ] before a voiced one: festa [ˈfɛʃtɐ], mesmo [ˈmeʒmu]. Word-final position is covered under connected_speech.

Source: Cintra (1971:93 and note 29). Álvarez Pérez (2014:36-38, §4).

### ⟨ch⟩ versus ⟨x⟩

⟨ch⟩ is the affricate [tʃ] and ⟨x⟩ is [ʃ]; they do not merge: chave [ˈtʃabɨ], chamar [tʃɐˈmaɾ], xaile [ˈʃajlɨ], caixa [ˈkajʃɐ]. ⟨j⟩, and ⟨g⟩ before e or i, are [ʒ]: já [ˈʒa].

Source: Cintra (1971:87), trait 3.

### Betacism

⟨v⟩ and ⟨b⟩ are one phoneme, written [b] in every position. The fricative allophone [β] is not written: vinho [ˈbiɲu], vez [ˈbes], uva [ˈubɐ]. The specification notes do not list betacism among the modelled deltas, but their own example chave [ˈtʃabɨ] shows it, and Cintra places all Northern dialects, Alto-Minho among them, inside the betacist area.

Source: Cintra (1971:87), trait 1.

### Diphthongs ⟨ei⟩ and ⟨ou⟩

⟨ei⟩ keeps the Northern [ej], not Lisbon [ɐj]: leite [ˈlejtɨ], primeiro [pɾiˈmejɾu]. ⟨ou⟩ keeps the diphthong [ow], not the standard monophthong [o]: ouro [ˈowɾu], pouco [ˈpowku], ou [ow].

Source: Cintra (1971), the North versus Centre-South diphthong isoglosses, where monophthongisation characterises the centro-meridional dialects. This departs from the Lisbon norm in Mateus & d'Andrade (2000, ch. 3).

### Strong r

⟨rr⟩, word-initial ⟨r⟩, and ⟨r⟩ after n, l or s are the apical trill [r], not the Lisbon uvular [ʁ]: rato [ˈratu], carro [ˈkaru], honra [ˈõrɐ]. All other ⟨r⟩ is the tap [ɾ]: caro [ˈkaɾu], mar [ˈmaɾ]. This departs from the inherited Lisbon base of the specification, following the conservative Northern pronunciation.

Source: Mateus & d'Andrade (2000, ch. 1-2), on the alveolar versus uvular realisation of the strong r and its regional distribution.

### Vowel reduction and lateral

The standard European Portuguese reduction is inherited. Unstressed ⟨a⟩ is [ɐ], unstressed ⟨e⟩ is [ɨ], and unstressed ⟨o⟩ is [u]: pesado [pɨˈz̺adu]. Initial unstressed ⟨e⟩ before s plus a consonant is [i]: estar [iʃˈtaɾ]. Initial unstressed ⟨e⟩ elsewhere is [i]: exame [iˈzɐmɨ]. Stressed ⟨a⟩ before a nasal consonant is [ɐ]: cama [ˈkɐmɐ]. Final unstressed ⟨-e⟩ is always written [ɨ], never dropped. Porto-type diphthongisation of stressed [e o] to [je wo] is not applied. Onset ⟨l⟩ is [l]. Coda ⟨l⟩ is [ɫ]: alto [ˈaɫtu].

Source: Mateus & d'Andrade (2000, ch. 2). Cintra (1971:93) excludes the Baixo-Minho and Douro diphthongisation from this group.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
