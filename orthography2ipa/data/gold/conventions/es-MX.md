# Transcription conventions: Mexican Spanish (Highland) (es-MX)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark primary stress with ˈ before the stressed syllable of every stressed word, monosyllables included: [ˈsol], [ˈbjen], [ˈkasa]. No syllable dots. Placement follows the spelling. A written accent marks the stressed vowel: [kaˈfe], [ˈarβol], [ˈmusika]. Without an accent, words ending in a vowel, n or s stress the penult: [ˈkasa], [ˈkantan], [ˈlunes]. Words ending in any other consonant stress the last syllable: [aˈβlaɾ], [sjuˈðað]. Enclitics written solid with the verb keep the host's stress: dámelo [ˈdamelo], diciéndole [diˈsjendole]. Adverbs in -mente carry two stresses, written ˌ on the adjective and ˈ on -men-: rápidamente [ˌrapiðaˈmente]. Compounds written as one word take a single ˈ on the last element: sacapuntas [sakaˈpuntas]. Spanish has no contrastive vowel length, no geminates and no case endings, so those parts of the topic do not apply. Never write ː.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 12 (word stress; -mente words with two stresses); Navarro Tomás, Manual de pronunciación española (1918 and later editions), on accentuation.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Unstressed, so no ˈ mark: (1) definite articles el la lo los las; (2) prenominal possessives mi tu su nuestro and their plurals; (3) object and reflexive clitics me te se lo la le nos los las les; (4) all simple prepositions except según: a de en con por para sin sobre hasta desde entre hacia contra; (5) conjunctions y e o u ni que pero mas sino si (conditional) porque aunque; (6) unaccented relatives and subordinators que quien cual donde como cuando; (7) tan before an adjective. Examples: [la ˈkasa], [de ˈmi ˈpaðɾe], [me lo ˈðio], [ke ˈβjene]. Stressed, so marked with ˈ: (1) indefinite articles: un [ˈun], una [ˈuna]; (2) demonstratives; (3) tonic possessives: mío [ˈmio], suyo [ˈsuʝo]; (4) subject pronouns: yo [ˈʝo], tú [ˈtu], él [ˈel], usted [usˈteð]; (5) pronouns after prepositions: mí [ˈmi], ti [ˈti]; (6) numerals; (7) según [seˈɣun]; (8) accented interrogatives and exclamatives: qué [ˈke], dónde [ˈdonde], cómo [ˈkomo]; (9) muy [ˈmuj]; (10) all verb forms, auxiliaries included: ha hablado [ˈa aˈβlaðo], está comiendo [esˈta koˈmjendo], es [ˈes]. Stressed monosyllables keep their ˈ even when unaccented in spelling.

Source: Quilis, Tratado de fonología y fonética españolas (1993), lists of tonic and atonic words (verbs, auxiliaries included, are tonic; un/una tonic); Hualde 2005, ch. 12 (unstressed function words).

## Pausal forms

Spanish has no pausal forms, case endings or nunation (tanwīn), so nothing changes at the end of a sentence or at a comma. The note's distinctive Mexico City devoicing or loss of unstressed vowels, especially next to /s/ and before pause, is variable and allophonic, so it is not written. Write the full voiced vowel: pues [ˈpwes], entonces [enˈtonses], noches [ˈnotʃes] (never [ˈnotʃe̥s] or [ˈnotʃs]). Final /s/ is always kept as [s] before pause, with no aspiration or loss. Pause matters only as a left environment: after a pause, at the start of the sentence or after a comma, /b d ɡ ʝ/ are stops or affricates: [ˈbamos], [ˈdime], [ˈɡɾasjas], [ˈɟʝa].

Source: Lope Blanch, 'En torno a las vocales caedizas del español mexicano' (1963, reprinted in Estudios sobre el español de México, UNAM 1972); Hualde 2005, ch. 8 (stops after pause); Lipski 1994, Mexico chapter (coda /s/ retained).

## Connected speech

Each word is written in its own slot. Each of the following processes changes the segment at the boundary only. (1) Spirantization. /b d ɡ/ are [β ð ɣ] after a vowel or any consonant, both inside a word and across a boundary: [la ˈβoka], [de ˈɣato], [ˈpoɾ ˈðonde]. They are [b d ɡ] after pause and after a nasal: [um ˈbeso], [en ˈdonde], [eŋ ˈɡeneɾal]. /d/ is also [d] after /l/: [el ˈdeðo]. After /l/, /b ɡ/ stay approximants: [el ˈβote], [el ˈɣato]. Coda /b d ɡ/ are written [β ð ɣ]: [aβsoˈluto], [sjuˈðað]. (2) Nasal place assimilation. Word-final /n/ is [m] before /p b m/ and [ŋ] before /k ɡ x/. Before all other consonants and before vowels it stays [n], also inside words: [um ˈpoko], [eŋ ˈkasa], [ˈkampo], [ˈteŋɡo], [en ˈfin]. Dental and palatal nasal allophones and [ɱ] are not written. (3) /s/ voicing. /s/ is written [z] before a voiced consonant in the same or the next word: [ˈmizmo], [loz ˈðeðos], [laz ˈmanos]. Otherwise it is [s]. (4) Rhotics. Word-initial r, and r after n, l or s, is [r]: [la ˈrosa], [onˈraðo]. A single r is otherwise [ɾ], including word-finally before a vowel: [ˈpoɾ ˈeso]. (5) /ʝ/ follows (1): see other/yeísmo. Not written: synalepha, vowel merger, gliding of vowels across words, and resyllabification. Each word keeps all its vowels: [la aˈmiɣa], [ˈesto ˈes], [ˈmi ˈixo]. The conjunction y is always [i]: [i ˈel].

Source: Hualde 2005, chs. 8–10 (spirantization, nasal assimilation, /s/ voicing, rhotics), following Navarro Tomás' allophonic rules.

## Glides and diphthongs

Write non-syllabic high vowels in diphthongs as [j] and [w], both before and after the nucleus, never [i̯ u̯]: bien [ˈbjen], bueno [ˈbweno], aire [ˈajɾe], rey [ˈrej], causa [ˈkawsa], ciudad [sjuˈðað], muy [ˈmuj]. This departs from Hualde 2005, which writes postnuclear glides as [i̯ u̯]; [j w] is chosen here for one symbol per glide. A written accent on i or u marks hiatus: día [ˈdia], país [paˈis], baúl [baˈul]. Sequences of mid and low vowels stay in hiatus as spelled: teatro [teˈatɾo], poeta [poˈeta]. The Mexican tendency to glide them ([ˈtjatɾo]) is not written. Word-initial hie-/hi+vowel is the consonant [ʝ]: hielo [ˈʝelo]. Word-initial hue- is [w], without [ɣw]: hueso [ˈweso].

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 9 (glides and diphthongs; postnuclear [i̯ u̯] there, replaced here by [j w]); Navarro Tomás (1918), on diphthongs and hiatus.

## Other choices

### yeísmo and /ʝ/

ll and consonantal y are one phoneme /ʝ/. It is written [ʝ] in spirantizing contexts (after vowels and after consonants other than nasals and /l/): [ˈkaʝe], [ˈʝo] after a vowel. It is written [ɟʝ] after pause, a nasal or /l/: [kon ˈɟʝuβja], [el ˈɟʝaβe]. The note's weak approximant [ʝ̞] is written [ʝ] without the lowering diacritic, matching [β ð ɣ], which are also written without it.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 10 (palatal obstruent allophones); Lipski 1994, Mexico chapter (weak intervocalic /ʝ/ in the central highlands).

### seseo, velar /x/, ch, ñ, h

c before e/i and z are [s]: [ˈsjelo], [ˈkasa] (caza). j and g before e/i are velar [x], never [h]: [ˈxente], [ˈoxo]. Mexican x in México, Oaxaca and Texas is [x]: [ˈmexiko], [waˈxaka]. ch is [tʃ]: [ˈmutʃo]. ñ is [ɲ]: [ˈaɲo]. h is silent: [ˈora] (hora).

Source: Lope Blanch 1972; Lipski 1994, Mexico chapter; Hualde 2005, ch. 10.

### letter x elsewhere and Nahuatl words

Intervocalic and coda x in ordinary words is [ks]: examen [ekˈsamen], texto [ˈteksto]. Initial x is [s]: xilófono [siˈlofono]. Nahuatl names follow the established Mexico City pronunciation: Xochimilco [sotʃiˈmilko], Xola [ˈʃola]. tl is written [tl]: [ˈatlas], [tlaˈlok].

Source: Lope Blanch 1972; Lipski 1994, Mexico chapter.

### assibilated /r/ and final /ɾ/

The Mexico City assibilated or devoiced final /ɾ/ is not written. Write [ɾ] for word-final r: [aˈβlaɾ], [ˈmaɾ].

Source: Lipski 1994, Mexico chapter (assibilation noted as variable).

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
