# Transcription conventions: Colombian Spanish (Coastal) (es-CO-x-costa)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Spanish lexical stress, taken from the spelling. A word with a written accent is stressed on the accented vowel. Otherwise a word ending in a vowel, -n or -s is stressed on the penult, and any other word on the final syllable. Write ˈ before the onset of the stressed syllable in every stressed word of two or more syllables: casa [ˈkasa], comer [koˈmeɾ], árbol [ˈaɾβol], camión [kaˈmjoŋ]. Stressed monosyllables take no mark: sol [sol], más [mah]. Unstressed function words take no mark (see function_words). The coastal deletion of final -s and -d never moves the stress: después [dehˈpwe], verdad [beɾˈða], ciudad [sjuˈða]. Adverbs in -mente keep the adjective's stress as secondary ˌ, with primary stress on -men-: fácilmente [ˌfasilˈmente], rápidamente [ˌrapiðaˈmente]. Clitic pronouns written as separate words never change the verb's stress. Long vowels, geminates and case endings do not apply: Spanish has no phonemic length, no geminates and no case.

Source: Hualde 2005, The Sounds of Spanish, chapter on word stress (accentual rules and the written accent; two stresses in -mente adverbs).

## Stressed and unstressed words

Unstressed, with no ˈ even when polysyllabic: definite articles el la lo los las; object and reflexive clitics me te se nos os lo la le los las les; all simple prepositions except según (a, de, en, con, por, para [paɾa], desde [dehðe], hasta [ahta], sobre, entre, hacia); conjunctions y o pero ni que si porque aunque como cuando mientras; relatives que quien donde cuando como cuyo (donde [donde]); prenominal possessives mi tu su mis tus sus. Stressed: indefinite articles un una unos unas (una [ˈuna]), numerals, según [seˈɣun], subject and prepositional pronouns (nosotros [noˈsotɾoh], él, mí), demonstratives, quantifiers, interrogatives and exclamatives with a written accent (dónde [ˈdonde]), nuestro/vuestro, postnominal possessives (mío [ˈmio]), and all verb forms including auxiliaries and copulas (había [aˈβia], estaba [ehˈtaβa]). Always write the full spelled form; never write colloquial reductions such as pa for para.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on word stress (the list of stressed versus unstressed words); Quilis-style tradition as summarised there.

## Pausal forms

There are no special pausal forms, case endings or nunation; tanwīn and final-vowel loss do not apply. Final vowels are never dropped. Every word gets the same form at a comma, at the end of a sentence and phrase-internally. The only exception is the rules under connected_speech that depend on what follows: /n/ and the voiced stops before a pause. Word-final /s/ is [h] in every position, including before a pause: las casas [lah ˈkasah]. Word-final /d/ is deleted: usted [uhˈte]. Word-final /n/ before a pause is [ŋ]: pan [paŋ].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapters on /s/ aspiration, /n/ velarisation and dialect variation (Caribbean); Fontanella de Weinberg 1992, on the Caribbean and Colombian coastal zone.

## Connected speech

Each word keeps its own segments in its own slot; nothing is joined and nothing moves to the next word. Resyllabification is not written. Three processes are written on the affected word. (1) Voiced obstruents: word-initial /b d g/ are [b d g] after a pause and after a nasal, and /d/ is also [d] after /l/. Elsewhere, including after a vowel-final word or after [h] [ɾ] [l], they are [β ð ɣ]: la boca [la ˈβoka], los dedos [loh ˈðeðoh], un gato [uŋ ˈgato], el dedo [el ˈdeðo]. A sentence-initial word and a word after a comma count as post-pausal. The same rule applies inside words. (2) Word-final /n/: [m] before p b m, [ŋ] before k g x and before a vowel or pause, and [n] before all other consonants: un poco [um ˈpoko], en casa [eŋ ˈkasa], con usted [koŋ uhˈte], un día [un ˈdia]. (3) Word-final /s/ is [h] before a consonant, a vowel and a pause alike: los amigos [loh aˈmiɣoh], es tarde [eh ˈtaɾðe]. Across word boundaries there is no vowel elision, no merger of identical vowels and no glide formation: la escuela [la ehˈkwela], mi amigo [mi aˈmiɣo], de el [de el]. Word-initial r is [r] after any word: los reyes [loh ˈreʝeh].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapters on the voiced obstruents (spirantisation), nasal assimilation and velarisation, /s/ weakening, and synalepha; Fontanella de Weinberg 1992, on Caribbean prevocalic aspiration.

## Glides and diphthongs

Write a non-syllabic i/y and u as [j] and [w], in both rising and falling diphthongs; never write i̯ u̯. Examples: bien [bjen], cuando [ˈkwando], aire [ˈajɾe], causa [ˈkawsa], hoy [oj], muy [muj], rey [rej], ciudad [sjuˈða]. Any unaccented i or u next to another vowel in the same word is a glide, including in triphthongs: buey [bwej]. A written accent on í or ú marks hiatus, and both vowels are written as syllabic: día [ˈdia], país [paˈih], baúl [baˈul]. Sequences of e, a and o are always in hiatus and are never glided: teatro [teˈatɾo], poeta [poˈeta]. Initial hu+vowel is [w]: huevo [ˈweβo]. Initial hi+vowel is [ʝ]: hielo [ˈʝelo]. The conjunction y is [i]. Consonantal y and ll are [ʝ], not [j] (see other).

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on diphthongs and hiatus (and his use of [j w] for glides versus [ʝ] for the palatal obstruent).

## Other choices

### seseo

s, z, and c before e/i are all /s/. Write [s] in an onset; in a coda, the aspiration rule applies: cielo [ˈsjelo], zapato [saˈpato], hace [ˈase], diez [djeh]. Never write θ.

Source: Hualde 2005, on seseo in Latin American Spanish; Penny 2002, on the history of seseo.

### coda /s/ inside the word

A syllable-final /s/ inside a word, or x before a consonant, is [h]: mismo [ˈmihmo], este [ˈehte], explicar [ehpliˈkaɾ]. Intervocalic x is [ks]: examen [ekˈsameŋ]. Never delete a coda /s/; always write [h], even though published descriptions also report zero.

Source: Fontanella de Weinberg 1992, on the Caribbean and Colombian coast; Hualde 2005, on /s/ aspiration.

### velar fricative

j, and g before e/i, are glottal [h]; never write x: gente [ˈhente], trabajo [tɾaˈβaho], México [ˈmehiko].

Source: Hualde 2005, on dialectal [h] for /x/ in Caribbean Spanish; Fontanella de Weinberg 1992.

### yeísmo

ll and consonantal y are one phoneme, written [ʝ] in every position (including after n, l and pause): calle [ˈkaʝe], yo [ʝo], llamar [ʝaˈmaɾ], con yeso [koɲ ˈʝeso] is NOT used; write con yeso [kon ˈʝeso]. ñ is [ɲ], ch is [tʃ].

Source: Hualde 2005, on yeísmo and the palatal obstruent.

### liquids

Coda /ɾ/ and /l/ are written as spelled ([ɾ], [l]), and word-final -r is kept: puerta [ˈpweɾta], comer [koˈmeɾ], alma [ˈalma]. This departs from the specification note: the published descriptions treat lambdacism, rhotacism and Cartagena-style gemination (puerta [ˈpwetta]) as variable and sociolinguistically marked, not categorical. rr, and r at the start of a word or after n, l or s, is [r]; other r is [ɾ]: perro [ˈpero], honra [ˈonra], Israel [ihraˈel].

Source: Hualde 2005, chapters on rhotics and liquids and on Caribbean variation; Fontanella de Weinberg 1992, on liquid neutralisation in the Caribbean.

### /d/ deletion

Delete word-final /d/ always: verdad [beɾˈða], Madrid [maˈðɾi]. Keep intervocalic /d/ as [ð], including in -ado/-ada/-ido: cansado [kanˈsaðo], comida [koˈmiða]. Keep coda /d/ before a consonant as [ð]: admirar [aðmiˈɾaɾ]. Although -ado → [ao] is widely reported, it is variable, so it is not written.

Source: Hualde 2005, on /d/ weakening; Fontanella de Weinberg 1992.

### /n/ inside the word

Word-internal coda n assimilates: [m] before p b m, [ŋ] before k g j, [n] elsewhere: campo [ˈkampo], tango [ˈtaŋgo], canto [ˈkanto], ángel [ˈaŋhel]. Word-final n follows connected_speech. The word-final [ŋ] before a vowel or pause is velarisation as Hualde describes it.

Source: Hualde 2005, chapter on nasals (assimilation and velarisation).

### b/v and h

b and v are one phoneme, [b] or [β] by the spirantisation rule: vino [ˈbino], la vaca [la ˈβaka]. h is silent: hablar [aˈβlaɾ].

Source: Hualde 2005, on the voiced obstruents; Penny 2002, on the b/v merger.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
