# Transcription conventions: Colombian Spanish (Bogotá) (es-CO)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Stress is lexical. Its place follows from the spelling under the RAE accent rules. Words ending in a vowel, -n or -s are stressed on the penult, and other words on the final syllable, unless a written accent marks another syllable. Write ˈ immediately before the stressed syllable, so before its onset consonant(s). Syllabify by Spanish rules: V.CV; obstruent+liquid clusters (pr, bl, tr, gl…) are onsets; other medial CC split as C.C. Examples: casa [ˈkasa], hablar [aˈβlaɾ], árbol [ˈarbol], canción [kanˈsjon], teléfono [teˈlefono]. Mark ˈ on every stressed word, including stressed monosyllables: sol [ˈsol], mí [ˈmi], es [ˈes]. Unstressed words (see function_words) get no mark. Enclitics written solid with the verb do not move its stress: dámelo [ˈdamelo], diciéndole [diˈsjendole]. Adverbs in -mente keep two stresses; mark the adjective's stress as secondary ˌ and mente as primary: rápidamente [ˌrapiðaˈmente]. Long vowels: Spanish has no phonemic length; never write ː. Sequences of identical vowels are two syllables: leer [leˈeɾ], alcohol [alkoˈol], creer [kɾeˈeɾ]. Geminates: none, except a heterosyllabic nn written twice: innato [inˈnato]. Case endings: not applicable.

Source: Hualde (2005) The Sounds of Spanish, ch. 12 (word stress, -mente adverbs) and ch. 9 (syllabification). Accent rules as in RAE orthography.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Unstressed words get no ˈ. Their vowels stay full and unreduced. They are: definite articles el la los las lo; all simple prepositions except según (a, ante, con, de, desde, en, entre, hacia, hasta, para, por, sin, sobre, tras); the conjunctions y e o u ni que pero mas si porque aunque; unaccented relatives and conjunctive que quien cual cuando como donde cuanto; object and reflexive clitics me te se nos os lo la le los las les; prenominal possessives mi tu su mis tus sus; and the titles don doña san. Examples: el libro [el ˈliβɾo], de la casa [de la ˈkasa], me lo dijo [me lo ˈdiho], que viene [ke ˈβjene], con mi padre [kom mi ˈpaðɾe]. Stressed words get ˈ even when monosyllabic. They are: indefinite articles un una unos unas; demonstratives; subject and prepositional pronouns (yo tú él ella usted nosotros mí ti sí); stressed possessives (mío, nuestro, vuestro); interrogatives and exclamatives with an accent (qué cuál quién dónde cómo cuándo cuánto); numerals; según; negative no; adverbs; and every verb form, including auxiliaries and copulas (ha, he, es, está, fue, hay). Examples: un perro [ˈum ˈpero], ha venido [ˈa βeˈniðo], ¿qué es? [ˈke ˈes], no sé [ˈno ˈse].

Source: Hualde (2005) The Sounds of Spanish, ch. 12, section on stressed and unstressed words (palabras tónicas y átonas).

## Pausal forms

Spanish has no pausal forms. Final vowels and consonants are never dropped or changed at a pause. Case endings and nunation (tanwīn) do not exist in this lect. Pause matters only for the allophones of the next word. Treat the start of the sentence, and any position right after a punctuation mark (, ; : . ? ! ¿ ¡ parentheses, dashes, quotes), as post-pausal. There, /b d g/ are stops and /ʝ/ is [ɟʝ]. Example: Bueno, yo voy. [ˈbweno ˈɟʝo ˈβoi̯]: 'bueno' and 'yo' are post-pausal, while 'voy' follows the vowel of 'yo' and so takes [β]. Example: Gracias, Diego. [ˈgɾasjas ˈdjeɣo].

Source: Hualde (2005) The Sounds of Spanish, ch. 8 (allophones of /b d g/ and /ʝ/ after pause). No pausal morphology exists in any of the cited descriptions.

## Connected speech

Write within each word the allophonic processes that the neighbouring word conditions; never join or split words. (1) Nasal place assimilation: a word-final /n/ becomes [m] before /p b m/ and [ŋ] before /k g x/; it stays [n] elsewhere, including before a vowel and at a pause, with no velar [ŋ] word-finally: un beso [ˈum ˈbeso], en casa [eŋ ˈkasa], son ellos [ˈson ˈeʎos] → [ˈson ˈeɟʝos]? No: write [ˈson ˈeʝos]. (2) /b d g/ take their stop or approximant form from the last segment of the preceding word (see other: voiced obstruents): la boca [la ˈβoka], los dedos [los ˈdeðos], con gusto [koŋ ˈgusto]. (3) Word-initial /ʝ/ takes its form from the preceding word: el yeso [el ˈɟʝeso], la yuca [la ˈʝuka]. (4) Word-initial r is a trill [r] in every context: la rosa [la ˈrosa]. A word-final r before a vowel stays a tap: por eso [poɾ ˈeso]. Not written: resyllabification of a final consonant onto the next vowel (los otros [los ˈotɾos]); synalepha and vowel fusion across words, so both vowels stay full (mi amigo [mi aˈmiɣo], de este [de ˈeste], la otra [la ˈotɾa]); voicing of /s/ before voiced consonants (mismo [ˈmismo], las manos [las ˈmanos]); colloquial elisions such as para → pa.

Source: Hualde (2005) The Sounds of Spanish, ch. 8 (nasal assimilation, spirantization across word boundaries) and ch. 10 (synalepha); Fontanella de Weinberg (1992) on the conservative consonantism of the Colombian highlands.

## Glides and diphthongs

Write a prenuclear glide as [j w] and a postnuclear glide as [i̯ u̯]. Examples: bien [ˈbjen], cuatro [ˈkwatɾo], ciudad [sjuˈðað], cuidado [kwiˈðaðo], aire [ˈai̯ɾe], causa [ˈkau̯sa], hoy [ˈoi̯], muy [ˈmui̯], ley [ˈlei̯]. In iu and ui the second vowel is the nucleus, except final -uy, which is [ui̯]. These spellings give the consonant /ʝ/, not a glide: word-initial hie- and hia-, y before a vowel, and ll. Examples: hielo [ˈɟʝelo], ya [ˈɟʝa], mayo [ˈmaʝo]. Conjunction y is always [i]. An accented high vowel next to another vowel is a hiatus: día [ˈdia], país [paˈis], río [ˈrio]. Sequences of non-high vowels (ea, eo, oa, ae…) are always written as hiatus, even though colloquial speech may glide them: teatro [teˈatɾo], poeta [poˈeta], toalla [toˈaʝa]. Never write glides across word boundaries.

Source: Hualde (2005) The Sounds of Spanish, ch. 9 (diphthongs, hiatus, glide symbols [j w] vs [i̯ u̯]) and ch. 8 (consonantal /ʝ/).

## Other choices

### vowels

Write only the five vowels [a e i o u]. Do not reduce them in unstressed syllables, and do not write open or close allophones: pero [ˈpeɾo], doctor [dokˈtoɾ].

Source: Hualde (2005) The Sounds of Spanish, ch. 7.

### seseo and spelling

Write c before e and i, z, and s all as [s]: cielo [ˈsjelo], zapato [saˈpato]. The letter h is silent. Write b and v alike as /b/. Write qu and gu before e and i as [k] and [g], and gü as [gw]. Write x as [ks] (taxi [ˈtaksi]), except in México, Oaxaca and similar names, where it is [h] (México [ˈmehiko]).

Source: Penny (2002) A History of the Spanish Language, on the spread of seseo; Hualde (2005) ch. 6.

### voiced obstruents /b d g/

Write stops [b d g] in these positions: after a pause; after a nasal; after any consonant, including /s l r/; and after a glide. This is the Colombian highland pattern. Examples: [ˈalgo], [ˈdesde], [ˈbarba], [ˈdeu̯da], [los ˈdeðos]. Write approximants [β ð ɣ] only after a vowel, within a word or across a word boundary: [ˈlaβo], [ˈkaða], [ˈlaɣo], [la ˈβoka]. Word-final /d/ is [ð]: verdad [beɾˈðað].

Source: Hualde (2005) The Sounds of Spanish, ch. 8 (spirantization; Colombian and Central American stops after non-nasal consonants).

### /ʝ/ (yeísmo)

ll and y are the same phoneme, /ʝ/. Write [ɟʝ] after a pause, after /n/ and after /l/: yo [ˈɟʝo], cónyuge [ˈkonɟʝuxe] → [ˈkonɟʝuhe], el llanto [el ˈɟʝanto]. Write the fricative [ʝ] elsewhere, chiefly after a vowel: calle [ˈkaʝe], la llave [la ˈʝaβe]. This departs from the specification note, which asks for a 'strong [ɟʝ]' throughout. The published descriptions give the affricate only in these strengthening contexts.

Source: Hualde (2005) The Sounds of Spanish, ch. 8 (/ʝ/ allophones); Fontanella de Weinberg (1992) on yeísmo in Colombia.

### /x/

Write the letters j, and g before e and i, as the glottal fricative [h]: hijo [ˈiho], gente [ˈhente], México [ˈmehiko]. This is the Colombian norm. It does not conflict with the note's 'no aspiration', which is about coda /s/.

Source: Hualde (2005) The Sounds of Spanish, ch. 8 (glottal [h] for /x/ in Colombia, the Caribbean and Central America).

### rhotics

Write the trill [r] for word-initial r, for r after n, l or s, and for rr: rosa [ˈrosa], honra [ˈonra], perro [ˈpero]. Write the tap [ɾ] everywhere else, including in the coda: caro [ˈkaɾo], mar [ˈmaɾ]. Do not write the assibilated rhotic of some older Bogotá speakers.

Source: Hualde (2005) The Sounds of Spanish, ch. 8; Fontanella de Weinberg (1992), Andean Colombia section.

### coda /s/ and nasals

Coda /s/ is always written as full [s], never as [h] or zero: estos [ˈestos]. Inside a word, a nasal is [m] before bilabials and [ŋ] before velars (campo [ˈkampo], tengo [ˈteŋgo]). It is [n] before every other consonant, including f and dentals (enfermo [enˈfeɾmo], cantar [kanˈtaɾ]); do not write [ɱ] or a dental nasal.

Source: Fontanella de Weinberg (1992) El español de América, on the conservative highland consonantism; Hualde (2005) ch. 8.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
