# Transcription conventions: Paraguayan Spanish (es-PY)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Spanish has lexical stress. Mark primary stress with ˈ before the onset of the stressed syllable in every stressed word, including stressed monosyllables: sol [ˈsol], tú [ˈtu], vos [ˈbos]. Words in the unstressed list under function_words get no mark. Place stress by the spelling. An acute accent marks the stressed vowel: café [kaˈfe], teléfono [teˈlefono], país [paˈis]. Without an accent, a word ending in a vowel, n or s is stressed on the penult: casa [ˈkasa], hablan [ˈaβlan], voseo tenés [teˈnes]. Any other word is stressed on the last syllable: ciudad [sjuˈðað], árbol has an accent, so [ˈaɾβol]. Final y counts as a consonant: Paraguay [paɾaˈɣwai̯]. Adverbs in -mente keep the stress of the adjective as secondary ˌ and take primary stress on -men-: rápidamente [ˌrapiðaˈmente]. There are no phonemic long vowels. Never write ː. Two identical vowels are two syllables: leer [leˈeɾ], creer [kɾeˈeɾ]. There are no geminates except the rare -nn- and -bv-, written as spelled: innato [inˈnato]. Spanish has no case endings, so that point does not apply.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 13 (word stress) and its spelling-based stress rule; -mente double stress after Hualde 2005, ch. 13.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Unstressed, with no ˈ: definite articles el la lo los las; possessive determiners mi tu su mis tus sus; object and reflexive clitics me te se le les lo la los las nos os; all simple prepositions except según (a ante bajo con contra de desde en entre hacia hasta para por sin sobre tras); conjunctions y e o u ni que pero mas si (conditional) porque; relatives que quien cual cuyo donde cuando como without an accent; proclitic titles don doña san. Examples: el perro [el ˈpero], me lo dio [me lo ˈðjo], para mi casa [paɾa mi ˈkasa]. Stressed, with ˈ: indefinite articles un una unos unas [ˈun] [ˈuna]; demonstratives; nuestro/vuestro; subject and prepositional pronouns yo vos usted él ella mí ti sí [ˈʝo] [ˈbos] [usˈteð] [ˈmi]; interrogatives and exclamatives with an accent qué quién dónde cómo cuándo [ˈke]; según; numerals; adverbs, including no [ˈno]. All verb forms are stressed, including auxiliary haber and ser and estar: he visto [ˈe ˈβisto], ha sido [ˈa ˈsiðo].

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 13 (stressed vs. unstressed words), following Navarro Tomás's classification as summarised there.

## Pausal forms

Spanish has no pausal forms, short-vowel loss, case endings or nunation, so that part does not apply. A final word before a pause is written as it is anywhere else: casa [ˈkasa], usted [usˈteð], dos [ˈdos]. Coda /s/ stays [s]. Pause matters in one place only. A word-initial /b d ɡ/ is a stop [b d ɡ] at the start of a sentence and right after a comma, semicolon, colon, dash, parenthesis or quotation mark: Dame eso. [ˈdame ˈeso].

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 8 (the distribution of stops vs. approximants: stops after pause).

## Connected speech

Every word keeps its own place and its own syllables. Never write syllable dots, resyllabification, synalepha, vowel fusion or elision across words: la amiga [la aˈmiɣa], los otros [los ˈotɾos], de él [de ˈel]. Three processes are written on the word that they change. (1) Spirantization. /b d ɡ/ (spelled b v d g) are approximants [β ð ɣ] after any vowel or consonant, inside a word or across a word boundary: la boca [la ˈβoka], lo dio [lo ˈðjo], el gato [el ˈɣato]. They stay stops [b d ɡ] after a pause, after a nasal, and in the case of /d/ after /l/: un beso [um ˈbeso], el día [el ˈdia], cuando [ˈkwando]. (2) Nasal place assimilation. Coda n becomes [m] before p b v m and [ŋ] before k ɡ x, inside a word and across a word boundary: en Paraguay [em paɾaˈɣwai̯], un gato [uŋ ˈɡato], banco [ˈbaŋko], en casa [eŋ ˈkasa]. It stays [n] elsewhere, including before f: énfasis [ˈenfasis]. (3) Voicing of coda /s/. It becomes [z] before a voiced consonant (b d g m n l r y ll), inside a word and across a word boundary: mismo [ˈmizmo], los dedos [loz ˈðeðos], es mío [ˈez ˈmio]. It stays [s] before a vowel or a pause. Word-initial r is the trill [r] whatever precedes it: la rosa [la ˈrosa].

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 8 (spirantization), ch. 10 (nasal assimilation), ch. 9 (/s/ voicing); coda /s/ retention in Paraguay per Lipski 1994 (Paraguay chapter), educated norm.

## Glides and diphthongs

Within a word, an unstressed i (or y) or u next to another vowel is a glide. Write [j w] before the syllable nucleus and [i̯ u̯] after it. Rising diphthongs: bien [ˈbjen], ciudad [sjuˈðað], cuando [ˈkwando], Paraguay [paɾaˈɣwai̯]. Falling diphthongs: aire [ˈai̯ɾe], hay [ˈai̯], causa [ˈkau̯sa], muy [ˈmui̯], hoy [ˈoi̯]. A written accent on i or u marks hiatus, and both vowels are written as syllabic: día [ˈdia], país [paˈis], baúl [baˈul], río [ˈrio]. Spelling ü is [w]: pingüino [piŋˈɡwino]. Word-initial hie- and y before a vowel are the consonant [ʝ]: hielo [ˈʝelo], yo [ˈʝo]. Initial hue- is [w]: hueso [ˈweso]. The conjunction y is always [i]. Never make a glide across a word boundary: mi amigo [mi aˈmiɣo].

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 6 (glides and diphthongs: j w prenuclear, i̯ u̯ postnuclear) and ch. 7 (palatal obstruent).

## Other choices

### ll vs y (lleísmo)

Paraguay is described as a traditional area that keeps the distinction. Write ll as the lateral [ʎ] and y (consonantal) as [ʝ]: calle [ˈkaʎe], llamar [ʎaˈmaɾ], mayo [ˈmaʝo], yo [ˈʝo]. This departs from the note's 'some žeísmo from Argentine influence'. That is a recent urban innovation, so never write [ʒ] or [ʃ] for ll or y.

Source: Lipski 1994, Paraguay chapter (retention of /ʎ/, attributed to Guaraní contact); Fontanella de Weinberg 1992, on the Paraguayan/NE-Argentine zone; Penny 2002, on the history of yeísmo.

### seseo

Spelled c (before e and i), z and s are all [s]. Never write θ: cinco [ˈsiŋko], azul [aˈsul], cielo [ˈsjelo].

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 9; Fontanella de Weinberg 1992 (general American seseo).

### rhotics

Write the tap [ɾ] for single r between vowels, in clusters and in coda: pero [ˈpeɾo], tres [ˈtɾes], amor [aˈmoɾ]. Write the trill [r] for rr, for word-initial r and for r after n l s: perro [ˈpero], rosa [ˈrosa], honra [ˈonra], Israel [izraˈel]. The assibilated rr and tr reported for Paraguay are not written in this broad transcription.

Source: Hualde 2005, ch. 11; assibilation noted in Lipski 1994, Paraguay chapter (not transcribed).

### velar fricative and other consonant symbols

Write j, and g before e or i, as [x]: gente [ˈxente], ojo [ˈoxo]. A weaker [h] is reported for some speakers but is not written. Write ch as [tʃ], ñ as [ɲ], x between vowels as [ks] (taxi [ˈtaksi]), x in México as [x], and h as nothing.

Source: Hualde 2005, chs. 7–9.

### Guaraní substrate in Spanish words

Never write prenasalised stops, glottal stops or nasal vowels in Spanish words. Write [b d ɡ] or [β ð ɣ] by the spirantization rule, as in andar [anˈdaɾ] and Paraguay [paɾaˈɣwai̯]. This keeps to the note's 'contact speech' rather than the standard.

Source: Lipski 1994, Paraguay chapter (prenasalization confined to Guaraní-dominant speech); Hualde 2005, ch. 8.

### unadapted Guaraní words and names

Apply these only to words in Guaraní spelling. Write y as [ɨ], j as [dʒ], ch as [ʃ], and mb nd ng as [ᵐb ⁿd ᵑɡ]. Write vowels with a tilde as nasal [ã ẽ ĩ õ ũ ɨ̃]. Stress the last syllable unless an accent marks another: Ypacaraí [ɨpakaɾaˈi], mitã [miˈtã], mbeju [ᵐbeˈdʒu]. The puso (') is [ʔ]. The apostrophe splits the word, so write [ʔ] as the onset of the following word and put the stress mark on whichever piece holds the stressed syllable: ka'a gives [ka ˈʔa]. Hispanicised loans and names in Spanish spelling follow the Spanish rules: tereré [teɾeˈɾe], Asunción [asunˈsjon].

Source: Lipski 1994, Paraguay chapter (Guaraní loans in Paraguayan Spanish); the note's mention of glottal stop and nasal vowels in Guaraní loans.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
