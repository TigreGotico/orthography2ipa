# Transcription conventions: Kuwaiti Arabic (ar-KW)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Mark primary stress with ˈ before the stressed syllable on every content word of two or more syllables. Do not mark monosyllables or unstressed function words. Syllabify the word as it is pronounced, after dropping any case ending or tanwīn (see pausal_forms), counting the article and attached clitics as part of the word. Apply these steps in order. (1) A final superheavy syllable (CVːC, CVCC, CVːCC) takes stress: [kiˈtaːb], [maˈħall], [ilˈbeːt]. (2) Otherwise a heavy penult (CVː or CVC) takes stress: [maˈdiːna], [ˈmaktab], [muˈdarris]. (3) Otherwise stress the antepenult: [ˈmadrasa], [ˈmadrasat]. In a two-syllable word with a light penult, stress the penult: [ˈwalad]. Long vowels: a long vowel makes its syllable heavy. A final open CVː is not superheavy, so it does not attract stress by itself: [ˈʕaʃa]. Geminates: the first half closes the preceding syllable, which makes it heavy: [muˈdarris]. A final geminate counts as CC: [maˈħall]. Suffixes count, so stress shifts when a suffix is added: [ˈbeːtak] → [beːˈtakum]. Lengthened object -h counts as superheavy: [ʃaːˈfaːh]. Case endings never count, because they are not pronounced.

Source: Holes, C. (1990), Gulf Arabic (Routledge Descriptive Grammars), phonology chapter, section on word stress; Qafisheh, H. (1977), A Short Reference Grammar of Gulf Arabic, phonology/stress section. This is the Eastern Arabian quantity-sensitive pattern, applied to Kuwaiti as in Johnstone (1967), Eastern Arabian Dialect Studies, Kuwait chapter.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Leave these unstressed, with no ˈ. The definite article il-/l-, because it is part of its host word and never takes stress on its own vowel: [ilˈwalad]. Attached proclitics: w-/u- 'and', b-/bi- 'with/in; future', l-/li- 'to', f-/fi-. Free monosyllabic prepositions: [fi], [min], [ʕan], [ʕind] when used as a preposition. The disyllabic prepositions [ʕala] and [maʕa], and Kuwaiti [wijja]. Conjunctions: [ʔaw], [bas] 'but', [ʔinn], [laːkin], [ʔiða], [loː]. The relative [illi]. Negators: [maː], [laː], [muː]. Suffixed pronouns are not words of their own; they belong to their host and can attract its stress. Leave these stressed, with ˈ if they have two or more syllables. Independent pronouns: [ˈʔana], [ˈʔinta], [ˈhuwa], [ˈhum], with the ˈ written only on polysyllables. Demonstratives: [ˈhaːða], [ˈhaːði]. Interrogatives: [ˈʃinu], [ˈweːn], [ˈʃloːn]. Numerals. The auxiliary/copular verb kaːn and its forms, which take ordinary verb stress: [ˈkaːnat]. Treat a negated or question word as a content word when it stands alone as an answer.

Source: Holes (1990), Gulf Arabic, chapters on prepositions, conjunctions and pronouns (clitic status of article, w-, b-, l-); Qafisheh (1977) on proclitics and the relative illi.

## Pausal forms

There is no separate pausal rule. Kuwaiti has no case inflection, so every word takes its dialect form, which is the pause-like form, in every position: sentence-final, at a comma, and phrase-internally alike. Drop final short case vowels (-u, -a, -i) where the diacritised input writes them: kitaːbu → [kiˈtaːb]. Drop tanwīn -un/-in: walad-un → [ˈwalad]. The exception is lexicalised adverbial -an, which is kept in every position: [ˈʃukran], [ˈʔabadan], [taɡˈriːban]. Write tāʾ marbūṭa as [a]: [ˈmadrasa]. In construct, or before a pronoun suffix, write it as [at]/[it]: [ˈmadrasat ilˈwalad], [madraˈsatha]. This departs from fully diacritised MSA-style input, whose case vowels are not part of the lect.

Source: Holes (1990), Gulf Arabic, morphology chapter (absence of case, feminine -a / construct -at); Johnstone (1967), Eastern Arabian Dialect Studies, Kuwait chapter.

## Connected speech

Write each word in its own place. Within a word, write these processes. (a) Sun-letter assimilation of the article: [iʃˈʃams], [itˈtiːn], [inˈnaːs]. (b) Velar affrication before an adjacent /i, iː/: [tʃibiːr] 'big' (written [tʃiˈbiːr]), [dʒidiːm]-type forms, and [ˈtʃalbitʃ]-type forms from the 2fs suffix (see other). Across a word boundary, write only one process: the elision of the initial vowel of the article and of other hamzat-waṣl words after a vowel-final word. The article is [il] after a consonant-final word or at the start of an utterance, and [l] after a vowel-final word: [ˈʃift ilˈwalad], but [fi lˈbeːt], [ʕala lˈmeːz]. The conjunction w- is [w] before a vowel, as in [wilˈwalad], and [u] before a consonant, as in [uˈbeːt]. Do not write nasal place assimilation, voicing assimilation, linking, resyllabification or epenthetic vowels across word boundaries: [min ˈbeːt], not [mim ˈbeːt]. Do not insert or delete vowels beyond what the tashkeel gives. A sukūn means no vowel, and the input vowels are written as given.

Source: Holes (1990), Gulf Arabic, phonology chapter (article allomorphy, assimilation of l-, elision of initial i- after vowels); Qafisheh (1977), phonology section on the article.

## Glides and diphthongs

Write consonantal glides as [j] and [w] in onsets, codas and geminates: [jaˈʔkil], [ʔajˈjaːm], [ˈwalad], [ʔawˈwal]. Never use i̯ or u̯. Write the old diphthongs /aj, aw/ in native words monophthongised, as [eː] and [oː]: [beːt] 'house', [joːm] 'day', [loːn] 'colour'. Keep [aj] and [aw] only before a vowel, where the glide is an onset ([ˈʔajaː]-type), or when doubled as a geminate. Kuwaiti ج is written [dʒ], not the traditional [j] (see other).

Source: Johnstone (1967), Eastern Arabian Dialect Studies, Kuwait chapter (monophthongisation of *ay, *aw); Holes (1990), Gulf Arabic, phonology chapter on vowels and diphthongs.

## Other choices

### Reflex of qāf (ق)

Write [ɡ] for ق in all native and everyday vocabulary: [ɡaːl], [ɡaˈriːb] (written [dʒiˈriːb] only by the adjacency rule if an /i/ follows the ɡ directly), [ɡahwa] (written [ˈɡahwa]). Write [q] only in religious or learned terms with MSA phonology: [qurˈʔaːn]. Do not write the [ɣ] reflex reported for some Kuwaiti speech (ɣirš).

Source: Johnstone (1967), Kuwait chapter; Holes (1990); Al-Balushi (2016), p.89, for the [ɣ] variant, which is not adopted.

### Velar affrication domain

Affricate /k/ → [tʃ] and /ɡ/ → [dʒ] only when /i/ or /iː/ is directly adjacent (before it, or after it in the same syllable rhyme): [tʃiˈbiːr], [ðˤiːdʒ] 'narrow'. Do not affricate across an intervening /r, l/, and do not affricate in other emphatic contexts. This follows the specification. Matar's (1969) wider, non-adjacent trigger is noted but not applied. Lexicalised Kuwaiti items such as [tʃaːn] 'was' are not written unless the input spells them with چ.

Source: Matar (1969), as reported in Alshammari (2026), p.1335; Johnstone (1967) on lexicalisation, as reported in Alshammari (2026), p.1335. The narrower rule follows the specification.

### 2nd person feminine singular suffix (kashkasha)

Where the diacritised input writes the 2fs object/possessive suffix -كِ, write the standard Kuwaiti [-itʃ]: [kiˈtaːbitʃ], [ʃaːˈfitʃ]. The specification does not model this as a morphological process, but it is the standard Kuwaiti pronunciation.

Source: Johnstone (1967), Eastern Arabian Dialect Studies, Kuwait chapter; Holes (1990), pronoun suffix tables.

### ض and ظ

Write both ض and ظ as [ðˤ]: [ðˤaˈrab], [ðˤuhur]. Keep [θ] and [ð] for ث and ذ. Write the emphatics as [sˤ], [tˤ] and [ðˤ].

Source: Holes (1990), Gulf Arabic, phonology chapter (merger of *ḍ and *ẓ as [ðˤ], retention of interdentals).

### Reflex of jīm (ج)

Write ج as [dʒ] in all words: [dʒaːr], [ˈridʒdʒaːl]. Do not write the traditional Kuwaiti [j] variant, which is old and receding.

Source: Al-Qenaie, S. (2011), Kuwaiti Arabic: A Socio-phonological Perspective (PhD, Durham), on the (dʒ) variable; Taqi (2010), PhD, Newcastle; Johnstone (1967), for the traditional [j] reflex.

### Loan consonants

Write /p/, /v/ and /ŋ/ where the input spells them (پ, ڤ, or a nasal before a velar in loans): [ˈpaːs], [ˈviːza], [ˈbaŋk].

Source: Holes (1990), on English loan phonology in Gulf Arabic.

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
