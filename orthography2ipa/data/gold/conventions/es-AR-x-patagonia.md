# Transcription conventions: Patagonian Spanish (es-AR-x-patagonia)

Every pass of `scripts/correct_silver_ipa.py` reads this sheet. Correct it before the run. A change to the text changes the cache key, so the rows of the lect run again.

## Stress

Primary stress is lexical. Write ˈ before the onset of the stressed syllable in every stressed word, monosyllables included. Syllabify by maximal onset, so the mark goes before the whole onset: padre [ˈpadɾe], otro [ˈotɾo], bueno [ˈbweno], tierra [ˈtjera], sol [ˈsol]. Place stress by the written accent where there is one: está [esˈta], árbol [ˈaɾbol], teléfono [teˈlefono]. Otherwise stress the penult in words ending in a vowel, -n or -s, and the final syllable in words ending in any other consonant: casa [ˈkasa], hablan [ˈablan], ciudad [sjuˈdad], comer [koˈmeɾ]. Rioplatense voseo forms keep their final stress: tenés [teˈnes], sos [ˈsos], vení [beˈni]. An enclitic written solid with its verb adds no stress and does not move it: decime [deˈsime], dámelo [ˈdamelo]. Adverbs in -mente take a single mark on -men-: rápidamente [rapidaˈmente]. There is no secondary stress. Long vowels, geminates and case endings do not exist in this lect, so they need no rule. Unstressed function words take no mark (see function_words).

Source: Real Academia Española & ASALE (2010), Ortografía de la lengua española, ch. II (accentuation rules); Hualde, J.I. (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, CUP, chapter on stress; Quilis, A. (1993), Tratado de fonología y fonética españolas, Gredos, chapter on the accent. Voseo stress follows Fontanella de Weinberg, M.B. (1987), El español bonaerense, and Lipski (1994), chapter on Argentina.

## Stressed and unstressed words

Unstressed words take no ˈ and keep full vowels, because Spanish has no vowel reduction. They are: the definite articles el, la, lo, los, las; prenominal possessives mi, tu, su, mis, tus, sus, nuestro; and the object clitics me, te, se, lo, la, le, nos, los, las, les. Also unstressed are all simple prepositions except según: a, de, en, con, por, para, sin, sobre, hasta, desde. The same holds for the conjunctions y, e, o, u, ni, que, pero, si, aunque, porque, como, and for the relatives que, quien, donde, cuando, como when they are not interrogative or exclamative. Examples: la casa [la ˈkasa], de mi padre [de mi ˈpadɾe], te lo dije [te lo ˈdixe], con vos [kon ˈbos], que viene [ke ˈbjene]. Stressed words take ˈ. They are: the indefinite articles un, una, unos, unas; subject and prepositional pronouns yo, vos, él, ella, usted, nosotros, ustedes, mí, ti; demonstratives; numerals; según; interrogatives and exclamatives qué, quién, cómo, dónde, cuándo, cuánto; and all verb forms, auxiliaries included (he, ha, es, está). Examples: un libro [ˈun ˈlibɾo], yo sé [ˈʃo ˈse], ¿qué hacés? [ˈke aˈses], ha venido [ˈa beˈnido].

Source: Navarro Tomás, T. (1918/1977), Manual de pronunciación española, the section on stressed and unstressed words (palabras acentuadas e inacentuadas); Quilis (1993), Tratado de fonología y fonética españolas; Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chapter on stress (unstressed function words). The treatment of un/una as stressed follows Hualde and Quilis.

## Pausal forms

This topic does not apply: Spanish has no pausal forms, case endings or nunation. A word is written the same at the end of a sentence, at a comma and inside a phrase: final vowels, -s, -n and -r are unchanged (casas [ˈkasas], comer [koˈmeɾ]).

Source: Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish; Quilis (1993). Neither describes pause-specific word forms for Spanish.

## Connected speech

Write each word in its own lexical broad form, and write no cross-word process. Resyllabification is not shown: los otros [los ˈotɾos], not [lo ˈsotɾos]. Synalepha or vowel merger is not shown: la otra [la ˈotɾa], de esto [de ˈesto]. Nasal place assimilation is not shown: un poco [ˈun ˈpoko], en casa [en ˈkasa]. Voicing of /s/ before a voiced consonant is not shown: los dos [los ˈdos]. Coda /s/ is always written [s], in every position and across boundaries: es tarde [ˈes ˈtaɾde], mismo [ˈmismo]. Preconsonantal aspiration to [h] is common in Argentine speech, including Patagonia, but it varies with style and region, so it is not written. The same no-allophone rule holds inside words: write b d g, never β ð ɣ (lado [ˈlado], abogado [aboˈgado]), and write n before labials and velars (enviar [enbiˈaɾ], tengo [ˈtengo]). The only boundary-sensitive fact written is a property of the word itself: word-initial r is always the trill [r], even after a vowel-final word (la rosa [la ˈrosa]).

Source: Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chapters on resyllabification, nasal assimilation and spirantization, which describe these processes as allophonic. The choice not to write them follows the specification's broad-transcription scope. Aspiration of coda /s/ in Argentine speech: Fontanella de Weinberg (1987), El español bonaerense; Lipski (1994), chapter on Argentina.

## Glides and diphthongs

Follow Hualde's split notation. A prenuclear glide in a rising diphthong is written j or w: bien [ˈbjen], tierra [ˈtjera], ciudad [sjuˈdad], bueno [ˈbweno], cuatro [ˈkwatɾo], hueso [ˈweso], pingüino [pinˈgwino]. A postnuclear glide in a falling diphthong is written i̯ or u̯: aire [ˈai̯ɾe], hay [ˈai̯], muy [ˈmui̯], rey [ˈrei̯], auto [ˈau̯to], Neuquén [neu̯ˈken]. Word-final orthographic y after a vowel is the glide i̯. Hiatus with a stressed high vowel is two syllables with full vowels: día [ˈdia], país [paˈis], río [ˈrio]. Unaccented i/u next to a vowel in Spanish spelling counts as a glide (piano [ˈpjano]). The conjunction y is always [i]. Orthographic y or hi before a vowel at the start of a syllable is not a glide; it is the consonant [ʃ] (see other): ya [ˈʃa], hielo [ˈʃelo].

Source: Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chapter on glides and diphthongs (j w versus i̯ u̯ notation); Quilis (1993), on diphthongs and hiatus; RAE & ASALE (2010), Ortografía, on hiatus and diphthong spelling.

## Other choices

### ll / y (sheísmo)

Write orthographic ll, y and hi before a vowel at the start of a syllable as [ʃ]: calle [ˈkaʃe], yo [ˈʃo], ella [ˈeʃa], ayer [aˈʃeɾ], hielo [ˈʃelo]. This departs from the specification note, which models pan-Latin-American yeísmo as /ʝ/. Argentine and Uruguayan Spanish, including the Patagonian variety settled largely from Buenos Aires, has the prepalatal fricative instead (rehilamiento). Today it is mostly voiceless [ʃ] among younger speakers. The older voiced [ʒ] is a conservative variant and is not written.

Source: Fontanella de Weinberg (1987), El español bonaerense; Vidal de Battini, B.E. (1964), El español de la Argentina; Rohena-Madrazo, M. (2015), on the devoicing of the Buenos Aires prepalatal fricative (Studies in Hispanic and Lusophone Linguistics); Lipski (1994), chapter on Argentina.

### seseo and the velar fricative

Write c before e/i, z and s as [s]: cielo [ˈsjelo], zapato [saˈpato], acción [akˈsjon]. Write j, and g before e/i, as velar [x], not [h]: gente [ˈxente], México [ˈmexiko]. Write ch as [tʃ]: chico [ˈtʃiko]. Write x elsewhere as [ks]: taxi [ˈtaksi]. Write b and v alike as [b]: vaca [ˈbaka].

Source: Lipski (1994), chapter on Argentina; Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chapter on consonants; the specification note (seseo).

### rhotics

Write the trill [r] for rr, for word-initial r, and for r after n, l, s: perro [ˈpero], rosa [ˈrosa], honra [ˈonra], Israel [israˈel]. Write the tap [ɾ] everywhere else, including the coda: caro [ˈkaɾo], mar [ˈmaɾ], parte [ˈpaɾte], decirle [deˈsiɾle]. Assibilated rr, reported for parts of the Andean and northwestern regions, is not written.

Source: Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chapter on rhotics; Vidal de Battini (1964), for the regional assibilation that is excluded.

### final and intervocalic /d/

Write orthographic d as [d] in every position, with no elision: verdad [beɾˈdad], usted [usˈted], cansado [kanˈsado]. The common weakening or loss in these positions in Argentine speech varies with style and is not written.

Source: Hualde (2005), The Sounds of Spanish, chapter on /d/ weakening; Fontanella de Weinberg (1987).

### place names and loanwords from Mapudungun and Welsh

Transcribe them as they are pronounced in local Spanish, using the conventions above, not the source language: Neuquén [neu̯ˈken], Esquel [esˈkel], Trelew [tɾeˈleu̯], Chubut [tʃuˈbut].

Source: Lipski (1994), chapter on Argentina (Mapudungun adstrate in the south); general Spanish adaptation patterns as described in Hualde (2005).

## Word segmentation

The engine splits a text into words at spaces, at hyphens and at punctuation. An apostrophe splits words, as punctuation does. A clitic joined to its host by a hyphen is a word of its own. Each pass gets the words of its text in this split, and writes one IPA word for each word, in the same order, separated by single spaces. A process across a word boundary changes the words on each side of the boundary; it does not join them.
